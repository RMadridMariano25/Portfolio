This study explores the perceptions of teachers and students regarding the integration of generative AI tools in
teaching and learning. The research focuses on understanding how teachers view the adoption of AI technologies
in lesson planning and their effects on students’ learning behavior and academic performance. Additionally, the
study investigates students’  experiences with AI, examining its impact on learning outcomes, preferences, and
the  challenges  they  encounter.  Data  were  collected  from  244  students  and 29  teachers  at  a public  university
through  surveys  and  semi-structured  interviews.  The  results  reveal  varying  perspectives:  while  students  and
teachers  acknowledged the  potential benefits  of  generative AI, including personalized  learning  and improved
access to academic resources, concerns were raised about data privacy, the accuracy of AI-generated content, and
its limitations in promoting independent and critical thinking. The findings highlight the need for a strategic,
inclusive approach to incorporating AI in education, emphasizing the importance of social influence, facilitating
conditions, and pedagogical integration as outlined in the UTAUT and TPACK frameworks.

1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence  (AI)  is driving  a  transformative shift in  edu-
cation, offering unprecedented opportunities to enhance both teaching
and  learning.  This  transformation  goes  beyond  a  mere  technological
advancement, reshaping how education is delivered and experienced.
AI’s potential to offer personalized learning experiences tailored to in-
dividual preferences, learning styles, and speeds is now more feasible
than  ever.  These  AI-powered  systems  not  only  facilitate  personalized
learning but also provide immediate feedback, analyze large datasets,
and  adapt  to  students’  needs,  leading  to  more  effective  and  efficient
learning processes (Baker &  Hawn, 2021; Maghsudi et al., 2021). The
integration  of  AI  extends  beyond  traditional  educational  practices,
enabling the use of intelligent tutoring systems and adaptive learning
platforms that cater to the diverse needs of students, promoting inclu-
sivity and equity in education.

In this study, we focus on the firsthand experiences and perceptions
of teachers and students regarding the adoption of generative AI tools in
educational settings. The research aims to explore how AI tools influence
lesson planning, learning behaviors, academic performance, and student
engagement. By examining the effects of AI, the study seeks to provide

insights  into  its  practical  application,  emphasizing  both  the  opportu-
nities and challenges it presents in education.

The theoretical foundation of this study is grounded in the Unified
Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) and the Tech-
nological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework. UTAUT
helps  understand  how  external  factors,  such  as  social  influence  and
facilitating  conditions,  affect  the  acceptance  and  usage  of  AI  tools  in
educational contexts (Venkatesh et al., 2003). The TPACK framework,
on the other hand, focuses on the integration of technology with peda-
gogy and content knowledge, offering a comprehensive perspective on
how educators can effectively incorporate AI into their teaching prac-
tices  (Mishra  &  Koehler,  2006).  These  frameworks,  when  combined,
provide  a  comprehensive  approach  to  understanding  the  factors  that
influence  the  adoption  of  AI  and  its  integration  into  educational
frameworks.

Given that AI technologies have the potential to drastically reshape
educational practices, it is critical to examine the broader implications
of their adoption. This study will explore how AI can be integrated into
curricula,  how  educators  can  adapt  to  its  use,  and  the  necessary  re-
sources  and  guidelines  required  for  effective  implementation.  By  un-
derstanding  teachers’  and  students’  perceptions,  the  study  aims  to

2. Literature review

2.1. The role of generative AI in education

Generative AI technologies, such as ChatGPT, are increasingly being
integrated into educational settings, offering personalized learning ex-
periences and administrative efficiencies. These tools can analyze indi-
vidual learning patterns, adapt content to meet diverse student needs,
and provide immediate feedback, leading to more effective and efficient
learning  processes  (Baker  &  Hawn,  2021;  Maghsudi  et  al.,  2021).
However, the integration of AI into education raises critical concerns,
particularly regarding data privacy, algorithmic bias, and equitable ac-
cess  to  technology  (Wargo  &  Anderson,  2024).  These  challenges
necessitate a critical examination of AI’s role in education and its im-
plications for teaching and learning.

2.2. Teachers’ perceptions and AI competence

Teachers’  perceptions  of  AI  in  education  are  influenced  by  their
technological,  pedagogical,  and  content  knowledge.  The  AI-TPACK
framework  emphasizes  the  interplay  between  these  knowledge  do-
mains in effectively integrating AI into teaching practices (Ning et al.,
2024).  Studies have  shown that  teachers’  AI competence  self-efficacy
and  their  TPACK  are  positively  correlated,  indicating  that  higher
levels  of  technological  knowledge  enhance  their  confidence  and  will-
ingness  to  adopt  AI  tools  in  their  teaching  (Ibragimov  et  al.,  2025).
Furthermore, teachers’ willingness to integrate AI is influenced by their
emotional responses and needs satisfaction, highlighting the importance
of  addressing  affective  factors  in  professional  development  programs
(Tan et al., 2024).

2.3. Students’ behavioral intentions and UTAUT

Students’ intentions to use AI tools for learning are shaped by factors

such as performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence,
as outlined in the UTAUT framework (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Recent
studies  have  applied  UTAUT  to  understand  students’  behavioral  in-
tentions  to  use  content  generative  AI  tools  for  learning  and  research.
Findings indicate that performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and
social  influence  significantly  influence  students’  intentions  to  use  AI
tools, while facilitating conditions and perceived risks have less impact
(Yakubu et al., 2025). These insights are crucial for developing strate-
gies to encourage AI adoption among students.

2.4. Integrating UTAUT and TPACK in AI adoption research

Integrating  UTAUT  and  TPACK  provides  a  comprehensive  frame-
work for understanding the factors influencing AI adoption in education.
While UTAUT focuses on the acceptance and use of technology from the
user’s perspective, TPACK addresses the integration of technology into
pedagogy and content delivery. Combining these frameworks allows for
a holistic examination of both teachers’ and students’ perceptions and
behaviors regarding AI tools, facilitating a deeper understanding of the
dynamics at play in educational settings (Ning et al., 2024; Yakubu et al.,
2025).

While existing literature has explored various aspects of AI adoption
in  education,  there  is  a  lack  of  comprehensive  studies  that  simulta-
neously apply the UTAUT and TPACK frameworks to investigate both
teachers’ and students’ perceptions and behaviors regarding generative
AI tools. Additionally, much of the current research focuses on devel-
oped countries, with limited attention given to contexts in developing
nations.  This  study  aims  to  fill  these  gaps  by  providing  a  dual-
perspective  analysis  of  AI  adoption  in  education,  considering  both
technological acceptance and pedagogical integration, within a devel-
oping country context.

This study aims to explore the perceptions of teachers and students
regarding the integration of generative AI tools in education, guided by
the UTAUT and TPACK frameworks. This study does not claim to un-
cover entirely new challenges in AI adoption, as issues such as limited
training,  insufficient  institutional  support,  and  privacy  concerns  have
been  widely  reported  in  both  developed  and  developing  contexts.
Instead, its contribution lies in offering a context-specific and theory-
informed  exploration  of  generative  AI  adoption  within  Pakistani
higher education. By integrating the Unified Theory of Acceptance and
Use of Technology (UTAUT) with the Technological Pedagogical Con-
tent  Knowledge  (TPACK)  framework,  the  study  moves  beyond  doc-
umenting surface-level barriers and examines how user perceptions of
performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence inter-
sect  with  pedagogical  and  content  considerations.  This  dual-lens
approach provides a more nuanced understanding of AI adoption in a
developing  country  setting,  generating  baseline  evidence  that  can
inform local practice while serving as a comparative case study for re-
searchers examining similar contexts internationally. The findings will
contribute to the development of strategies for effectively integrating AI
into  educational  practices,  addressing  both  technological  and  peda-
gogical considerations.

3. Research design

The study adopts a mixed-methods approach, utilizing both quanti-
tative  and  qualitative  data  collection  techniques.  Two  primary  tools
were used: a survey and interviews. The quantitative data were collected
through two sets of structured questionnaires: one aimed at students and
the other at teachers. The survey data were analyzed using SPSS version
26. The study employed modified survey instruments adapted from prior
studies  (Gr´ajeda  et  al.,  2024;  Kaplan-Rakowski  et  al.,  2023),  which
included  items  corresponding  to  performance  expectancy,  effort  ex-
pectancy, and AI competence. However, not all UTAUT constructs (e.g.,
moderating  effects  of  demographics)  and  not  all  TPACK  components

3.1. Consent and ethical considerations

Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Informed consent
was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in both the
survey  and  interview  components.  Consent  was  recorded  either  elec-
tronically  (for  online  responses)  or  paper-based  (for  in-person  in-
terviews), depending on the mode of participation. All participants were
assured of confidentiality and their right to withdraw from the study at
any time without penalty. No direct benefits were offered for partici-
pation,  but  students  were  informed  that  their  involvement  would
contribute to the academic understanding of AI tools in education.

university in Pakistan, admitted in their first semester of undergraduate

3.4. Data analysis

The data collected in this study were analyzed using both quantita-
tive and qualitative methods, providing a comprehensive understanding
of teachers’ and students’ perceptions of generative AI tools in educa-
tion, as well as their experiences with the challenges, limitations, and
future expectations of these tools. The analysis process was divided into
two main components: quantitative and qualitative data analysis.

For the quantitative data, Likert-scale questionnaires were used to
gather numerical information on teachers’ and students’ perceptions of
AI tools. Descriptive statistics were initially applied to summarize the
responses, with the mean scores and standard deviations providing an
overview  of  participants’  agreement  or  disagreement  with  various
statements  about  AI’s  impact  on  lesson  planning,  learning  behavior,
academic performance, and future expectations. This approach helped
to identify trends and patterns in the data, such as the extent to which AI
tools  were perceived  as  beneficial by  teachers  and students. The  reli-
ability  of  the  data  was  assessed  using  Cronbach’s  alpha  coefficient,
which revealed high internal consistency. The teachers’  questionnaire
had a reliability coefficient of 0.89, while the students’  questionnaire
had a coefficient of 0.87, indicating that the questionnaires were reliable
measures of participants’ perceptions.

In addition to descriptive statistics, comparative analysis was con-
ducted to examine potential differences in perceptions based on specific
variables  such  as  gender,  prior  experience  with  AI,  and  academic
background. Independent t-tests or ANOVA were used, depending on the
nature of the data, to assess whether there were significant differences
between groups. For example, this analysis revealed whether teachers
and students with varying levels of exposure to AI tools viewed their
impact differently, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the
findings.

The  qualitative  data  were  collected  through  semi-structured  in-
terviews, which were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic
analysis.  Thematic  analysis  followed  the  approach  outlined  by  Braun
and Clarke (2006), which included several stages: familiarization with
the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing the
themes, defining and naming the themes, and writing up the findings.
This inductive approach allowed the themes to emerge directly from the
data, rather than being pre-determined. Thematic analysis identified key
themes such as familiarity with AI tools, challenges and limitations, the
impact of AI on students’ learning behavior, teachers’ views on lesson
planning and student academic performance, and students’  future ex-
pectations and preferences regarding AI.

NVivo 12 software was utilized to assist in organizing the qualitative

data. The software allowed for the efficient coding, categorization, and
organization of themes and sub-themes, providing a systematic way to
explore  and  analyze  the  data.  The  coding  process  helped  identify
recurring patterns in the interviews, offering deeper insights into how
both teachers and students view the use of AI tools in education.

To ensure the robustness and validity of the findings, triangulation
was  employed  by  combining  the  results  from  both  quantitative  and
qualitative  analyses.  This  process  involved  cross-referencing  the  find-
ings  to  identify  consistent  patterns  and  complementary  insights.  For
instance, if a significant portion of students reported that AI tools were
beneficial for academic performance in the quantitative data, this was
supported by qualitative responses where students elaborated on how AI
tools  helped  them  better  understand  course  content  or  complete  as-
signments. The qualitative data also provided context for the quantita-
tive  findings,  offering  deeper  insights  into  the  reasons  behind
participants’ responses. For example, while the quantitative data indi-
cated  a  positive  impact  on  academic  performance,  the  interviews
revealed concerns about over-reliance on AI and its potential to reduce
independent learning. By combining these findings, the study was able
to provide a more comprehensive and well-rounded understanding of
the  effectiveness,  challenges,  and  future  potential  of  AI  tools  in
education.

Through  this  mixed-methods  approach,  the  study  successfully
triangulated the quantitative and qualitative findings to strengthen the
conclusions, offering a nuanced and detailed perspective on the role of
generative AI tools in enhancing teaching and learning experiences.

4. Findings

The current research adopted a mixed-methods approach, combining
both qualitative and quantitative data to provide a comprehensive and
generalized understanding of the impact of generative AI tools in edu-
cation. This approach allowed for a detailed exploration of participants’
perspectives  through  both  closed-ended  Likert-scale  statements  and
open-ended interview questions. The study was guided by the integra-
tion  of  the  UTAUT  and  TPACK  models,  which  provided  a  robust
framework  to  analyze  the  adoption  and  integration  of  AI  tools  in
educational  contexts.  The  constructs  from  the  UTAUT  model  Perfor-
mance Expectancy, Effort Expectancy, Social Influence, and Facilitating
Conditions  were  particularly  useful  in  examining  the  behavioral  in-
tentions of both teachers and students to use generative AI, while the
TPACK  model  helped  explore  how  technological,  pedagogical,  and
content knowledge influenced the integration of AI tools into teaching
practices.

For the first research question, which examined the perceptions of
teachers and students on the impact of generative AI tools in education,
the study focused on four key areas: lesson planning, students’ learning
behavior,  academic  performance,  and  the  challenges  and  limitations
associated  with  AI tool  usage.  Quantitative  data  were collected  using
closed-ended questionnaires, with mean scores and standard deviations
calculated to quantify the relationship between the key variables. These
findings  are  presented  in  Tables  3  to  9.  Additionally,  qualitative  in-
terviews were conducted with both teachers and students to gain deeper
insights into how AI tools affect teaching and learning experiences. The
qualitative data served to complement and contextualize the quantita-
tive  results,  providing  richer,  more  nuanced  perspectives  on  the
observed correlations between AI use and educational outcomes.

To  address  the  second  research  question,  which  delved  into  the
challenges and limitations faced by students when using generative AI
tools,  in-depth  interviews  with  students  were  conducted.  These  in-
terviews  offered  valuable  perspectives  on  the  barriers  to  effective  AI
integration,  such  as  concerns  about  privacy,  the  reliability  of  AI-
generated  content,  and  the  impact  on  independent  learning.  By
combining both qualitative and quantitative data, this methodological
approach allowed for a multifaceted understanding of the opportunities,

5. Interview data findings

The  interview  responses  from  both  teachers  and  students  were
analyzed  using  NVivo  12  software  to  identify  and  organize  recurring
patterns  and  themes  in  the  data.  Thematic  analysis  was  employed,
allowing  the  researcher  to  explore  the  various  dimensions  of  partici-
pants’  experiences  and  perceptions  related  to  generative  AI  tools  in
education. During the coding process, the interviews were subjected to
open  coding,  where  key  words  and  phrases  were  identified  and  cate-
gorized based on the central ideas expressed by the participants. From
this process, several core themes emerged, reflecting the diverse views
and experiences related to the use of AI tools in educational contexts.

The analysis revealed key codes and themes across both teacher and
student groups, including: familiarity with AI tools, students’ changing
learning  behaviors,  challenges  and  limitations,  lesson  planning,  and
learners’ academic performance. Teachers’ responses revealed a blend
of enthusiasm and apprehension, with a strong focus on how AI tools can
enhance instructional practices, but also concerns regarding their inte-
gration into existing curricula. Students were more focused on the im-
mediate  benefits  of  AI  in  offering  personalized  feedback  and
independent  learning,  while  also  expressing  concerns  about  over-
reliance on technology and the potential impact on mental health and
information overload.

5.2. Students’ learning behavior

During an interview question regarding students’ learning behavior
in  education,  educators  observed  a  substantial  transition  towards
inquiry-based and autonomous learning. The students were observed to
be  more  likely  to  independently  search  out  information  and  are
leveraging digital resources to supplement their understanding. Never-
theless, educators also noted that technology could sometimes result in
superficial  learning  and  distractions  if  it  is  not  managed  carefully,
despite the fact that it facilitates engagement.
Students Respondent 4 acknowledged,
“I  encountered  difficulties  with  self-discipline  and  time  manage-
ment, underscoring the necessity of balanced guidance from educators
to  effectively  implement  these  new  learning  behaviors,  despite  these
advantages.”

Students  acknowledged  that  their  learning  behavior  has  changed
because of the availability of a variety of learning aids and platforms.
They expressed their gratitude for the ability to learn at their own tempo
and reported feeling more empowered to determine their own learning
paths.

When  asked  about  educational  challenges,  educators  frequently
identified resource limitations, including inadequate access to current
technology  and  inadequate  instruction  for  utilizing  new  educational
tools. They also emphasized the challenges of accommodating a variety
of  learning  styles  in  a  single  classroom  and  the  heightened  strain  of
standardized  testing,  which  frequently  stifles  critical  thinking  and
creativity).

Teacher Respondent 5 said,
“There’s a risk of bias in the generated content, as AI models may
reflect biases present in the training data. This limits my exposure to
diverse perspectives and viewpoints. Moreover, technical issues such as
software glitches or internet connectivity problems can also disrupt my

In the meantime, students identified obstacles such as the strain to
perform well academically and the overwhelming volume of informa-
tion. They expressed apprehension regarding the mental health conse-
quences of this burden, which included anxiety and stress. Furthermore,
a few students expressed their frustration with the challenge of locating
dependable and pertinent resources in the plethora of available infor-
mation, emphasizing the significance of instructors’ assistance in over-
coming these educational obstacles.

Student respondent 6 highlighted,
“I face several challenges and limitations when I use generative AI

5.4. Lesson planning

One  of  the  important  aspects  of  teaching  process  is  lesson  plan,
which is center of every successful classroom-teaching context. Teacher
participants shared some valuable insight about impact of AI in lesson
planning.

Teacher respondent 3 stated,
“The impact of using AI tools on lesson planning can result in such a
way that I, as a teacher, can personalize how students will learn things
by analyzing individual students’ performance. Every student learns at a
different pace; some learn faster, while some take time to learn some-
thing; I can use AI to plan my teaching resources and enhance them in
such a way that every student can catch the lesson easily.”

5.5. Learners’ academic performance

Teachers  frequently  emphasized  the  advantageous  effects  of

performance of their students.

“I observed that students who actively participate in the use of these
resources tend to demonstrate an increase in their comprehension and
retention of the material.”

On the other hand, students asserted that the level of support and
feedback  they  receive  from  both  instructors  and  educational  tools
significantly impacts their academic performance. They recognized that
interactive and adaptive learning platforms facilitate their comprehen-
sion of  complex concepts  and their  ability to prepare  for assessments
more effectively. However, students also noted the pressure to achieve
high  grades,  which  can  sometimes  come  at  the  expense  of  profound
learning  and  comprehension.  This  underscores  the  necessity  of  a
balanced approach that prioritizes both performance and well-being.

6. Discussion

The integration of generative AI tools into educational settings has
proven to be a transformative force, enhancing the educational experi-
ence for both teachers and students. This study explored the perceptions
of  teachers  and  students  regarding  the  use  of  generative  AI  tools  in
lesson  planning,  learning  behavior,  academic  performance,  and  their
challenges and limitations. The findings corroborate existing research
while offering new insights into the effectiveness and limitations of these
technologies in educational contexts.

In line with the findings of Yakubu et al. (2025), the study demon-
strates  that  performance  expectancy  and  effort  expectancy,  key  con-
structs of the UTAUT framework, significantly influence students’ and
teachers’ acceptance and usage of AI tools. However, it also aligns with
Kanont et al. (2024), who observed a paradox where perceived useful-
ness decreased as AI tools became more intuitive. Students in this study,
particularly those with higher digital literacy, reported a decrease in the
perceived benefit of AI tools over time, as they became more personal-
ized. This challenge reflects the increasingly high expectations of digital-
native students who may perceive AI tools as insufficient in addressing
their  nuanced  educational  needs,  echoing  the  concerns  raised  by  Ma
et al. (2024) about the role of AI in reducing learning autonomy.

Teachers in this study recognized the potential of generative AI tools
to enhance lesson planning and improve student engagement, aligning
with findings from Ghimire and Edwards (2024). AI tools were seen as
essential  for  adapting  lesson  plans  to  individual  student  needs  and
improving  classroom  management.  However,  similar  to  the  insights
from Bygstad et al. (2022), teachers also identified a significant gap in
professional development and training, which hindered their ability to
fully integrate AI into their instructional practices. This challenge is not
unique  to  this  study,  as  Kaplan-Rakowski  et  al.  (2023) noted  that
teachers’ willingness to adopt AI is contingent upon adequate training
and institutional support.

Furthermore, Baker and Hawn (2021) emphasized that AI-generated
content is often plagued by issues of bias and inaccuracy, which were
also observed in this study. Students expressed concerns over the reli-
ability  of  AI-generated  responses,  which  aligns  with  the  findings  of
Gonz´alez-Calatayud et al. (2021). These concerns underline the impor-
tance of using AI tools as supplementary resources rather than as sole
providers of information, particularly in the context of fostering inde-
pendent  critical  thinking  skills.  The  issue  of  data  privacy,  which
emerged as a significant concern among students, resonates with Akgun
and Greenhow (2022), who argue that privacy risks associated with AI
tools must be carefully managed. The study participants highlighted a
need for stronger data protection policies to mitigate these concerns; a
sentiment echoed in the work of Yu (2020) on the algorithmic divide and
the challenges of ensuring equity in access to AI technologies.

While  AI  tools  have  demonstrated  the  potential  to  revolutionize
education, this study identifies several limitations. First, teachers indi-
cated that AI tools are not yet capable of fostering student autonomy and
critical  thinking  at  the  desired  level.  As  observed  by  Zheng  and  Tse
(2023), the over-reliance on AI tools could lead to a reduction in stu-
dents’ problem-solving abilities, as AI often provides ready-made solu-
tions rather than promoting independent cognitive effort. This concern
was also reflected in the students’  responses, with many expressing a
need for balance between AI support and human interaction to prevent
over-dependence.

Second,  both students  and  teachers faced  significant  challenges in
integrating AI tools into their daily academic routines. This is consistent
with the findings of Holmes et al. (2021), who highlighted that while AI
can  improve  educational  outcomes,  its  integration  into  traditional
pedagogies  remains  challenging,  particularly  in  resource-limited  set-
tings. In this study, students faced difficulties related to the accessibility
of AI tools, with many reporting that they lacked sufficient guidance or
training to use these tools effectively, a gap also noted by Fitria (2023).

6.1. Implications for AI Integration in Education

The findings of this study provide critical insights for both policy and
practice in the adoption of generative AI tools in education. Given the
significant  role  that  facilitating  conditions  (as  per  UTAUT)  play  in
fostering  AI  adoption,  it  is  crucial  that  institutions  invest  in  teacher
training  and  ensure  that  AI  tools  are  integrated  into  curricula  in  a
manner that complements pedagogical goals. Teachers must be equip-
ped not only with the technical skills to use AI but also with strategies for
blending  AI  with  traditional  teaching  methods  to  maintain  a  balance

between technological innovation and human-centered education.

Incorporating  AI tools such  as ChatGPT, Grammarly,  and Quillbot
has  the  potential  to  enhance  personalized  learning  and  improve  effi-
ciency in administrative tasks. However, as indicated by Almaraz-L´opez
et al. (2023), AI’s role in fostering collaboration and creativity remains
limited,  as  evidenced  by  students’  lower  mean  scores  on  questions
regarding  AI’s  impact  on  teamwork  and  independent  learning.  To
address these challenges, educators should focus on guiding students in
navigating AI tools critically, helping them identify when to rely on AI
and  when to engage  more deeply with  traditional learning resources.
This approach is supported by Przegalinska et al. (2019), who argue that
AI should not replace but rather augment human learning capabilities.

6.2. Limitations

The generalizability of the findings is limited due to the study being
conducted at a single public university in Pakistan. The perceptions and
experiences of teachers and students at this institution may differ from
those  at  private  universities,  other  countries,  or  K-12  settings,  where
access to technology and AI tools, as well as educational practices, may
vary. Given the correlational nature of this study, future research using
experimental  or  longitudinal  designs  would  be  necessary  to  explore
causal relationships between generative AI use and learning outcomes.
This  would  help  isolate  the  effect  of  AI  tools  from  other  potential
influencing factors. Additionally, the  sample size was relatively small
and homogenous, consisting primarily of first-year undergraduate stu-
dents, which may not reflect the views of students at different academic
levels or from diverse disciplines. A theoretical limitation of the present
study is that while UTAUT and TPACK provided the theoretical framing,
the quantitative instruments did not comprehensively operationalize all
constructs.  Thus,  the  frameworks  inform  interpretation  but  are  not
empirically validated in full. Future research should employ validated
UTAUT-  and  TPACK-based  instruments  and  incorporate  structural
modeling  to  test  hypothesized  relationships  among  constructs. Lastly,
the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to biases.
Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying these findings to
broader educational contexts.

7. Conclusion

This study explored teachers’ and students’ perceptions of generative
AI  tools  in  higher  education  through  the  combined  application  of
UTAUT and TPACK frameworks. The findings revealed that while both
groups  recognized  the  potential  of  AI  to  enhance  lesson  planning,
learning engagement, and academic performance, they also expressed
concerns  related  to  insufficient  training,  lack  of  institutional  support,
and the reliability and privacy of AI-generated content. These challenges
are  not  unique  to  Pakistan;  however,  situating  them  within  a  dual
theoretical framework provides new insights into how acceptance fac-
tors and pedagogical integration interact in shaping AI adoption. Given
its reliance on convenience sampling, a single site, and a modest sample
size,  the  study  should  be  regarded  as  exploratory.  Its  value  lies  in
providing  a  contextualized,  theory-driven  account  of  generative  AI
adoption  in  Pakistan,  offering  a  foundation  for  further  multi-site  and
cross-cultural research. For policymakers and practitioners, the findings
highlight  the  need  to  design  professional  development  programs  that
simultaneously  build  technological  skills,  pedagogical  strategies,  and
confidence  in  AI  use,  while  also  addressing  ethical  and  institutional
concerns.  Future  research  should  expand  to  diverse  institutions  and
employ larger, more representative samples to validate and extend these
insights.
