1. Introduction

Generative  Artificial  Intelligence  (GenAI)  is  rapidly  reshaping  the  higher  education  landscape.
While debates continue over the appropriateness of GenAI in teaching and learning, this paper
shifts the focus toward its practical application in entrepreneurship education (EE). It examines
how GenAI can  be meaningfully embedded into curriculum design  to develop and sustain stu-
dents'  critical  thinking.  Central  to  this  argument  is  the  claim  that  reflective  learning,  when
deliberately  scaffolded,  is  a  pivotal  pedagogical  strategy  to  uphold  critical  engagement  with
GenAI. Drawing on an intervention-based case study at an Australian higher education institution,
the  paper  details  the  implementation  of  GenAI  in  an  introductory  entrepreneurship  unit.  Data
include structured reflections from 147 students, educator reflections, and course material. The
findings  show  that  scaffolded  reflective  learning,  progressively  introduced  at  the  individual,
group, and class levels, supports critical thinking, while also enhancing student autonomy and
leadership  in  GenAI  use.  It  further  facilitates  ethical  and  contextual  awareness  that  extends
beyond academic settings, enabling what we term as “ethical” knowledge transfer into students’
future careers. Accordingly, the paper proposes a multi-level reflective framework encompassing
student, group/class, and educator reflections to guide effective GenAI integration in EE, posi-
tioning critical ethical discernment as a core competence in AI-augmented professional practice.

The rapid integration of Generative AI (GenAI) into higher education presents a fundamental paradox. From 2024 to 2025, the
proportion of undergraduate students using GenAI tools has surged from 53% to 88%, signalling its rapid normalisation in university
learning  (Freeman,  2025).  While  these  tools  promise  to  revolutionize  learning  through  unprecedented  access  to  information  and
personalised support; they simultaneously threaten to erode the critical thinking capabilities that universities have long sought to
cultivate (Gonsalves, 2026; Larson et al., 2024). This tension is particularly acute in entrepreneurship education (EE), where students
must develop both action-oriented mindsets and reflective abilities to navigate uncertainty and make strategic decisions (McMullen &
Shepherd, 2006). Despite initial institutional responses ranging from outright bans to uncritical adoption (Newton, 2025), the central
pedagogical challenge remains unaddressed: how can educators harness GenAI's potential while preserving and enhancing students'

The  integration  of  GenAI  in  business  education  occurs  at  a  critical  juncture  when  entrepreneurial  and  critical  thinking  are
increasingly recognized as complementary cognitive capabilities essential for professional success (Verzat et al., 2017). GenAI tools,
particularly ChatGPT, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities by generating human-like responses, offering research support, and
facilitating brainstorming processes (Gao et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). However, excessive reliance on these tools may impede
students' inclination to engage in independent thinking and problem-solving (Cotton, Cotton, & Shipway, 2024; Essien et al., 2024).
This concern is particularly salient in EE, where students must learn to evaluate opportunities critically under conditions of extreme
uncertainty (Sarasvathy, 2001). Recently, the  academic discourse has shifted from whether GenAI should be permitted in higher
education to how it can be meaningfully integrated while maintaining pedagogical integrity (Ratten & Jones, 2023). In EE, a growing
body of work demonstrates that GenAI can be productively embedded into the curriculum, showcasing improvements in students'
entrepreneurial ability, self-efficacy, and creativity (Gallage & Scott, 2026; Xie & Wang, 2025). This literature, however, concentrates
primarily on student performance and competency outcomes. Thus, the question of how GenAI integration affects students’ critical
thinking and reflective capacity remains comparatively underexplored, with existing studies being predominantly conceptual or re-
view based (see Gonsalves, 2026; Larson et al., 2024). Current approaches, moreover, focuses predominantly on policy development or
detection  mechanisms  rather  than  pedagogical  frameworks  that  actively  support  critical  thinking  development  in  AI-mediated
learning environments (Luo, 2024).

This study is theoretically grounded in the intersection of critical thinking (CT) theory and reflective learning (RL) pedagogy. CT,
defined by Ennis (1996) as "reasonable reflective thinking focused on deciding what to believe or do," provides the foundational
framework for understanding how students evaluate AI-generated content. RL conceptualized as "a process of reflecting on all sources
of knowledge that can help to understand a situation" (Fullana et al., 2016), offers a pedagogical mechanism for maintaining CT
capabilities in technology-enhanced environments. Recent conceptual work reinforces this convergence, arguing that reflective critical
thinking and ethical reasoning are central conditions for students to engage critically with AI-generated content rather than accept it
uncritically (Gonsalves, 2026). The convergence of these theoretical perspectives is particularly relevant in EE, where CT serves as the
cornerstone for opportunity identification, evaluation of market feasibility, and strategic resource allocation (Calma & Davies, 2026;
Neck & Greene, 2011). While the incorporation of RL or CT in management education is not novel (Calma & Davies, 2021, 2026),
research on how these capabilities can be cultivated and sustained when GenAI mediates the learning process remains underexplored.
Accordingly, this research addresses the following question: How can RL practices develop and sustain CT capabilities when GenAI
is integrated into EE? This question is examined through an intervention-based case study of an introductory entrepreneurship unit at
an Australian higher education institution, by drawing on structured reflections from 147 undergraduate students, educator obser-
vations, and course material, where GenAI tools were systematically integrated across the curriculum.

This study makes several contributions to management education literature. First, we present empirical evidence demonstrating
that structured RL can sustain CT capabilities in GenAI-integrated curricula, addressing concerns about AI-induced cognitive com-
placency (Bellary et al., 2025; Lindebaum & Fleming, 2024). Second, we develop and validate a three-stage reflective framework that
operates  across  individual,  group,  and  educator  levels,  extending  existing  RL  theory  (Fullana  et  al.,  2016)  to  GenAI-mediated
educational contexts. Third, we introduce the concept of “ethical knowledge transfer", thus extending the current conceptualisation
of  knowledge  transfer  (Dismore  et  al.,  2024).  We  argue  that  ethical  discernment  in  knowledge  transfer  is  a  critical  dimension  in
GenAI-integrated education, to recognise not all AI-mediated knowledge is inherently beneficial or accurate; and that students must be
equipped to evaluate the accuracy and the ethical implications of AI-generated content (Hannigan et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023).
Fourth, our findings reveal that educator reflection is equally crucial as student reflection in AI-integrated pedagogy, challenging the
predominantly  student-centred  focus  of  current  RL  literature  (Myllykoski-Laine  et  al.,  2024).  These  contributions  offer  practical
guidance for educators navigating the integration of GenAI while providing theoretical advancement to understand how traditional
pedagogical approaches must evolve in response to GenAI. Furthermore, as graduates are increasingly required to work with GenAI,
the capacity to critically and ethically reflect on AI outputs will constitute a key employability concern, not solely an academic matter.
The  paper  concludes  by  proposing  future  research  directions  for  GenAI  integration  in  management  education.  Throughout,  we
demonstrate that rather than viewing GenAI as either friend or foe, educators can leverage RL to transform it into a powerful tool for
developing critical, ethical, and entrepreneurially minded graduates.

2. Literature review

CT is pivotal in higher education, with institutes actively fostering CT skills in students (Erikson & Erikson, 2019; Puig et al., 2019),
and it holds equal significance in professional domains (Kirby, 2004). Calma and Davies (2021) argue that, just as CT helps health care
professional make decisions to save lives or assist lawyers in supporting their clients, it is critical to how business practitioners make
strategic decisions in developing and sustaining a successful business. In business settings, CT can support the development of strategic
thinking in leadership and decision making, it is also an important element in entrepreneurial mindset and improves ethical reasoning
(Bloch & Spataro, 2014; Verzat et al., 2017). How these skills are developed in the business education is critical to whether they are
instilled in graduates (Calma & Davies, 2021).

Ennis's conceptualisation of CT is the most widely used definition in management learning (Calma & Davies, 2026) According to
Ennis (1998), for thinking to be considered critical, it must be reasonable, meaning it should not be arbitrary, nonsensical, or without
purpose. It must also be reflective, implying it goes beyond surface-level responses or impulsive reactions. Furthermore, it should be
focused, encouraging a deliberate pause in decision-making. Finally, CT should lead to an informed outcome—either forming a belief or
action. While this process does not always result in action, the possibility is always present. Accordingly, reflective thought is primary

CT can be taught and learnt (Niu et al., 2013), developed and improved through purposefully designed curriculum (Halpern, 1996).
Accordingly, institutions could specifically design teaching pedagogies specifically to develop CT skills, and there is a growing body of
education research into how teaching may lead to the development of CT (Ennis, 2018). There is, however, concern in academia that
accessibility of technological advancements is impeding the development of CT in business students.

2.1. Promoting CT in EE

EE particularly benefits from CT, as entrepreneurs constantly engage in decision-making under conditions of extreme uncertainty
(McMullen  &  Shepherd,  2006).  Unlike  other  business  disciplines  that  may  operate  with  established  frameworks  and  predictable
variables, entrepreneurship exists in "true uncertainty" (Sarasvathy, 2001). Entrepreneurs must employ rigorous analytical reasoning
to navigate complex, ill-structured problems with incomplete information and high stakes (Garcia, 2009), making CT the cornerstone
for opportunity identification, evaluation of market feasibility, and strategic resource allocation (Bloch &  Spataro, 2014; Neck &
Greene, 2011).

While CT and entrepreneurial thinking are distinct cognitive processes, they share a symbiotic relationship that is valuable in EE.
Entrepreneurial thinking, characterized by opportunity recognition, creative problem-solving, and comfort with ambiguity, drives the
generation of novel business possibilities, while CT enables the rigorous evaluation of these possibilities for feasibility and value (Neck
&  Greene,  2011;  Sarasvathy,  2001).  When  entrepreneurs  combine  both  thinking  modes,  they  develop  more  robust  ventures  by
balancing creative ideation with analytical assessment (Verzat et al., 2017). The reflective dimension of CT is particularly crucial for
entrepreneurs, who must constantly evaluate their assumptions and adapt on the basis of evidence rather than wishful thinking (Calma
&  Davies,  2026),  enabling  students  to  generate  innovative  business  concepts  and  systematically  evaluate  their  viability  through
evidence-based decision-making (Larson et al., 2024; Walmsley & Wraae, 2022).

Despite recognition of CT's importance in EE, significant barriers impede its development. EE often faces a fundamental tension
between  cultivating  action-oriented  mindsets  and  developing  reflective  analytical  abilities  (Calma  &  Davies,  2021),  leading  to
pedagogical approaches that prioritize quick decision-making and execution over deeper critical evaluation (Bloch & Spataro, 2014).
The  entrepreneurial  narrative  frequently  celebrates  rapid  iteration  and  "failing  fast,"  which,  while  valuable  for  innovation,  can
inadvertently undermine the deliberative pause that is essential for CT (Ennis, 1998). Garcia (2009) notes that business students are
frequently encouraged to value decisiveness and confidence—traits that, while important for entrepreneurs, can conflict with the
willingness to question assumptions and consider alternative perspectives that characterize CT. This challenge is compounded by the
pressure to develop immediately marketable skills that appear more tangibly valuable than reflective capabilities whose benefits may
seem less direct (Kirby, 2004).

As the following section will explore, reflective capacity becomes even more essential with GenAI integration, where students must
not only critically evaluate entrepreneurial opportunities but also critically assess the AI-generated content that increasingly informs
their decision-making processes (Larson et al., 2024).

2.2. Impact of GenAI on CT

GenAI has numerous benefits. It can help students access a range of perspectives and information (Hyde et al., 2024), engage in
nuanced conversations, and, with ease of use, provide responses that close resemble human communication (O'Dea, 2024; Yang et al.,
2024). However, scholars are acutely aware of a more foundational issue with GenAI, that it appears to lower the capability and
disposition to engage in meaningful CT (Lindebaum & Fleming, 2024).

First, GenAI provides responses rapidly and with confidence, which can cause users to assume the output is accurate, unbiased and
well-structured, despite it being shaped by potentially biased training data (Bender et al., 2021, pp. 610–623). While GenAI makes
information more accessible, it often provides content of questionable quality or “hallucinations”, and in some cases, incorrect in-
formation (Hannigan et al., 2024; Lindebaum & Fleming, 2024). This can discourage students from engaging in critical research, as
they may over-rely on GenAI-generated content. Consequently, they may not notice or question the hidden assumptions, nuances or
perspectives that underpin the responses, nor recognise the viewpoints that are excluded (Larson et al., 2024).

Second, GenAI's language can appear human-like, often demonstrating empathy and readability. This can lead users to place both
emotional and intellectual trust in the system (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). This can create a false sense of interpersonal connection and
understanding, despite lacking the embodied experience that shapes authentic human thought and may reduce a user's likelihood of
engaging in independent CT (Larson et al., 2024). Third, research has shown that GenAI systems are capable of unethical actions, such
as lying, falsification or deception, not only when prompted to deceive (Park et al., 2023), but also when no such instruction was given
(Scheurer et al., 2023). The implications of relying on GenAI without critical engagement are serious. Students may unknowingly use
inaccurate information and are less likely to verify the content against other sources. Furthermore, they risk reinforcing existing biases
in AI's training data (Bianchi et al., 2023; Bellary et al., 2025). Business schools therefore have a responsibility to equip future pro-
fessionals,  who  will  almost  certainly  interact  with  AI  tools  in  the  workplace,  with  the  ability  to  critically  assess  and  challenge
AI-generated content (English, 2023; Vecchiarini & Somia, 2023).

The  nature  of  this  engagement,  however,  varies  with  how  GenAI  is  positioned  pedagogically.  Recent  study  in  the  EE  context
identified that students valued ChatGPT primarily for its efficiency, particularly its capability for faster idea generation, while also
perceiving its outputs as formulaic and “robotic” (Vecchiarini & Somia, 2023). Thus, without deliberate pedagogical structuring, the

2.3. RL as a pedological tool to promote CT in EE

The ability to reflect is critical to CT (Erikson & Erikson, 2019). This includes the capability to critically reflect on personal ex-
periences and social processes. Pnevmatikos et al. (2019) suggest that reflections enable students to challenge their value system and
adopt more humanistic values. When students are encouraged to reflect on the process of decision-making and examine whether the
preferable solution is feasible, it offers the opportunity for genuine, self-directed, inquiry-based learning, either individually or in
group settings (Harding-Smith, 1993). RL is “a process of reflecting on all sources of knowledge that can help to understand a situation,
including personal sources and experience” (Fullana et al., 2016, p. 1009). RL emphasises reflective thinking to encourage students to
transform experience and create their own meanings. This process is centred on the individual, their emotions and feelings, self and
subjectivity in the construction of knowledge and a contextualised learning process (Dyer & Hurd, 2016). RL can help to avoid overly
technical teaching focused on procedures and instead enable students to cope with ambiguity and uncertainty in their future pro-
fessional practice (Miller &  Konstantinou, 2022), to resolve complex situations and not limit themselves to applying preconceived
formula and develop capacity for CT (Fullana et al., 2016).

EE has evolved beyond traditional lecture-based methods to incorporate a variety of pedagogical approaches specifically designed
to foster CT. Effective EE requires methods that develop students' capacity to evaluate opportunities critically and make reasoned
judgments under uncertainty (Neck & Greene, 2011). Experiential approaches including business simulations, venture creation pro-
jects, and field-based learning, have proven particularly effective in fostering CT in authentic entrepreneurial contexts (Verzat et al.,
2017). Case studies have also proven especially valuable, as they present students with complex entrepreneurial dilemmas requiring
them to analyze multiple perspectives, identify assumptions, and evaluate possible actions (Calma & Davies, 2021). These approaches
are  often  enhanced  through  structured  reflection  activities  that  prompt  students  to  critically  examine  their  decision-making  and
challenge  their  assumptions  (Pnevmatikos  et  al.,  2019).  From  a  critical  pedagogy  lens,  Walmsley  and  Wraae  (2022) argue  that
reflection and the educator–student relationship are central to EE's transformative and emancipatory potential whereby equipping
students to question assumptions rather than uncritically reproduce them. This capacity is becoming increasingly important as stu-
dents prepare to navigate business landscapes influenced by GenAI.

Accordingly, RL represents a particularly promising approach to enhance CT capabilities within EE. By engaging in structured
reflections, students develop metacognitive awareness. RL enables students to move beyond merely absorbing entrepreneurial con-
cepts to critically examining the assumptions and biases underpinning both their own thinking and established wisdom in practice
(Pnevmatikos et al., 2019). This calls for a shift from questioning whether to allow GenAI in education, to exploring how it should be
embedded to enhance learning experiences. This shift necessitates a robust CT frameworks in EE, to ensure students develop both
action-oriented and analytical capabilities needed for entrepreneurial success in an AI-enhanced world.

Educator's awareness of their teaching process is also critical to student success. Reflection is important for educator development,
and it is  through this process educators can think about their teaching and  teaching experiences (Myllykoski-Laine et  al., 2024).
Surface-level reflections focus on strategies and methods used to achieve teaching goals, with educators exploring what works rather
than the values, beliefs and assumptions underlying their strategies (Jay & Johnson, 2002). At the level of pedagogical reflection,
educators  reflect  on  theories  underlying  their  teaching  and  connect  theoretical  principles  with  practice  (Larrivee,  2008).  Critical
reflection involves the examination of the educators own personal and professional belief systems. Decisions at surface-level reflections
are made for efficiency, while decisions at the pedagogical level are grounded on value judgment, and decisions at the critical level are
based on worth judgment. Educators move from initially asking ‘Am I doing it right?’ to eventually asking ‘Is this the right thing to do?’
(Larrivee, 2008). In the context of CT and GenAI integration, we suggest that RL should include not just the student, but also the
educator.

In  this  paper  we  argue  that  RL  is  vital  to  promote  CT  skills  when  embedding  GenAI  into  business  curriculum,  and  to  ensure
transferable  skills  in  EE.  This  paper  addresses  several  gaps  in  literature.  First,  the  paper  contributes  to  CT  studies  in  the  under-
researched area of business education (Calma &  Davies, 2021). There are visibly more articles on CT in the medical, nursing and
health studies than in business (Chan, 2018; Reale et al., 2018). While recently scholarship has explored CT in management education
(for example, Calma & Davies, 2026), there is a lack of research exploring how CT is cultivated and/or sustained when GenAI mediates
learning. Second, this paper adds to the exploration and development of CT in the context of GenAI, which, despite much debate over
GenAI's potential to enhance and impede individuals CT, is yet to be adequately understood in the context of management education
(Bellary et al., 2025; Larson et al., 2024). Recent contributions in this field remain largely conceptual, and therefore some argue that
reflective and ethical engagement is required for students to think critically with AI rather than defer to it (Gonsalves, 2026). Third,
this paper contributes to the development of a reflective teaching and learning framework to promote CT, that encompasses both
learner and educator reflections in the process of embedding GenAI into EE. This paper is the first to explore the combination of CT, RL
and GenAI in teaching and learning entrepreneurship in higher education.

3. Methodology

This paper reports on case study of an Australian undergraduate introductory entrepreneurship unit embedding GenAI into the
curriculum. The integration of GenAI into Australian higher education has occurred within a rapidly evolving regulatory landscape.

The Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA), serving as Australia's national higher education regulator, established

3.1. The intervention structure

In this case study, GenAI is integrated within a second-year undergraduate level introductory entrepreneurship unit through a
structured intervention. The use of interventions, specifically, the implementation of teaching and learning activities, in EE is not novel
(see Cris¸an et al., 2024). Interventions have been employed to develop and nurture entrepreneurial mind-set, focusing on the skills,
behaviours, attributes, knowledge, and values that characterize entrepreneurs (Bosman et al., 2019; Daniel, 2016). Research also
demonstrates that structured interventions, such as GenAI supported education interventions, significantly enhance both entrepre-
neurial self-efficacy and entrepreneurial intention (Xie & Wang, 2025). This intervention was designed to prioritize critical engage-
ment with GenAI prompts and outputs rather than utilising it simply to maximise efficiency, recognizing that without intentional
pedagogical design, students are likely to resort to GenAI simply as a tool for efficiency (Vecchiarini & Somia, 2023).

The intervention deliberately embedded GenAI tools, primarily ChatGPT, into classroom activities where students engaged indi-
vidually,  in  pairs,  and  in  collaborative  groups.  Rather  than  prohibiting  these  emerging  technologies,  the  pedagogical  approach
positioned GenAI as an explicit learning tool. The intervention included activities where students utilized ChatGPT to explore and
discuss entrepreneurial case stories, formulate relevant questions/prompts to deepen their theoretical understanding through appli-
cations of real entrepreneurial experiences, and critically reflect on AI-generated information. These activities were structured to
gradually  progress  from  prompt  formulation  towards  critical  engagement  and  evaluation  of  GenAI  outputs  to  support  reflective,
ethical, disciplinary engagement that is required to facilitate and sustain CT with GenAI (Gonsalves, 2026). Students were system-
atically guided through these activities following a step-by-step protocol designed to cultivate both domain-specific entrepreneurial
knowledge and critical digital literacy competencies (see Appendix A). The unit comprised 12 hybrid-delivery lessons totalling 36
contact hours. Each session included approximately 1 h of GenAI-integrated activities, with students participating both in-person and
online.

Primary  data  collection  employed  a  structured  self-reflective  questionnaire  survey  developed  by  drawing  on  Ellmers's  (2014)
four-stage model of structured critical reflective practice (Appendix B). A structured reflective instrument with scaffolded reflective
prompts was chosen deliberately to guide evaluative engagement with GenAI rather than assume it, consistent with evidence that CT
must be purposefully cultivated rather than left to emerge (Calma & Davies, 2026). The qualitative survey consisted of a series of
open-ended  questions,  crafted  by  the  research  team,  and  centred  on  Ellmers  (2014) critical  reflective  practice.  The  survey  was
self-administered, with questions presented in a fixed standard order to all the students (Braun et al., 2021). Students responded by
typing their reflections rather than selecting pre-determined response options, producing rich and complex reflections, particularly
useful for qualitative research. Students were informed about the research at the start of the unit, and the reflective survey hosted on
Microsoft Forms, was distributed for completion outside class time after the final lesson.

The educator also engaged in an ongoing observation and reflection of the intervention (Myllykoski-Laine et al., 2024). Building on

the Larrivee (2008), during the intervention, the educator was guided by several open-ended questions:

• What went well in this lesson? Why?
• What problems did I experience? Why?
• How engaged and active were the students?
• What could I have done differently?
• What did I learn from this experience that will help me in future lessons?

This process was recorded, and the reflective notes were used to revise the learning process to ensure continuous improvement in

the teaching and learning process.

3.3. Ethical considerations

This research received ethical approval from the institutional ethics committee. Ethical considerations were carefully integrated
into the research process, particularly due to the sensitive nature of collecting data from students. The initial plan was to administer the
structured reflections in-class. However, to address ethical concerns, the surveys were provided to students to complete outside of
class. This approach mitigated the inherent power imbalance between educator and student, which could otherwise pressure students
into participating and compromise the voluntary nature of data collection. Informed consent was obtained at the point of survey
submission, and participation was not mandatory.

3.4. Analysis

This research employed thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Thematic analysis is a flexible method to identify, organise and
offer insights into patterns of meaning across a data set. The analysis process was hybrid, which included deductive coding by drawing
on the CT model by Ennis (1996) and RL conceptualisation by Fullana et al. (2016). The inductive thematic coding was followed by
mapping codes and themes from the data. In the first stage of the analytical process, both authors familiarised themselves with the
data. A codebook (Appendix C) was developed to guide the data analysis process. In the development of the codebook, authors relied
on the conceptual definitions of the key terms and allowed the definitions to evolve inductively. Analyst triangulation was employed,
with  the  first  and  second  author  independently  analysing  the  same  reflective  data  to  compare  findings  (Patton,  2014).  This  was
particularly valuable as the second author, who conducted the intervention, offered an insider perspective, while the first author
provided an outsider, objective lens. Through collaborative analysis and discussion, several recurring patterns emerged around the

4. Findings

This section presents findings from the introductory entrepreneurship unit integrating GenAI through RL to sustain CT. The findings
reveal a multi-level reflective framework operating at individual, group, and educator levels (Table 2), demonstrating how RL develops
and sustains CT while enabling ethical knowledge transfer.

4.1. RL sustains CT through deliberate questioning and iterative refinement

Student reflections indicate metacognitive engagement with GenAI, involving deliberate questioning of approaches, evaluation of
effectiveness, and iterative refinement of methods. As one student noted: “One significant realization for me was that the way I posed the
questions had a significant impact on the calibre of AI responses. The responses significantly improved after I began creating questions that were
more targeted …” (Student_116).

Similar understandings emerged across cohorts, where students expressed surprise at AI's limitations, highlighting the learner's
essential role as leader in the process actively questioning, evaluating, and refining communication rather than becoming dependent
on simplistic AI responses:

I first thought AI would just "know" what I needed, but I soon discovered that the quality of its answers depends totally on how precisely
and clearly, I frame my cues. This helped me to think more deliberately, an ability useful in research, communication, and problem-
solving that transcends mere application of AI (Student_109).

Students demonstrated capacity not only for purposive prompting but also for critical reflection of AI outputs. They identified

inaccuracies and recognized that AI often focuses more on theory than practical applications. One student reflected:

"I encountered challenges while using AI tools. At times, the responses were too generic, especially when prompts lacked specificity. There
were also inaccuracies and a tendency for the AI to focus more on theory than practical application. To overcome this by becoming more
specific with my prompts and talking through the outputs with my group made a difference" (Student_118).

This illustrates the interaction between individual and group-level reflection: students first identify AI limitations through personal
engagement, then validate and extend their understanding through collaborative discussion. This progression from accepting GenAI
outputs to interrogating them reflects movement towards higher-order critical engagement that is essential when GenAI mediates
learning (Gonsalves, 2026).

Through this process, students were able to understand that GenAI is not necessarily a decision-maker, particularly in entrepre-
neurship,  but  rather  a  tool  that  supports  decision-making.  This  metacognitive  reflection  on  the  role  of  GenAI  in  entrepreneurial
contexts is essential, as it helps students recognise their autonomy in making decisions. Moreover, it reinforces their identity as critical
thinkers, which is key to preventing overreliance on AI: “Even though AI can't make decision, AI-driven analytics help entrepreneurs make
data-backed decisions” (Student_5). By maintaining CT capabilities throughout AI interaction, students develop sophisticated judgment
that upholds intellectual rigor while leveraging AI capabilities.

However, students did not master efficient prompting immediately. Educator reflections indicate most students initially struggled
to craft effective prompts beyond those provided, with common challenges including vague prompts leading to irrelevant responses. In
response,  the  intervention  adopted  progressive  scaffolding  (Appendix  A):  starting  with  simple  individual  tasks  and  gradually
advancing to complex applications at group and class levels to support collective meaning-making. These activities required original
thinking before AI use, such as initial reading, brainstorming, or discussions. This finding highlights the importance of first equipping
students with foundational subject knowledge, which then enables them to critically evaluate AI outputs.

4.2. RL cultivates autonomous CT and develops independent judgement

Student reflections revealed the development of metacognitive awareness, enabling them to critically examine their own thinking
processes, question underlying assumptions, and make independent decisions regarding the use of AI. These reflections captured a
progression  from  initial  hesitation  toward  a  more  nuanced  understanding  of  AI's  role  in  entrepreneurship.  Students  moved  from
uncertainty to recognizing AI as a supportive tool, while simultaneously cultivating their own judgment about its appropriate use. One
student articulated: “At first, I believed AI could replace human decision-making because of its detailed, and logical responses. However,
through the activity, I came to understand that AI should complement human judgment, not replace it”  (Student_134). This reflection il-
lustrates the shift from passive engagement to active evaluation, where students challenge their assumptions and develop informed
perspectives. The intervention encouraged students to sustain autonomy as learners and users of AI, rather than relying on it un-
critically: “[T]his endeavour significantly changed my perspective. By the end, I regarded AI as a valuable component of the entrepreneurial
process,  despite  my  initial  uncertainty  and  hesitancy.  It's  not  flawless,  but  when  combined  with  creativity  and  critical  thinking  …”  (Stu-
dent_116). Such reflections demonstrate how students began to see themselves differently as learners, recognizing entrepreneurship as
a meaningful pursuit shaped by human interaction and values. This assertion of autonomy and leadership reflects the emancipatory
educator–student relationship that is critical to EE (Walmsley &  Wraae, 2022). The reflective process fostered CT by encouraging
students to question their own preconceptions and AI outputs. RL enables students to move beyond merely absorbing entrepreneurial

The educator plays an important role in guiding students to challenge assumptions through structured activities, helping them
understand entrepreneurship and AI limitations naturally by moving from individual to pairs to groups, exposing them to diverse
perspectives. Student_23 reflects: "I was surprised by how collaborative and iterative the learning process became. I expected more inde-

between individual and group-level reflection that characterizes the framework.

Importantly, not all students experienced a shift in belief. One student maintained scepticism: “To be honest, my perception didn't
change much. I went in thinking AI wouldn't be helpful for creativity or original thinking, and I still feel that way. It was only really useful for
quick facts or surface-level research” (Student_120). This resistance is equally valuable, as it reflects the development of autonomous
judgment. Students were not directed to adopt a particular stance on AI; rather, they were supported in forming their own informed
conclusions.

4.3. RL facilitates ethical knowledge transfer through critical awareness

The findings indicate students actively reflect on their ability to integrate AI experiences across academic and professional contexts.
This demonstrates the potential for knowledge transfer, a critical component of higher education (Dismore et al., 2024), that sustains
CT  while  leveraging  AI  across  diverse  applications.  Knowledge  transfer  involves  the  movement  of  productive  knowledge  from
knowledge holders to knowledge recipients such as businesses and community organizations (Cantú et al., 2009). A significant novel
finding highlights how RL enables ethical knowledge transfer, the process by which students develop critical awareness of ethical
dimensions of AI use and carry that awareness to future professional contexts.

4.3.1. Emergence of ethical awareness

Students demonstrated organic emergence of ethical considerations without explicit prompting from the educator. As one student
observed: "I believe ethical considerations around AI use need deeper exploration, especially regarding data privacy and bias. Improving AI's
accuracy and reliability is also crucial for trustworthy decision making" (Student_118). Another student noted concerns about AI providing
overly optimistic responses that failed to mention unethical aspects or issues regarding trust and privacy (Student_7). Students also
identified how relying too heavily on AI could lead to biased thinking: "I will no longer rely too much on AI, causing my own ideas to be
biased" (Student_4).

When  students  identified  GenAI  errors,  educators  leveraged  these  as  teachable  moments  to  discuss  intellectual  property,  bias
amplification, and ethical responsibility. Guided questions such as "Who might be harmed by uncritical AI adoption?" and "What values
should guide our AI use?" prompted students to reflect on ethical practices. This educator-level intervention demonstrates the facil-
itative role in the multi-level framework, transforming individual discoveries into collective ethical learning.

4.3.2. Developing ethical judgement capabilities

These reflections highlight another core outcome of the intervention, where students not only identified ethical considerations but
developed strategies for navigating ethical challenges in AI use. One student described a practical approach to mitigating AI limita-
tions: “AI tools do have limitations. Their creativity is restricted by existing data, and sometimes they generate inaccurate or non-existent
sources. To overcome this, I learned to cross-check information from multiple sources and ask more specific questions” (Student_134). This
reflects critical verification skills essential for responsible AI integration. Another student articulated the importance of understanding
AI ethics for business contexts: "I think I need to explore more how to use AI in a simple, cost-effective way in a business. Also, I'd like to get
better at understanding AI ethics and how it really affects different customers" (Student_1).

Students recognized that verification was essential for responsible AI use in professional contexts. One student reflected: "When
using AI tools in the future I believe that it is crucial to verify the output the AI provides as occasionally its output can be incorrect" (Student_3).
Another noted the importance of balancing AI with human creativity and ethics: "Initially, I saw AI as a purely technical tool, but through
the activity, I realized its strategic value in entrepreneurship for innovation and efficiency. I also recognized the importance of balancing AI with
human creativity and ethics" (Student_12).

4.3.3. Transfer to professional practice

Student reflections demonstrated meaningful transfer of knowledge from their current roles as learners to their anticipated pro-
fessional practices: “Future business endeavours and decision-making can undoubtedly benefit from the knowledge I've received from this
experience. AI is now a useful corporate tool in my opinion, not only for research but also for increasing productivity, efficiency, and strategic
planning” (Student_116). This represents a fundamental outcome of the intervention: students not only developed reflective skills but
also demonstrated the ability to envision future AI applications beyond the classroom. One student articulated this perspective: “As
someone planning to become a mortgage broker, I can see AI playing a big role in making my future work more efficient and client focused. AI
could help me analyze market trends, personalize client recommendations, and streamline the application process” (Student_129).

Students  identified  continuous  learning  strategies  including  following  topic  experts,  attending  workshops,  refining  prompting
capabilities, critically appraising AI-generated content, experimenting with new AI technologies, and applying tools to real entre-
preneurial projects. One student noted: "The insights from this experience can be applied by leveraging AI for data-driven decision-making,
market analysis, and operational efficiency in future projects. Emphasizing ethical AI use and balancing automation with human creativity will
ensure responsible entrepreneurial decision-making" (Student_12). This forward-looking mindset reflects commitment to lifelong learning
while maintaining ethical awareness.

5. Discussion

Empirical research remains indecisive on the role of GenAI in facilitating or diminishing CT. Most academic discourse to date has
focused on assessment redevelopment to counter GenAI, to detect misuse, and navigate GenAI in academia through policy (Lim et al.,
2023; Luo, 2024). Instead, this research develops a structured pedagogical approach to sustain CT in an AI-integrated curriculum.
Following a structured intervention, our findings highlight the crucial role of RL as a pedagogical mechanism that upholds CT in an
GenAI-integrated curriculum, enabling students to maintain intellectual autonomy while leveraging AI capabilities effectively and
facilitating ethical knowledge transfer. Accordingly, this research makes several contributions to management education literature,
particularly from an EE perspective.

First, while Ennis's (1996) foundational conceptualisation of CT is a widely used framework for understanding the core charac-
teristics of CT (Calma & Davis, 2026), this work was developed prior to the emergence of GenAI-mediated learning environments.
Recent studies highlight the added complexities introduced by GenAI, particularly how the system efficiencies may discourage learners
capability to critically engage with and evaluate GenAI-generated information (Bearman et al., 2024; Hyde et al., 2024; Lindebaum &
Fleming, 2024). While research further suggests that use of GenAI in classroom activities can deepen students' understanding of the
business  environment  and  promote  CT  (Vecchiarini  &  Somia,  2023),  empirical  research  does  not  clearly  articulate  how
GenAI-integrated  learning  activities  can  be  embedded  to  management  education.  This  study  illustrates  a  case  of  designing  and
implementing an EE curriculum that supports CT development in Gen-AI embedded learning environments (see Appendix A; Fig. 1).
Ennis (1996) characterised CT as reasonable, reflective thinking focused on deciding what to believe or do, resulting in informed
outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that CT characteristics manifest distinctly in such environments, as students must navigate and
critically engage with GenAI-generated information. Student reflections reveal purposeful engagement with GenAI, avoiding arbitrary
interactions  while  critically  reviewing  and  refining prompts  to  align  with  learning goals.  Thus,  this study  extends  Ennis's  (1996)
framework  by  demonstrating  that  two  established  CT  abilities,  judging  source  credibility  and  reflective  self-assessment,  operate
interdependently in GenAI-mediated contexts, where students must assess the reliability of GenAI outputs and the quality of their own
reasoning. Accordingly, management education should position CT not only as central to teaching disciplinary knowledge, but also as a

Second, prior research has widely examined RL in higher education contexts, establishing its importance for developing higher-
order thinking skills (Liu et al., 2025; Myllykoski-Laine et al., 2025). However, this research advances RL theory by applying it to
an  evolving  GenAI-mediated  classroom  environment.  While  RL  has  been  widely  studied  in  higher  education  contexts  (Liu  et  al.
202Myllykoski-Laine  et  al.,  2025),  its  application  in  GenAI-mediated  learning  environments  remains  underexplored.  Emerging
scholarship raises concerns that GenAI may hinder student reflectivity, potentially diminishing CT capabilities (Larson et al., 2024;
Lindebaum & Fleming, 2024). In contrast, our findings suggest that CT can be sustained, and even enhanced, when RL practices are
intentionally embedded to GenAI-mediated learning activities. This intervention highlights that RL helps students understand that
GenAI is not a decision-maker, but a tool that supports entrepreneurial (business) decision-making. This realization empowers students
to define their roles in entrepreneurial contexts and assert their autonomy and leadership in decision-making. Building on these in-
sights,  this  research  contributes  to  theory  by  extending  the  conceptualisation  of  RL  (Fullana  et  al.,  2016)  into  GenAI-mediated
management education settings. Specifically, it positions RL as a foundational mechanism underpinning the development of CT in
AI-enhanced environments and in evolving student-AI learning processes.

Third,  research  on  RL  has  predominantly  emphasised  individual  reflection  as  an  individual  cognitive  process,  emphasizing
learners’ self-directed sensemaking and critical evaluation (Fullana et al., 2016). While this work has been instrumental in establishing
the importance of RL in higher education, it has largely overlooked the potential for reflection to operate across multiple, interacting
levels within increasingly complex learning environment. Our findings challenge this individual focus by highlighting that CT emerges
more robustly through the interaction of multi-level reflection, spanning individual, group, and educator level reflective processes. At
the individual level, for example, students engaging with GenAI prompts recognized the need for deliberate questioning, strategic
evaluation, and iterative refinement of their input. Group activities amplified this process, where students observed peers achieving
different outcomes, prompting critical reflection: Why are the results different? What strategies or prompts led to these variations? At the
class level, educator  synthesised these experiences and  integrated disciplinary knowledge,  enabling students to consider multiple
perspectives and deepen their CT processes. Building on these insights, this research extends RL by reconceptualising it as a multi-level
construct that is essential for fostering CT in GenAI-mediated learning environments. The emergence of CT in individual tasks, group,
and class interactions provide richer opportunities for students to engage in comparison, dialogue, and perspective-taking. Multi-level
RL encourages learners to interrogate their own understanding, to question their experiences, and be questioned by their peers. This
scaffolding is especially important in introductory EE, where students are still developing foundational skills in both reflection and AI
literacy. This contribution also responds to recent calls to recognise broader manifestations of CT in management education beyond
written  assessments  (Davis  &  Calma  2026),  highlighting  the  importance  of  interactive  formats  such  as  classroom  discussion  and
presentations. Accordingly, this study advances RL by positioning multi-level reflection as a necessary condition for maximising CT
development in GenAI-mediated educational settings.

Lastly, previous research highlights that students are identifying challenges associated with the use of GenAI, including perceptions
that outputs  can be  overly formulaic, lacking  creativity, or “robotic”  in  nature (Vecchiarini &  Somia, 2023). In  parallel, existing
literature has raised concerns with GenAI, including its nature to perpetuate the propagation of misinformation, bias, and intellectual
property violations if not managed ethically (Bellary et al., 2025; Hannigan et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023). The findings of this research
demonstrate that students not only recognise functional limitations of GenAI, but also critically engage with its ethical implications.
Specifically, students identified how the use of GenAI outputs affect both their learning processes and their future professional re-
sponsibilities, indicating a more sophisticated and reflexive engagement with technology. Thus, leads to the final contribution of this
study extending existing knowledge transfer literature by introducing the concept of ethical knowledge transfer as a distinct theoretical
dimension  (Dismore  et  al.,  2024).  While  previous  scholarship  has  examined  knowledge  transfer  as  the  movement  of  productive
knowledge from holders to recipients (Cantú et al., 2009; Dismore et al., 2024), this literature has not adequately addressed the quality
of transferred knowledge. Our findings reveal that not all knowledge transferred from education to professional practice is ethically
sound. We extend knowledge transfer theory (Cantú et al., 2009; Dismore et al., 2024) by proposing that ethical knowledge transfer
requires learners to develop critical awareness enabling them to discriminate between knowledge that upholds and knowledge that
compromises ethical standards. This constitutes a significant theoretical contribution, positioning critical ethical discernment as a
necessary competence for effective knowledge transfer in GenAI- enhanced management education contexts.

5.1. Reflective teaching and learning framework to promote CT in GenAI-integrated curriculum in EE

As a practical contribution, this research proposes a reflective teaching and learning framework designed to promote CT in a GenAI-
integrated curriculum in EE (see Fig. 1). The framework operationalises the study's theoretical contributions by translating them into a
structured pedagogical practices that advance CT development in AI-mediated learning environments.

In implementing the framework in the classroom, we offer several practical recommendations for educators. First, educators should
design learning activities that intentionally foster reflection at individual, group, and class levels. This multilevel reflective process is
particularly  important  in  large  classrooms  where  students  subject  knowledge  and  AI  experience  varies  greatly.  The  scaffolded
approach, beginning with structured prompts for individual tasks and progressing to collaborative and class level activities, provides a
replicable model for integrating GenAI while maintaining CT development.

Second, educator engagement in reflective activity is valuable for iteratively refining interventions (Larrivee, 2008). For example,
early intervention sessions revealed that some students were hesitant to use GenAI due to ethical or privacy concerns. Accordingly,
educator's reflection of the teaching process enabled pre-planning of contingencies for future class activities, considering students with
varying stances on GenAI, ensuring inclusivity in learning design. In the context of GenAI, a rapidly evolving classroom tool, it is

critical for educators to engage in reflection to remain contextually aware, critical of one's own assumptions, and adapt pedagogical
strategies accordingly (Myllykoski-Laine et al., 2024).

Third, the emphasis on ethical knowledge transfer has direct implications for professional preparation. The intervention enabled
students to deliberately pause in decision making, critically examine their evolving roles in GenAI collaboration, and consider how
GenAI might be used in future employment or their future entrepreneurial journeys. Students explored continuous learning strategies
to adapt to the evolving GenAI landscape while contextualising emerging ethical issues within their career pathways. This prepares
graduates who can navigate ethical challenges without compromising personal values or professional integrity.

5.2. Future research and conclusion

While this research offers valuable insights into the integration of GenAI and RL in EE, several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, the study was conducted within a specific context. Although this limits generalizability, this framework has potential applica-
bility across other business disciplines. Future research could replicate and adapt this framework in varied educational settings and
subject areas to assess its broader relevance and effectiveness. Second, due to ethical considerations, a structured reflection activity
was implemented as a non-compulsory ungraded post-class exercise. This may have influenced student engagement and depth of
reflection. Future studies should explore the impact of integrating reflective activities into the formal curriculum, either as in-class
exercises or as assessed components, to better understand how structure and incentives affect student outcomes.

Third,  while  this  study  applied  Ellmers’  (2014) structured  reflection  model  to  capture  RL  at  an  individual  level,  alternative
reflective models may yield different insights. Student reflections in this study indicate that a structured approach to RL is particularly
effective in guiding introductory undergraduate level students through the process of developing CT in GenAI-integrated environ-
ments. However, future research could explore with other frameworks, or unstructured reflections, to compare their effectiveness in
fostering CT and knowledge transfer in GenAI-integrated learning environments.

Despite limitations, the proposed framework offers a transferable approach for developing and sustaining CT in higher education.
Its theoretical grounding in extended CT and RL theories, combined with the novel contribution of ethical knowledge transfer, provides
a foundation for future scholarship. The framework's adaptability, emphasis on ethical engagement, and dual focus on student and
educator  reflection  position  it  as  a  valuable  resource  for  navigating  the  evolving  landscape  of  GenAI-  enhanced  education.  For
management education research, this research reframes GenAI from a threat to be policed into a pedagogical resource to be reflectively
harnessed.
