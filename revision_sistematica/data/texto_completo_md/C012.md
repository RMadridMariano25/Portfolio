Introduction
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly reshaped the
landscape of higher education, offering new opportunities to enhance teaching and learning
processes. AI-powered systems, such as intelligent tutoring systems, generative AI tools, and
adaptive learning platforms, enable personalized learning experiences and improve students’
76

engagement and efficiency. Recent studies have emphasized that AI integration can support
higher-order thinking skills and academic achievement, particularly in digital learning
environments (Badawi & Adiyono, 2026; Holmes et al., 2019; Ke et al., 2024; Zawacki-
Richter et al., 2019). Despite these advancements, empirical evidence regarding how AI
influences students’ academic performance remains inconclusive, particularly in terms of the
underlying learning mechanisms.
From a theoretical perspective, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) explains
how users adopt and utilize technology based on perceived usefulness and ease of use (Davis,
1989). While TAM has been widely applied in educational technology research, it primarily
focuses on technology adoption rather than learning outcomes. This limitation suggests the
need to integrate cognitive and learning-based perspectives to better understand how
technology contributes to academic performance (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Granić &
Marangunić, 2019). In this regard, Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) theory provides a relevant
framework for explaining how students actively manage their learning processes. SRL
emphasizes students’ ability to plan, monitor, and evaluate their learning, which has been
consistently linked to academic success (Broadbent & Poon, 2015; Zimmerman, 2002)).
Recent studies further suggest that AI technologies can enhance SRL by providing real-time
feedback and adaptive learning support (Azevedo et al., 2017; Viberg et al., 2018).
In addition to cognitive factors, motivational and technological competencies also
play a crucial role in AI-supported learning. Motivation significantly influences learning
outcomes in AI-supported environments. For example, self-determination theory highlights
that intrinsic motivation, supported by teacher guidance, enhances students' competence and
autonomy when using AI tools (Chiu et al., 2024; Lu, 2026; Zhen, 2025). Similarly, flow
experiences mediated by psychological needs like autonomy and relatedness sustain
engagement with AI systems (Lu, 2026). Meanwhile, digital literacy is often considered a key
determinant of success in digital learning environments. However, recent evidence indicates
that digital literacy may not directly improve academic performance but instead operates
indirectly through learning processes (Thelma et al., 2024; Van Laar et al., 2019). These
findings highlight an important gap in the literature, namely the lack of an integrative model
that simultaneously explains the roles of motivation, technology use, and self-regulated
learning in shaping academic performance.
Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by proposing and empirically testing a
structural model that examines the role of artificial intelligence in influencing academic
performance through self-regulated learning, while also incorporating learning motivation
and digital literacy as key antecedents. Specifically, this study investigates how AI usage
contributes to academic outcomes both directly and indirectly, and how motivational and
cognitive factors interact within this process. In doing so, this study extends TAM by linking
technology adoption to learning outcomes, advances SRL theory by positioning AI as a
cognitive scaffold, and clarifies the indirect role of digital literacy in academic performance.
Based on this theoretical framework, several hypotheses are proposed. Learning
motivation is expected to positively influence artificial intelligence usage (H1), while artificial
intelligence usage is hypothesized to positively affect both academic performance (H2) and
self-regulated learning (H3). Furthermore, self-regulated learning is expected to have a
positive effect on academic performance (H4) and to mediate the relationship between
artificial intelligence usage and academic performance (H5). In addition, digital literacy is
hypothesized to positively influence self-regulated learning (H6) and to have a direct effect
on academic performance (H7). Through this integrative framework, this study seeks to
provide a more comprehensive understanding of how artificial intelligence contributes to
academic performance in higher education.

Method
1. Research Design
This study employed a quantitative approach using a cross-sectional survey design to
investigate the relationships between artificial intelligence usage, learning motivation, self-
regulated learning, digital literacy, and students’ academic performance. The study was
designed to examine both direct and indirect effects within a structural model framework.
2. Participants and Data Collection
The study involved 547 undergraduate students from Institut Agama Islam Al-Jihad
Shalahuddin Al-Ayyubi Jakarta, Indonesia. A proportionate random sampling technique was
applied to ensure balanced representation across faculties and academic levels. Data were
collected using an online questionnaire distributed via Google Forms. All respondents had
prior experience using artificial intelligence tools, such as ChatGPT, to support their
academic activities. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to
data collection.
3. Measurement Instruments
The instrument consisted of 36 items distributed across five constructs, developed
based on established theoretical frameworks and adapted to the context of AI-assisted
learning. All items were measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly
disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The distribution of measurement items across constructs is
presented in Table 1.

4. Data Analysis Technique
The data were analyzed using covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-
SEM) to examine the relationships among artificial intelligence usage, learning motivation,
self-regulated learning, digital literacy, and academic performance. The analysis was
conducted using JASP (Version 0.96), which utilizes the lavaan package for structural
equation modeling, with maximum likelihood (ML) estimation.
Prior to the main analysis, data screening procedures were performed to ensure data
quality and suitability. This included examining missing values, detecting potential outliers,
and assessing the normality of the data distribution. Descriptive statistics and correlation
analyses were also conducted to provide an initial overview of the relationships among
variables.
Given that the data were collected using a single-source, self-reported survey, the
potential for common method bias (CMB) was assessed. Harman’s single-factor test was first
conducted as an initial diagnostic, and the results indicated that no single factor accounted
78

for the majority of the variance. However, this technique has been widely criticized for its
limited sensitivity in detecting method bias. Therefore, several procedural remedies were
implemented during data collection, including ensuring respondent anonymity, reducing
evaluation apprehension, and carefully designing questionnaire items to minimize ambiguity
and social desirability bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Nevertheless, more advanced statistical
techniques, such as marker variable analysis or the inclusion of a common latent factor in
confirmatory factor analysis, were not applied. As such, the possibility of residual common
method bias cannot be entirely ruled out.
A two-step analytical approach was employed. First, confirmatory factor analysis
(CFA) was conducted to evaluate the measurement model, including the assessment of factor
loadings, construct reliability, and convergent validity. Reliability was examined using
Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability, while convergent validity was assessed through
average variance extracted (AVE). Second, the structural model was evaluated to test the
hypothesized relationships among constructs.
Model fit was assessed using multiple goodness-of-fit indices, including the chi-square
statistic (χ²), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square
Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).
The significance and strength of path coefficients were examined to determine the direct
effects among variables. In addition, mediation effects were tested using bootstrapping
procedures with 5,000 resamples to estimate indirect effects and their corresponding
confidence intervals, providing a robust assessment of the mediating role of self-regulated
learning.
5. Ethical Considerations
All participants were informed about the purpose of the study and provided consent
prior to participation. The data were collected anonymously and used solely for academic
purposes to ensure confidentiality and ethical compliance.

Discussion
1. The Role of Artificial Intelligence in Enhancing Academic Performance
The findings of this study demonstrate that artificial intelligence (AI) plays a
significant role in shaping students’ academic performance, both directly and indirectly. The
results show that AI usage positively influences academic performance, indicating that the
integration of AI into learning activities enhances students’ access to information, efficiency
in completing tasks, and overall learning effectiveness. This finding is consistent with the
Technology Acceptance Model, which suggests that perceived usefulness and ease of use
drive positive outcomes (Davis, 1989). More recent studies also confirm that AI-supported
learning environments can improve student engagement and academic achievement through
adaptive and personalized learning systems (Holmes et al., 2019; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019).
Beyond its direct effect, AI also significantly influences students’ learning processes,
particularly self-regulated learning. AI technologies provide real-time feedback, intelligent
recommendations, and adaptive scaffolding that enable students to regulate their learning
more effectively. This aligns with research suggesting that digital and AI-based tools can
foster deeper learning engagement and metacognitive awareness (Badawi & Adiyono, 2026;
Bond et al., 2021; Nurjanah et al., 2025; Rizvi et al., 2025). In this sense, AI functions not
merely as a technological tool but as a facilitator of learning processes.
Furthermore, the dual role of AI as both a performance enhancer and a process
facilitator highlights its transformative potential in higher education. Rather than serving
solely as a productivity tool, AI reshapes how students interact with knowledge and manage
their learning. In summary, AI's value lies not only in improving measurable outcomes but
also in enhancing the processes that lead to those outcomes, such as personalized learning,
real-time feedback, and skill development. This dual focus underscores AI's transformative
potential in education (Badawi & Adiyono, 2026; Hubballi et al., 2025; Kushwah et al., 2025;
Ntjamba & Ashipala, 2025).

2. Self-Regulated Learning as a Key Mechanism
One of the most important findings of this study is that self-regulated learning (SRL)
emerges as the strongest predictor of academic performance. This result reinforces the central
premise of Self-Regulated Learning theory, which emphasizes that students’ ability to plan,
monitor, and evaluate their learning is essential for academic success (Zimmerman, 2002).
Prior studies consistently show that students with strong self-regulation skills achieve better
academic outcomes across various learning environments (Broadbent & Poon, 2015).
In addition, AI usage was found to significantly enhance self-regulated learning,
suggesting that AI can act as a cognitive scaffold that supports metacognitive development.
AI systems can guide students in setting goals, tracking progress, and reflecting on their
learning, thereby strengthening self-regulatory behaviors. This finding is supported by
previous research indicating that technology-enhanced learning environments can promote
SRL by providing structured support and feedback (Azevedo et al., 2017).
The mediation analysis further confirms that SRL plays a critical role in linking AI
usage to academic performance. This suggests that the effectiveness of AI depends on how
students use it within their learning processes. In other words, AI serves as a tool, but SRL
functions as the mechanism that transforms technological use into meaningful academic
outcomes. This mechanism-based perspective contributes to the literature by integrating
technological and cognitive dimensions into a unified explanatory model.
3. The Conditional Role of Digital Literacy and Learning Motivation
The non-significant direct effect of digital literacy on academic performance requires
critical interpretation, as it challenges the dominant assumption that digital competence
inherently leads to improved learning outcomes. This finding suggests that possessing digital
skills does not automatically translate into academic success, particularly in AI-supported
learning environments.
One plausible explanation lies in the distinction between digital skills and learning
strategies. Digital literacy primarily reflects students’ ability to access, navigate, and evaluate
digital information; however, it does not guarantee that these capabilities are applied in
cognitively meaningful ways. Without strong self-regulated learning, digital competence may
remain procedural rather than strategic, limiting its direct contribution to academic
performance (Nassar & Khattab, 2024; Van Laar et al., 2019; Victor & Ujir, 2025).
Moreover, the result highlights a potential gap between surface-level engagement and
deeper cognitive processing. Students with high digital literacy may efficiently interact with
digital platforms and retrieve information, yet this efficiency does not necessarily foster
critical thinking, conceptual understanding, or knowledge integration. In this context, digital
literacy facilitates access to information but does not ensure meaningful learning (Kirschner
& De Bruyckere, 2017; OECD, 2021).
Another important consideration is the increasing reliance on artificial intelligence
tools in academic tasks. While AI can enhance efficiency, excessive dependence on such
technologies may reduce students’ cognitive effort and active engagement. As a result, digital
proficiency may shift from being a learning enabler to a shortcut mechanism, weakening its
direct impact on academic performance. This phenomenon aligns with emerging concerns
that overreliance on intelligent systems may hinder deep learning and metacognitive
development (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023).
Overall, this finding calls for a reconsideration of the linear assumption that digital
competence directly leads to academic success. Instead, it supports a more nuanced
perspective in which digital literacy contributes to academic performance only when mediated
by self-regulated learning, highlighting the central role of cognitive and metacognitive

processes in AI-supported education.
4. Theoretical Contribution
This study advances a mechanism-based reconceptualization of how artificial
intelligence (AI) contributes to academic performance, challenging the prevailing tendency in
the literature to treat technology adoption as an end rather than a process. Existing research
grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model has largely emphasized perceived usefulness
and ease of use while offering limited explanation of how technology translates into
measurable learning outcomes. By introducing self-regulated learning (SRL) as a mediating
mechanism, this study addresses this conceptual limitation. It repositions AI from a passive
technological artifact to an active component within a cognitively mediated learning system.
This shift is critical, as it redirects the analytical focus from adoption metrics toward learning
mechanisms, which remain underdeveloped in prior empirical models.
Moreover, this study provides a substantive extension to Self-Regulated Learning by
situating it within an AI-supported learning environment. While SRL has been widely
recognized as a predictor of academic success, its role as a mechanistic bridge between
technology use and performance outcomes has not been sufficiently theorized. The findings
demonstrate that AI enhances academic performance only to the extent that it strengthens
students’ metacognitive regulation, underscoring that technological effectiveness is
contingent upon learners’ cognitive engagement rather than inherent in the technology itself.
This challenges deterministic assumptions about AI in education and reinforces the need to
embed technological innovation within established learning theories.
Finally, the study offers a critical re-evaluation of digital literacy by demonstrating its
non-significant direct effect on academic performance, a finding that problematizes the
dominant narrative that digital competence is inherently beneficial. Instead, the results
suggest that digital literacy operates indirectly through SRL, implying a hierarchical
dependency between technological skills and cognitive processes. This insight exposes a
theoretical oversimplification in prior studies that conflate access and competence with
learning effectiveness. By integrating motivational, technological, and cognitive dimensions
into a unified model, this study not only bridges fragmented theoretical perspectives but also
establishes a more analytically rigorous framework for understanding academic performance
in AI-mediated educational contexts.
5. Limitations and Future Research
This study is subject to several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use
of a cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences, as the observed
relationships reflect associations rather than temporal dynamics. Second, the reliance on self-
reported data raises concerns regarding common method bias and potential response
inflation, particularly in measuring constructs such as academic performance and AI usage.
Third, the sample was drawn from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of
the findings across different educational contexts and cultural settings. In addition, the study
does not differentiate between types or intensity of AI usage, thereby overlooking potential
variations in how specific AI tools influence learning processes. These limitations suggest
that future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs, incorporate
objective performance indicators, and examine diverse institutional contexts as well as more
nuanced dimensions of AI engagement.
Another limitation concerns the potential presence of common method bias due to
the use of self-reported data collected from a single source. Although Harman’s single-factor
test suggested that common method variance is unlikely to be a serious concern, this
85

technique is considered insufficient as a standalone diagnostic tool. Future research is
therefore encouraged to employ more robust statistical approaches, such as marker variable
techniques or common latent factor modeling, to better control for and assess common
method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003).
Conclusion
This study demonstrates that artificial intelligence (AI) contributes to students’
academic performance through both direct and indirect pathways, with self-regulated learning
(SRL) emerging as the central mechanism linking technology use to learning outcomes. The
findings indicate that while AI can enhance efficiency and access to learning resources, its
effectiveness is not inherent but depends on students’ ability to actively regulate their learning
processes. In this regard, SRL functions as the primary driver that transforms AI usage into
meaningful academic gains.
Furthermore, the results highlight that learning motivation plays a crucial role in
encouraging students to engage with AI technologies, whereas digital literacy does not directly
improve academic performance but operates indirectly through SRL. This suggests that
technological competence alone is insufficient to enhance learning outcomes without the
support of strong cognitive and metacognitive skills. Therefore, the integration of AI in
education should not be approached solely as a technological intervention but as part of a
broader pedagogical strategy that fosters students’ self-regulation and motivation.
Overall, this study underscores the importance of adopting a holistic approach to AI
integration in higher education by aligning technological tools with students’ learning
processes. Future research is encouraged to further investigate the dynamic interplay between
AI, cognitive engagement, and contextual factors across diverse educational settings to
develop more robust and generalizable models of AI-supported learning.
