As Generative AI becomes increasingly used in various educational contexts, understanding how
students  engage  with  these  tools  during  collaborative  problem-solving  is  critical.  While  prior
research suggests that critical thinking is essential in human-AI problem-solving, few studies have
examined how instructional interventions, targeting critical thinking, might shape their reliance
behaviors  and  collaborative  outcomes.  This  study  investigates  the  effects  of  a  critical  thinking
intervention embedded in a problem-based learning (PBL) environment where students are engaged
with Generative AI. The intervention combined strategies that foster critical thinking, including
authentic instruction, structured dialogue, and AI-supported peer mentoring, aiming to promote
students' thoughtful  engagement and improve  problem-solving  performance.  Participants  (N =
226) were assigned to experimental (with critical thinking interventions) or comparison (without
critical thinking interventions) conditions. We used pre- and post-surveys to measure participants'
trust, critical thinking, and AI reliance behaviors, and group reports and chat histories to assess their
problem-solving  quality  and  creativity.  Results  revealed  that  the  intervention  did  not  produce
significant improvement in self-reported critical thinking, possibly due to the short intervention
duration. However, the intervention led to a marginal reduction in students' thoughtless use of
Generative AI and significantly reduced the direct adoption of AI-generated content. Notably, stu-
dents in the intervention condition produced more creative solutions, demonstrating higher levels of
originality and idea density in their group reports. These findings suggest that how students use
Generative AI is critical, especially when it is almost impossible to control whether they use it or not.
The study  highlights  the importance  of  designing interventions  that  cultivate  students’  critical
thinking to support creative human-AI problem-solving.

1. Introduction

Critical thinking is  essential for high-quality  learning in higher  education (Anggraeni  et  al., 2023; Benvenuti et  al., 2023). In
problem-based learning (PBL), where students navigate complex and ill-structured problems (O'Neil et al., 2003), critical thinking
enables them to  articulate reasoning, weigh  alternatives, construct  well-justified  arguments, and  justify  solutions  (Butterworth  &
Thwaites, 2013). As Generative AI is being integrated into problem-solving, tools such as ChatGPT offer new opportunities to support
creativity, reasoning, and knowledge construction (Boussioux et al., 2024; Joksimovic et al., 2023). These tools can assist students in
generating novel ideas, elaborating on complex issues, and organizing solutions. Rather than being viewed as a passive tool, Generative
AI can function as a situational partner that co-constructs solutions with learners (Chiou & Lee, 2023).

However, the outcomes of human-AI collaboration largely depend on how students engage with AI (Pham &  Sampson, 2022).
Previous studies (e.g., Benvenuti et al., 2023; Zhai et al., 2024) suggest that critical thinking could improve human-Generative AI
collaborative outcomes. Critically evaluating AI-generated content, including questioning assumptions, revising prompts, and inte-
grating evidence (Hou et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2024), might lead to more desirable outcomes such as higher-quality solutions and
greater  creativity.  On  the  other  hand,  passive  overreliance  on  AI-generated  content  might  compromise  collaborative  outcomes
(Klingbeil et al., 2024). Thus, the importance of critical thinking becomes even more pronounced in human-Generative AI collabo-
rative problem-solving.

Building on the rationale, the present study designs and implements a critical thinking intervention embedded in a PBL context with
Generative  AI.  Research  suggests  that critical  thinking  can  be  most  effectively  cultivated  through  a  combination of  authentic  in-
struction, structured dialogue, and mentoring (Abrami et al., 2015), which informs the design and implementation of the critical
thinking intervention embedded in a PBL framework involving Generative AI of the current study. Specifically, we examine how the
intervention affects students’ reliance behaviors when working with AI and how it shapes human-Generative AI collaborative out-
comes, including problem-solving quality and creativity.

2. Literature review

2.1. Critical thinking and problem-solving

Educational research identifies critical thinking as a vital skill for problem-solving (Anggraeni et al., 2023). The Delphi Report
defines it as purposeful judgment that supports interpretation, analysis, evaluation, and inference, grounded in evidential, conceptual,
methodological, and contextual considerations (Facione, 1990).

The importance of critical thinking is further heightened with the integration of Generative AI into problem-solving processes.
Gajos and Mamykina (2022) found that participants experienced learning gains when interacting only with AI explanations, in the
absence of AI decisions. Conversely, in scenarios where both AI decisions and explanations were presented after people made their
initial decisions and people could then revise their decisions, no learning gains were observed. These findings indicate that learning
occurs when individuals make decisions by critically and independently examining available information and explanations, which
highlights the crucial role of critical thinking in effective human-AI collaboration. Similarly, a study found that Generative AI can
support high school students in scientific problem-solving (Tong et al., 2025). However, when students utilize Generative AI, they
often use it to obtain answers rather than engaging in more collaborative exploration. This lack of critical thinking may undermine
their deeper understanding of scientific concepts (Lai, 2011). Therefore, insights from both studies (i.e., Gajos & Mamykina, 2022; Lai,
2011) suggest that employing critical thinking during human-AI collaboration is essential for effective collaborative outcomes.

2.2. Collaborative outcomes in human-AI problem-solving

Evaluating the educational value of human–Generative AI collaboration requires close attention to its collaborative outcomes.
Creativity,  which  reflects  learners’  ability  to  produce  original  ideas  through  human-AI  problem-solving,  and  quality  of  problem-
solving, which reflects their capacities to produce coherent and feasible solutions, complement each other to indicate collaborative
outcomes in PBL.

Creativity, defined as the ability to generate innovative responses to complex, ill-structured problems (Jonassen, 2010; Sowden
et  al.,  2019),  is  one  of  the  desired  learning  outcomes  of  problem-solving  and  an  important  21st-century  skill  (Geisinger,  2016).
Emerging evidence suggests that Generative AI can aid students' creativity during problem-solving. For instance, Urban et al. (2024)
found that students collaborating with ChatGPT produced more original responses and reported higher creative self-efficacy, which
means that they were more confident in producing innovative ideas.

Creativity  is  often  associated  with  producing  responses  that  are  divergent,  unique,  and  original.  Prior  studies  have  evaluated
creativity through human labeling of artifacts based on dimensions including fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration (Chang
et al., 2022), as well as dimensions such as elaboration, originality, and quality (Urban et al., 2024). Recently, research has explored
automatic analysis of creativity in writing through linguistic analysis and text mining. For instance, Zedelius and colleagues (2019)
studied creative story writing using Coh-Metric to assess text cohesion and readability, which significantly correlate with human
judgments of creativity. Likewise, semantic distance measured through Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA) can be used to evaluate novelty
and essay quality by comparing texts to a training set (Latifi & Gierl, 2021).

Quality  of  problem-solving,  on  the  other  hand,  emphasizes  the  cognitive  strategies  used  to  produce  a  well-rounded  solution

including clarity, feasibility, and alignment with contextual demands (Puente-Díaz et al., 2021). High-quality problem-solving has
been linked to cognitive processes such as problem analysis, stakeholder perspective integration, solution justification, and feasibility
evaluation (Kwon, Song, Sari, & Khikmatillaeva, 2019; Puente-Díaz et al., 2021), and to collaboration processes such as negotiation of
ideas and sharing of resources (Zhang et al., 2022).

While creativity and problem-solving quality are conceptually distinct, they are also interdependent. A solution must be both novel
and useful to be considered truly creative (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). For example, when assessing the creativity of students’  design
solutions, beside originality of ideas, Chang et al. (2022) included fluency (referring to producing alternative solutions to a problem),
flexibility (i.e., showing multiple possibilities), elaboration (i.e., providing details and supportive evidence of the ideas), and use-
fulness (i.e., the feasibility and functionality of ideas). Thus, creativity includes novelty and ideational richness, while problem-solving
quality is associated with analytical rigor and contextual relevance. Together, these constructs capture both the originality and the
practicality of problem-solving artifacts. Creativity and quality of problem-solving could together offer a comprehensive picture of
collaborative outcomes in human-AI problem-solving.

2.3. Reliance on Generative AI

Reliance, in the human-AI problem-solving context, refers to the behavioral patterns of how humans interact with AI systems
(Bansal et al., 2021; Bertuccelli & Cummings, 2011; Wang et al., 2008). A conceptualization of reliance focuses on the outcomes of
reliance patterns: overreliance, where humans accept AI suggestions even when they are incorrect, and underreliance, where humans
reject AI advice when it is correct (Passi & Vorvoreanu, 2022). However, this conceptualization ties reliance behaviors with binary
outcomes (correct or incorrect), which might not be applicable in the problem-solving context where outcomes such as creativity or
problem-solving quality are not binary (Fok & Weld, 2024). In ill-structured problem-solving, simplistic outcome-based definitions of
reliance may obscure the cognitive processes that underpin thoughtful AI engagement (Hou et al., 2025).

To address this limitation, Hou et al. (2025) developed a self-report instrument that captures reliance behaviors independent of task
outcomes. This instrument identifies four distinct patterns of Generative AI use: reflective, cautious, collaborative, and thoughtless.
Reflective use involves critical evaluation and iterative refinement of AI outputs, such as revising prompts or questioning AI sug-
gestions.  Cautious  use  reflects  active  skepticism,  where  users  detect  flaws  and  selectively  engage  with  AI-generated  content.
Thoughtless use indicates passive adoption of AI outputs without substantial scrutiny. Collaborative use captures the integration of AI
contributions into group discussions and joint refinement processes. This instrument allows researchers to investigate reliance be-
haviors in complex problem-solving settings.

A consistent finding across studies is that higher levels of trust are associated with greater reliance on AI systems (Hoff & Bashir,
2015; Klingbeil et al., 2024). Trust is commonly defined as the belief that an agent will assist in achieving personal goals in situations
marked by uncertainty and vulnerability (Lee & See, 2004). Excessive trust can lead users to accept AI recommendations even when
they are contradicted by other evidence (Klingbeil et al., 2024). Conversely, insufficient trust may cause users to dismiss accurate AI
suggestions, resulting in under-reliance and diminished performance in human-AI collaboration (Hoff & Bashir, 2015). Thus, trust is an
important  predictor  of  reliance  in  human-AI  collaboration,  but  it  is  not  its  determinant.  In  addition,  people  tend  to  engage  in  a
cognitive evaluation process prior to accepting or rejecting AI suggestions. Vasconcelos et al. (2023) demonstrated that individuals
strategically change their reliance on AI based  on a  cost-benefit evaluation  of cognitive effort and  task demands.  By varying  the
complexity of AI explanations and reward structures, they showed that participants were more likely to engage critically with AI when
the cognitive costs were manageable and the perceived utility was high.

These findings highlight that reliance is not fixed but shaped by cognitive and individual factors (Hou et al., 2025), suggesting that
learners can be guided to engage with AI more thoughtfully. This underscores the need for targeted interventions that cultivate critical
thinking, enabling students to manage their reliance on AI in ways that enhance problem-solving and learning outcomes.

2.4. Interventions to foster critical thinking in problem-solving

A review (Abrami et al., 2015) demonstrated that combining different strategies showed the most substantial and sustained im-
provements in students’ critical thinking. Specifically, the three effective instructional strategies identified in the review are: authentic
instruction, dialogue, and mentoring (Abrami et al., 2015).

Authentic  instruction,  which  situates  learning  within  real-world,  ill-structured  problems,  has  been  identified  as  a  particularly
effective  strategy  for  promoting  critical  thinking  (Abrami  et  al.,  2015).  Meta-analysis  also  demonstrated  that  collaborative
problem-solving tasks involving authentic, poorly structured real-world problems yielded a substantial overall improvement in stu-
dents' critical thinking (Xu et al., 2023). For example, Yang and Chou (2008) examined college students collaborating on web-based
bulletin boards to debate and reflect on controversial real-world issues, such as environmental protection and social justice, leading to
measurable  improvements  in  their  critical  thinking  skills.  These  findings  imply  the  importance  of  designing  authentic,  sustained
problem-solving experiences to cultivate students’ critical thinking. When Generative AI is involved, without real-world problems that
demand critical analysis and evaluation, students may default to passively accepting AI-generated outputs, leading to thoughtless
reliance behaviors.

In addition, dialogue has been identified as a critical mechanism for fostering students' critical thinking skills. Abrami et al. (2015)
emphasized that learning through dialogue, including teacher-led questioning, small group discussions, or Socratic exchanges, en-
courages students to clarify ideas, challenge assumptions, and reason through multiple perspectives, leading to significant gains in
critical  thinking.  Role-play  activities,  by  design,  create  structured  opportunities  for  this  type  of  dialogue.  As  Latif  et  al.  (2018)

Last but not least, mentoring that offers personalized feedback and scaffolds learners’  development can foster critical thinking

chatbot engages learners through structured, thought-provoking questions designed to stimulate reflection, challenge assumptions,
and encourage exploration of multiple perspectives. Thus, Generative AI chatbots designed to implement Socratic questioning prin-
ciples may serve as an innovative form of mentoring, offering personalized support that mirrors the essential elements of human
mentoring.

2.5. The current study

In  summary,  there  is  growing  attention  to  critical  thinking  when  integrating  Generative  AI  in  educational  settings  to  ensure
productive human-Generative AI collaboration. However, few empirical studies have tested whether a critical thinking intervention
can intentionally shape students’ reliance behaviors with AI in authentic classroom problem solving. Addressing this gap, we designed
a critical thinking intervention in the PBL context with three strategies informed by previous research (e.g., Abrami et al., 2015; Liu &
P´asztor, 2022), including authentic instruction, dialogues, and mentoring. The intervention includes a clear focus on critical thinking
and its relationship to Generative AI use (authentic instruction), a role-playing element for interdisciplinary communication (dia-
logue), and a GPT-based peer tutor to offer constructive feedback (mentoring). In contrast, the comparison condition allows for free use
of Generative AI for problem-solving.

We  investigated  the  intervention  effect  across  two  levels.  First,  on  the  individual  level,  we  examined  whether  the  designed
intervention could improve students' perceived critical thinking, and how the intervention influences students ' reliance patterns on AI.
Considering that the participants in the current study had diverse disciplinary backgrounds, and previous studies have shown that trust
is an important predictor of reliance behaviors, we included disciplinary backgrounds and trust in Generative AI as control variables.
Second,  on  the  group level,  we  investigated  how  the  intervention  influences  the  quality  and  creativity  of  students’  collaborative
problem-solving outcomes. By systematically exploring these relationships, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of how to
foster thoughtful engagement with AI technologies. Specifically, the research questions are:

RQ1  Does the intervention improve students' perceived critical thinking during human-AI problem-solving, considering individual

differences in trust and disciplinary background?

RQ2  How does the intervention impact individual students' reliance behaviors (i.e., reflective use, collaborative use, thoughtless use,

and cautious use) on Generative AI, considering the effect of trust and disciplinary background?

RQ3  On the group level, how do human-AI collaborative outcomes, including problem-solving qualities, adoption of Generative AI's

outputs, and creativity, correlate with each other?

3. Methods and materials

3.1. Participants

The participants of this study were 226 undergraduate students. They were first- and second-year students (enrolled in the uni-
versity in 2023 or 2024) enrolled in a digital literacy course in the August semester of 2024 at an Asian university. The participants
came from 14 different classes, with around 20 to 50 students in each class. In each class, 5 to 6 students from different schools were
purposefully assigned to a group that would work on various problem-solving activities. We chose one problem-solving activity on
drafting a policy proposal that allowed students to use Generative AI extensively (Please see Appendix A for the complete problem-
solving activity used in this study). We assigned the 14 classes to the experiment and comparison conditions, and the students in
the same class were in the same condition. In the experiment condition, students received support from three strategies to promote
critical thinking (Please see Intervention Design for Critical Thinking for details). The comparison group completed the same task but
used Generative AI without these supports. The demographic information of the 226 individual participants in the comparison and
intervention conditions is presented in Table 1. We have 112 participants in the comparison condition and 114 participants in the
intervention condition. The distributions of age, gender, academic year, ethnicity, and disciplinary backgrounds are roughly balanced
across the two conditions. For disciplinary backgrounds, STEM disciplines include engineering, computer science, physics, mathe-
matics, and medical programs. Non-STEM includes social sciences, humanities, and business programs. Considering that STEM stu-
dents usually have higher AI literacy than non-STEM students (Kong et al., 2021), we included disciplinary background as a control
variable for the individual-level analysis.

3.2. Critical thinking intervention design

The study involves a critical thinking intervention to promote human-AI collaborative outcomes. The intervention was imple-
mented in 14 classes in a 2-hour in-class session. The study followed a between-subjects experimental design, with classes assigned to
either the experiment condition or the comparison condition. The goal of the intervention was to improve students' critical thinking
and promote more thoughtful reliance on Generative AI during collaborative problem-solving (Klingbeil et al., 2024). Informed by
Abrami  et  al.’s  meta-analysis  (2015),  the  intervention  incorporates  three  core  strategies:  authentic  instruction,  AI-mediated  peer
tutoring, and role-play. In contrast, the comparison condition followed the same problem-solving structure but allowed students to use
Generative AI freely without any additional scaffolding.

First,  for authentic  instruction, students  were guided to identify potential  biases in AI-generated content before  beginning the
problem-solving task. This critical thinking exercise explicitly highlighted the importance of critical thinking when using Generative
AI. Students were tasked with creating prompts that may expose AI biases and analyzing the Generative AI outputs to identify the
biases. In addition, as outlined in the instructional design, the activity centered on real-life, complex scenarios relevant to students’
experiences. This approach is grounded in prior empirical studies showing that authentic problems are effective in fostering critical
thinking (Abrami et al., 2015).

Second, we designed a GPT-based peer tutor to support problem solving (see Fig. 1). Mentoring, such as teachers' feedback and
tutors' facilitation, fosters critical thinking in problem-solving (Abrami et al., 2015). Since instructors may find it challenging to track
all groups' progress and provide timely and personalized mentoring during class, we developed the GPT-based peer tutor to help
students with this problem-solving task. This customized chatbot, using the GPT model, interacted with students by posing reflective
questions, identifying logical gaps, and suggesting counterarguments (Open AI, 2025). As presented in Fig. 1, specifically, the chatbot
was customized to 1) identify students' problem-solving stages to provide adaptive feedback (e.g, “I understand that you are currently
working on Step 3 – Present and evaluate the solution, because you are asking for feedback on what you've proposed”, see Fig. 1a); 2)
promote critical thinking by asking Socratic questions (e.g., “What interests of which stakeholders might be compromised in your
solution?”, “What are some possible causes or contributing factors to the issue?”, see Fig. 1b); 3) monitor the problem-solving progress
(e.g., “You've outlined clear steps for fairness … To strengthen your evaluation, could there be any unintended consequences?”, see
Fig. 1c). This method draws on the effectiveness of AI-mediated Socratic tutoring in fostering critical thinking among students (Fakour
& Imani, 2025).

Third, we implemented a role-play component to foster dialogue among students in the problem-solving activity. Group members
were assigned as different stakeholders, including administrators, instructors, and students, and then they were required to adopt their
assigned stakeholder perspectives to evaluate ethical and pedagogical issues in real AI misuse cases. Role-play is recognized as a
productive strategy to promote critical thinking, engaging students cognitively and affectively to resolve issues (Rashid &  Qaisar,
2017).

with stakeholders’ needs. They assessed the strengths and weaknesses of their solutions and reflected on potential conflicts of interest.

solutions addressed the initial problem, considered consequences, and discussed how to measure effectiveness with success indicators.

engage with Generative AI critically, while the comparison group could use Generative AI freely. Following the session, all participants
completed a reflection survey that captured their critical thinking and reliance behaviors. They also submitted a group report sum-
marizing their problem-solving solutions, their chat histories with Generative AI, and reflections.

information (i.e., age, gender, school year, major and ethinicity) and trust level towards Generative AI. We adopted the Human-
Computer Trust Scale (Gulati et al., 2019), which evaluates the degree of trust users place in automation, to assess students' trust
in Generative AI for real-world problems and complex tasks. The scale comprises 12 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 =
strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Example items include “I feel I must be cautious when using Generative AI in problem-solving”
and “I think Generative AI is competent and effective in helping me solve real-world problems.” The scale has been validated with good
reliability (Cronbach's alpha = .84).

After the in-class activity, participants were instructed to complete the Reliance Behaviors Scale and Critical Thinking Skills. The
Reliance Behaviors Scale was created and validated by the research team (Hou et al., 2025) in a problem-solving environment with
similar participants. This scale assesses how frequently different usage behaviors of Generative AI are adopted, utilizing a 5-point
Likert Scale (1 = Almost never, 5 = Almost always). It comprises 16 questions divided equally into four subcategories: reflective
use (e.g., “I read Generative AI's output critically when the output was generated”), cautious use (e.g., “I found Generative AI's output is
not perfect”), thoughtless use (e.g., “I copied the Generative AI's output as part of our solution”), and collaborative use (e.g., “My peer
(s) helped me modify Generative AI's output”). According to the authors' earlier study validating this scale (2025), it demonstrates
good reliability and validity (Cronbach's alpha = .84).

The Critical Thinking Skills scale was adopted from the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ; Pintrich et al.,
1993). Individual MSLQ subscales have been utilized to assess various aspects of the learning experience (Cred´e & Phillips, 2011). The
Critical Thinking Skills subscale specifically evaluates how well students use their knowledge in problem-solving or conduct critical
assessments to develop practical and comprehensive solutions for given tasks. It employs a 7-point Likert Scale (1 = not true of me at
all; 7 = very true of me) and comprises 5 items, including statements like “I often find myself questioning things I hear or read in this
activity to decide if I find them convincing” and “I try to play around with ideas of my own related to what I am learning in this course.”
According to the original research (Pintrich et al., 1993), this scale shows good reliability and validity (Cronbach's alpha = .80).

4.2. Group-level data

For group-level data, we collected participants' group reports, which included their proposed solutions and reflections on their use
of Generative AI during problem-solving, as well as chat histories with Generative AI. The data at the group level requires all the group
members’ consent to be included in the study. We collected 38 problem-solving reports and reflections, and 29 of them also provided
their chat histories with Generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT, Bard, Perplexity).

Problem-solving Reports. The problem-solving reports include groups’ proposed solutions to address the cases of using Generative

4.3. Data analyses

To answer the research questions, we performed a series of data analyses (Please see Fig. 3 for an overview of the analyses). We
performed analysis at two levels: individual-level analysis using individual surveys and group-level analysis, based on group reports
that included chat histories, problem-solving reports, and reflections. We separated these two levels of analysis because of the con-
ceptual  distinction  between  individual-level  constructs  (e.g.,  trust,  reliance  behaviors)  and  group-level  outcomes  (e.g.,  creativity,
solution quality). Aggregating individual data to the group level or disaggregating group-level data to individuals might compromise
validity if within-group variability is not properly accounted for (Bliese, 2000).

First, to investigate whether the intervention has changed students perceived critical thinking, we conducted a regression analysis
controlling for the effect of trust and disciplinary backgrounds (RQ1). Second, to examine the effect of interventions on individual
reliance behaviors with trust and disciplinary backgrounds as control variables, we conducted a series of regression analyses (RQ2).
Third, for group-level analyses that investigate human-AI collaborative outcomes (RQ3 & RQ4), we conducted several preparatory
analyses. For the quality of problem-solving, we conducted a content analysis. Then, to assess the creativity of ideas in students'
problem-solving reports, we conducted two text-mining techniques (i.e., semantic similarity and idea density metrics; Bower et al.,
2023) and human rating of novelty. We also computed the similarity between students' reports and their chat histories with Generative
AI as an indicator of their adoption of Generative AI's output. After these preparatory analyses, we conducted correlation analyses
among these indicators of collaborative outcomes (RQ3). Further, to compare collaborative performances between the conditions, we
conducted a series of t-tests for each collaboration indicator from the adoption of Generative AI's output, the quality of problem-solving
reports, and from creativity (RQ4). The reflection was used to supplement the quantitative analyses on the comparison of group-level
collaborative outcomes.

4.4. Quality of problem-solving reports

We first  conducted  a  content analysis  of  students’  problem-solving  reports  to  evaluate  their  problem-solving performance.  As
presented in Appendix B, the coding scheme was developed in alignment with the learning objectives of the interdisciplinary un-
dergraduate course designed to foster communication across disciplines, and with the core goals of PBL, which emphasize critical
analysis, higher-order thinking, and evaluative reasoning (Hmelo-Silver, 2004; Vidergor & Krupnik-Gottlieb, 2015).

Effective problem-solving begins with a clear and comprehensive understanding of the problem. In the problem description stage,
Problem Definition assesses students' ability to identify and articulate the core issue in the case study. According to Jonassen (2000),
problems  in  everyday  and  professional  contexts  are  inherently  situated  within  complex  environments,  which  require  students  to
distinguish relevant from irrelevant information and to construct a meaningful problem space. Additionally, Impact Analysis assesses
students' ability to analyze how the identified problem impacts learning experiences and course outcomes. Assessing the broader
impact  of  a  problem  is  essential  for  prioritizing  actions  and  anticipating  consequences  (Hmelo-Silver,  2004).  Perspective-Taking
measures students' recognition and integration of different stakeholders' concerns. In PBL, it is necessary to gather knowledge from
different disciplines to enhance the depth and quality of solution, during which students also develop skills such as communication and
negotiation (Hmelo-Silver, 2004).

In the second stage, problem exploration, students start to draft solutions to address the problem. After students identify different
perspectives in the first stage, it is important to align proposed solutions with stakeholders' needs to ensure relevance and feasibility.
Solution Alignment assesses the extent to which students' solutions address the concerns of all relevant parties. Evaluation examines
students' ability to assess their solutions' strengths, weaknesses, and potential improvements. Studies have highlighted that reflective
evaluation  enhances problem-solving skills by  promoting deeper understanding  (Noroozi et al., 2016). Conflict Analysis evaluates
students' recognition of potential disagreements among the stakeholders they represent. Students need to be aware that their solutions
would require compromises from stakeholders, and they need to effectively identify these conflicts, which is an important aspect of
collaboration skills (Chinn & Malhotra, 2002). By navigating conflicts, students can enhance the robustness of their solutions.

In the third stage, solution presentation, the students need to present the solutions and evaluate the feasibility. Solution Justification
assesses students' ability to support their proposed solutions with logical reasoning and evidence. Justifying solutions is linked to
critical thinking and the ability to communicate complex ideas effectively (Vidergor and Krupnik-Gottlieb, 2015). Refinement measures
students' ability to revise their solutions in response to identified weaknesses in the second stage. It is expected that students would
refine their solution, which could enhance their learning and problem-solving capabilities (Sato, 2022). Feasibility evaluates students'
consideration of implementation challenges and resource constraints. Assessing the practicality of solutions ensures their viability in a
real-world context. Measurable Success examines students' ability to establish clear criteria for assessing solution effectiveness and
impact.

Using the consolidated coding scheme, two raters independently evaluated the 38 reports collected from student groups. The inter-
rater reliabilities between the two-raters are as follows: .63 for problem definition, .62 for impact analysis, .88 for perspective taking,
.51  for  solution  alignment,  .96  for  evaluation,  .80  for  conflict  analysis,  .68  for  solution  justification,  .48  for  refinement,  .80  for
feasibility,  and  .76  for  measurable  success.  The  inter-rater  reliabilities  for  most  dimensions  have  reached  substantial  to  perfect
agreement (Chaturvedi & Shweta, 2015; Landis & Koch, 1977). Although some dimensions (i.e., refinement and solution alignment)
yielded lower inter-rater reliability coefficients, all discrepancies were reconciled through discussion between the two raters, adhering

closely to the coding scheme to ensure consistency and rigor in the final coding.

4.5. Creativity of problem-solving reports

We  employed  both  computational  methods  and  human  ratings  to  evaluate  the  creativity  of  problem-solving  reports.  The
computational methods assessed semantic originality and idea density (Bower et al., 2023). Human raters evaluated creativity in terms
of idea novelty and writing novelty.

Computational Indicators of Creativity. The computational methods include two approaches (i.e., semantic originality and idea
density metrics) with three indicators (i.e., Originality, CPIDR and DEPID) that are commonly used to evaluate creativity in written
text (Bower et al., 2023).

Originality.  Semantic  originality  was  assessed  using  centroid-based  cosine  similarity,  wherein  each  problem-solving  report
embedding was compared to the centroid of all submissions. We implemented this calculation using the sentence-transformers Python
package, which allows for efficient generation of contextualized sentence embeddings (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). Then, this method
quantifies the uniqueness of a group report's content relative to the collective dataset (all the group reports in our case), with lower
similarity scores indicating higher originality. We computed originality by subtracting the cosine similarity score of each proposal from
one, such that higher values indicate greater semantic divergence from the central tendency of all proposals, and thus the higher values
indicate higher originality. Such semantic measures have been instrumental in capturing the novelty aspect of creativity (Bower et al.,
2023).

CPIDR. We analyzed idea density using two computational tools available in the Python package ideadensity (Robison, 2024): the
Computerized  Propositional  Idea  Density  Rater  (CPIDR)  and  the  Dependency-based  Propositional  Idea  Density  (DEPID)  method.
CPIDR calculates the ratio of propositions to total words, where a proposition refers to an expressed idea or meaning-bearing unit, such
as a subject–verb–object clause (e.g., “students use AI”), a predicate (e.g., “should be avoided”), or a logical connector indicating
contrast or causality (e.g., “however,” “because”). This indicator can effectively gauge the informational richness of given text (Brown
et al., 2008).

DEPID. DEPID leverages syntactic dependency parsing to identify unique propositions. For instance, in the sentence “Students
should cite AI tools,”  distinct propositions might include “students cite tools”  and “tools are AI.”  In a more complex sentence like
“Although AI can assist with research, students must critically evaluate its output,” DEPID may identify several distinct propositions
such as “AI assists research,” “students evaluate output,” and “evaluation must be critical.” DEPID captures the underlying semantic
structure of ideas, offering a nuanced perspective on the complexity and depth of ideas presented (Sirts et al., 2017). High idea density
scores from these tools suggest a sophisticated articulation of concepts, which is indicative of creative problem-solving (Brown et al.,
2008; Sirts et al., 2017).

Human Rating of Novelty. We also conducted a human rating procedure to assess reports' creativity. Two human raters inde-
pendently reviewed all reports and first summarized the key ideas proposed in each submission using short keywords (e.g., alternative
assessment, AI workshops, penalty system). Drawing on prior work on scoring novelty and originality (e.g., Mozaffari, 2013; Plucker
et al., 2004), the raters evaluated creativity along two dimensions that capture both the content and expression of students’ ideas (see
Table 2 for rubrics). First, two human raters independently assessed idea novelty. Each idea was rated on a 1–2 scale, where 1 indicated
that the idea was common across many reports and 2 indicated that the idea was novel within the context of this dataset. Because
reports varied in the number of ideas presented, we calculated an average idea novelty score for each report to avoid conflating
creativity with simply producing more ideas. Second, writing novelty was also rated on a 1–2 scale, with 1 representing general or
shallow  explanations,  and 2  reflecting elaborated  reasoning  that demonstrated  unique or  original  explanations.  After  coding  and
discussion, the raters reconciled disagreements. We did not compute inter-rater reliability because coders worked collaboratively to
reach a consensus score for each report, and the sample size was small. We combined average idea novelty with writing novelty into a
single novelty score to capture both the originality of the ideas and the distinctiveness of how they were articulated. This integrated
measure offers a more balanced and meaningful representation of creativity than either component alone:

Novelty = (Sum of Idea Novelty / Number of Ideas) + Writing Novelty

4.6. Adoption of Generative AI's outputs

We assessed  the similarity between  29 group reports  and the associated  chat histories with Generative AI to understand how
students incorporated AI-generated content into their final outputs. We computed the cosine similarity between each group's final
report and its AI chat transcripts. A higher similarity score suggests closer alignment with the AI-generated outputs, while a lower score
implies greater independence. This method provides an objective indicator of students' reliance on Generative AI in collaborative
problem-solving contexts.

4.7. Statistical analyses

Following these analyses, we first conducted regressions to explore whether the intervention improved students' perceived critical
thinking (RQ1). Then, we conducted more regression analyses to explore the effect of intervention on critical thinking and reliance
behaviors, with trust and disciplinary backgrounds as control variables (RQ2). We conducted a t-test to compare participants' trust in
Generative AI and found that the trust level in the comparison group is significantly higher than that in the experiment group (t(225) =
2.61, p < .05). In addition to trust, the participants have unbalanced disciplinary backgrounds, with more students from STEM dis-
ciplines than students from non-STEM disciplines. Thus, trust and disciplinary background were included in the analysis as control
variables. Further, we conducted correlation analysis among the group-level indicators, including the adoption of Generative AI's
outputs  as  an  objective  indicator  of  AI  reliance,  quality  of  problem-solving,  and  creativity  (RQ3).  Lastly,  to  explore  the  effect  of
intervention  on  collaboration  outcomes,  we  conducted  a  series  of  t-tests  on  indicators  associated  with  collaboration  outcomes,
including 10 indicators for quality of problem-solving, 5 indicators for creativity, and the adoption rate of Generative AI (RQ4).

Contextualizing  Statistical  Analysis.  To  complement  the  statistical  findings  of  group-level  analysis  and  deepen  our  under-
standing of human–AI collaboration, we conducted a qualitative analysis of the 38 group reflections submitted alongside the problem-
solving reports. Specifically, these reflections captured students’ descriptions of how they used Generative AI during the activity and
their perceptions of its helpfulness or limitations. This qualitative data deepens our understanding of how Generative AI has been used,
as well as whether people have carefully evaluated the output. By complementing these qualitative insights with quantitative results,
we aimed to contextualize the observed outcomes and gain a deeper understanding of the underlying collaborative dynamics between
students and Generative AI.

dimension (t(31) = 2.49, p < .05), suggesting that the comparison group provides more elaborate evaluation during problem-solving.

maintain human autonomy and independence, compared to students who used Generative AI freely.

This study investigated how a classroom-based intervention, designed to promote critical thinking, influenced students’ reliance
behaviors, quality of problem-solving, and creativity during human-AI collaboration. Drawing on prior literature highlighting effective
strategies for promoting critical thinking (e.g., Abrami et al., 2015; Liu & P´asztor, 2022), the intervention integrated three strategies:
authentic  instruction,  reflective  dialogue,  and  mentoring.  Specifically,  students  were  prompted  to  identify  potential  biases  in  AI
outputs (authentic instruction), were supported by a GPT-based peer tutor that encouraged reflection and deep inquiry (mentoring)
and were instructed to engage in role-play to evaluate solutions from multiple stakeholder perspectives (dialogues). These strategies
were embedded within a problem-solving activity, where students collaborated to define problems, explore information, and propose
solutions with the support of Generative AI.

We first examined whether the intervention designs improved students' critical thinking and found no significant effect on their
self-reported outcomes. A possible reason is that critical thinking is less likely to change through short-term interventions. A meta-
analysis of interventions on critical thinking in higher education reveals that short-term interventions (one to four weeks) have no
effect on critical thinking (Liu &  P´asztor, 2022). The current study only implemented the intervention for 2 hours in one session.
Another explanation concerns the intensity and implementation of the intervention. Although brief, the intervention was cognitively
demanding. Students were required to engage in authentic instruction, structured dialogue, and AI-mediated mentoring within a short
timeframe. Thus, students might have engaged unevenly with the available critical thinking scaffolds, for instance, some participating
more actively in group dialogues, others interacting more frequently with the GPT-based peer tutor. These variations in engagement
could have led to different levels of critical thinking in the experiment condition and therefore weaken the overall observed inter-
vention effect (Horner et al., 2006). Third, the non-significant result may stem from limitations in the measurement approach. The
current study relied on a self-report measure adapted from the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ; Pintrich et al.,
1993). Because problem-based learning inherently fosters critical thinking (Anggraeni et al., 2023), students in both conditions may
have perceived themselves as engaging in critical thinking regardless of the intervention conditions. Consequently, the subtle dif-
ferences in critical thinking between conditions might not be fully captured through a self-reported survey, but may be better observed
in students’ behaviors, such as reflective or thoughtless use of AI. It is therefore plausible that students acted more reflectively without
registering an increase in perceived critical thinking.

At the individual level, the intervention was associated with a marginal reduction in thoughtless use of Generative AI. The finding
suggests  that  at  the  individual  level,  participants  themselves  perceived  less  thoughtless  use  of  Generative  AI,  including  passive
adoption of AI-generated content. Such a self-perceived difference in the thoughtless use of Generative AI is also observed through a
group-level analysis of students' group reports and chat histories. We found that the experiment groups have less direct adoption of
Generative AI-generated content. These results suggest that intervention strategies can shift students' reliance patterns away from
passive adoption. The results highlight the potential of instructional strategies to change reliance behaviors on Generative AI, aligning
with the studies that emphasize careful pedagogical designs when integrating Generative AI in the classroom (Moussa, 2024; Zhai
et al., 2024). Additionally, we found that trust in AI is a positive predictor for both reflective and thoughtless use of Generative AI,
which aligns with our previous finding (Hou et al., 2025). In that earlier study, we observed that trust can lead to increased overall
usage of Generative AI, but the type of usage (i.e., reflective or thoughtless), is related to students’ critical thinking. Therefore, it is
important to promote critical thinking to channel trust toward constructive rather than thoughtless use of Generative AI.

At the group level, this study investigated the effect of intervention on collaborative outcomes, including problem-solving quality

and  creativity.  The  correlations  between  group-level  variables  show  that  direct  adoption  of  Generative  AI's  content  is  positively
associated with some aspects of problem-solving quality, including Impact Analysis, Refinement, and Solution Formation. Also, groups
in the comparison condition, which demonstrated more thoughtless use of Generative AI, scored higher on certain aspects of problem-
solving quality, particularly Evaluation. These results suggest that Generative AI use may support analytical clarity and coherence. In
students' reflections, some also stated that “Generative AI provided us with better flow, comprehension, description of our solutions.”
The findings reflect previous studies which show that AI-based chatbots can improve the organization, sufficiency, and elaboration of
students' arguments in debates (Guo et al., 2023). One study found that students engaged in more knowledge-based dialogue and
elaborated discussions with chatbots, whereas they engaged in more social dialogues in peer interactions (Song et al., 2025). Engaging
more in knowledge-based dialogues with Generative AI might explain why the comparison group can achieve better in elaboration
during problem-solving. In contrast, the experiment groups, which were instructed to engage more with their peers to maintain human
autonomy, might allocate more time to social regulation but less time for elaborating and analyzing their solutions to problem-solving.
Another explanation is, as Zhai et al. (2024) observed, Generative AI exhibits resilience against cognitive intensity, enabling it to
perform  consistently  across  tasks.  Human  learners,  in  contrast,  may  face  cognitive  overload  when  simultaneously  engaging  in
reflection, integration, and articulation during problem-solving.

Aside from the quality of problem-solving, originality is another critical factor of human-AI problem-solving. The moderate cor-
relation between the human novelty score and the semantic distance indicates that both approaches capture some shared aspects of
creativity. Human ratings emphasize contextual judgment and the perceived uniqueness of ideas, whereas computational methods
quantify semantic distance at scale. Both human ratings and semantic distance could measure closeness among concepts and ideas.
Such relatedness between ideas could be apparent  to human  judgment and can also be assessed  through computational methods
(Runco et al., 2025). The weaker associations between idea density measures and human novelty scores indicate that computational
metrics can also assess dimensions of creativity, such as information richness, that may be difficult for humans to capture. Compu-
tational  methods  can evaluate  a  large amount  of  information  to quantify  the  idea  richness  (Brown  et  al.,  2008). Together,  these
findings suggest that computational metrics can complement human assessments and potentially provide a practical alternative for
evaluating creativity in large-scale text analyses.

Groups in the intervention condition, who had lower thoughtless use of Generative AI, demonstrated significantly higher levels of
creativity as measured by originality and idea density. Adoption of Generative AI's content is also negatively associated with origi-
nality. These findings contribute to the ongoing research around the role of Generative AI in creativity. While some studies report that
AI assistance may inhibit creative outputs (Balt`a Salvador, El Madafri, Braso Vives, & Pe˜na Carrera, 2024), others highlight its po-
tential to generate novel or unconventional ideas. For instance, Fu et al. (2024) demonstrated that GPT-produced visual designs that
are more divergent and surprising, although not necessarily more useful. Similarly, Urban et al. (2024) found that students who used
GPT generated more creative problem-solving solutions than those who did not, as measured by higher elaboration (amount of detail),
originality (novelty of ideas), and quality (alignment with task goals). While the current study also observed that Generative AI use
improved the quality of problem-solving, we found that originality was lower among groups that relied more heavily on Generative AI.
This discrepancy may stem from the difference in measurement and experiment setups. Urban and colleagues (2024) relied on expert
ratings to evaluate creativity in a manufacturing product improvement task, whereas our study employed computational measures
such as idea density and semantic uniqueness in policy proposals. Another explanation is that students in our experiment groups were
encouraged to interact critically with AI, discouraging passive acceptance of AI outputs and leading to higher originality. These findings
build  upon  Medeiros  et  al.  (2025),  who  cautioned  that  low-quality  or  misaligned  AI  assistance  can  hinder  human  creativity  in
problem-solving.

Therefore, our findings suggest that the benefits of AI on creativity are conditional, depending on how people use Generative AI. In
our  context,  the  problem-solving  task  did  not  explicitly  require  students  to  generate  innovative  solutions.  Yet,  even  within  such
boundaries, students who engaged more critically and independently produced more creative outputs in terms of originality and idea
diversity. This may be attributed to the relatively predictable nature of GPT responses compared to the associative leaps enabled by
human cognition (Beaty &  Kenett, 2023). When students are prompted to contextualize and revise AI output, rather than adopt it
uncritically, they may contribute more distinct and meaningful ideas. Thus, while general Generative AI use can enhance elaboration
and clarity, it is thoughtful use that appears to foster creativity.

7. Implications

Our findings have important implications for ongoing research and educational practice concerning human-Generative AI col-
laborations. For research, our study is among the first to intervene in students' reliance behaviors through instructional designs. Our
findings contribute to the ongoing research on the effect of Generative AI on learning outcomes, such as problem-solving qualities and
creativity. Our findings suggest that Generative AI may be highly capable of producing detailed and goal-aligned responses, thus
elevating  quality  indicators  (Urban  et  al.,  2024).  However,  human  inputs  remain  crucial  for  generating  novel  and  contextually
grounded ideas. Thus, effortful engagement with Generative AI is vital to unlocking creative potential in human-AI problem-solving.
The finding also reinforces Bearman, Ryan, and Ajjawi’s (2023) notion of “altering authority,” where AI's expanding role in intellectual
tasks reshapes the purpose and value of student contributions. In this view, the emphasis is on how people engage with Generative AI,
instead of whether it should be used.

For practitioners, the study affirms the importance of designing interventions that scaffold critical thinking when using Generative
AI. The evidence suggests that even short interventions can reduce passive overreliance and stimulate creative outputs. To translate
such interventions into classroom practice, instructors can guide students to engage critically by questioning, refining, and contex-
tualizing AI-generated ideas. At the same time, learning activities should frame AI as a collaborative partner in open-ended problems
that invite exploration and judgment, where students contribute insights that go beyond what AI can offer. Assessment should also
evolve to value how students reason with and about AI, emphasizing justification, integration of perspectives, and creative synthesis
rather than the accuracy or polish of AI-assisted outputs. At the institutional level, the findings advocate for professional development
initiatives that equip educators to integrate AI thoughtfully and ethically in their teaching and enable students with time and space to
think critically and creatively in their learning tasks, even when Generative AI is pervasive.

8. Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the intervention was implemented during a single 2-h session, which may have been
insufficient  to  produce  measurable  changes  in  students’  self-reported  critical  thinking.  Future  studies  should  therefore  consider
repeated sessions or embedding the strategies across an entire course to examine how critical thinking skills can be cultivated and
reinforced in sustained ways. Longitudinal interventions are more likely to yield measurable changes in critical thinking. Additionally,
tracking changes over time could provide insight into whether and how reliance patterns evolve over time, and how students adapt
their strategies to use AI through repeated exposure. Furthermore, assessing critical thinking through self-report alone may not capture
the behavioral and process-oriented aspects of how students reason with AI. Future studies should incorporate process-tracing methods
such as log data or think-aloud to more comprehensively capture critical thinking. This would yield richer insight into how students
evaluate and regulate AI contributions during problem-solving.

Second, while the problem-solving task required students to develop policy solutions, it did not explicitly prompt them to generate
creative or novel ideas. Nevertheless, we observed that passive reliance on Generative AI was associated with more predictable and less
original  responses,  suggesting  that  thoughtless  use  may  suppress  creativity  when  not  explicitly  required.  Future  research  should
incorporate problem tasks that explicitly require creative ideation or divergent thinking, such as open-ended design challenges or
innovation-focused  scenarios.  This  design  would  help  further  uncover  how  reliance  behaviors  interact  with  creativity  in  human-
Generative AI collaborative learning.

In addition, the current study relied solely on group reports to assess collaborative outcomes. We apply automated text-mining
techniques to approximate creativity and human labeling of problem-solving quality. Although such measures offer scalability and
objectivity, they may not fully capture the contextual and domain-sensitive aspects of problem-solving. For creativity, triangulating
computational indicators with rubric-based or expert ratings in future research would provide a more robust and nuanced evaluation
of creativity outcomes. While assessing problem-solving quality with a coding scheme is common, it provides only one perspective on
collaborative  outcomes.  Future  studies  should  incorporate  multimodal  evidence,  such  as  process  data,  recorded  discussions,  or
observational measures, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of problem-solving quality.

Finally, the sample for group-level analysis is relatively small, with only 38 groups in total, and the individual participants con-
sisted of relatively homogeneous university students from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to
more diverse educational or cultural settings. The relatively small sample size also limits our ability to do multilevel data analysis on
students’  individual factors and collaborative performance. In addition, because the intervention combined three strategies simul-
taneously, we were not able to isolate the differential contributions of authentic instruction, structured dialogue, and mentoring.
Although this integrated design was grounded in prior meta-analytic evidence showing that combined approaches yield the strongest
effects (Abrami et al., 2015), future studies with larger and more diverse samples could vary these strategies or track their use over time
to  identify  which  elements  are  most  influential  so  as  to  improve  the  practical  utility  of  research  results.  Future  studies  should
implement the intervention across diverse educational contexts, including different disciplines, institutions, and cultural settings, with
a larger sample size to explore multilevel effects and examine whether the AI reliance typology generalizes beyond this setting.

9. Conclusion

This study advances our understanding of how instructional interventions on critical thinking, including authentic instruction,
GPT-based peer tutors, and role-playing, can shape students' reliance behaviors and collaborative outcomes during human-AI problem-
solving. Although the short intervention did not significantly enhance critical thinking, it effectively reduced thoughtless reliance and
direct adoption of AI-generated content. Notably, the intervention fostered greater creativity in students' solutions, underscoring the
value of promoting effortful engagement with Generative AI. These findings highlight that how students use AI, rather than whether
they use it, is critical to supporting thoughtful learning and creative outcomes in PBL environments. Future research should further
explore  scalable,  longer  interventions  targeting  students’  critical  thinking  across  diverse  contexts  and  learning  tasks  to  cultivate
sustained critical engagement with AI and examine its impacts.
