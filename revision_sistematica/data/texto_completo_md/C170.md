This study investigates the role of ChatGPT in supporting university students' mastery of English verb tenses.
Over  two  semesters,  66  students  at  a  technical  university  were  assigned  to  one  of  three  instructional  modes:
independent use of ChatGPT (at home), ChatGPT use with teacher guidance (in-class), or traditional instruction
without AI (in-class). Pre- and post-tests measured grammar learning gains, while transcripts of student-ChatGPT
interactions were analyzed to assess engagement levels. A post-study questionnaire captured students' percep-
tions  of  ChatGPT's  usefulness  and  motivational  impact.  Findings  show  that  students  who  used  ChatGPT  with
teacher  guidance  achieved  the  highest  average  learning  gains,  while  those  who  used  the  tool  independently
showed  more  variable  results.  Higher  engagement  levels  with  ChatGPT  strongly  correlated  with  greater
improvement, emphasizing that the tool's effectiveness depends on how actively it is used. Students generally
found  ChatGPT  enjoyable  and  appreciated  its  immediate  feedback,  though  some  reported  challenges  with
inconsistent  responses.  This  study  contributes  to  the  field  by  providing  empirical  evidence  on  how  different
modes  of  ChatGPT integration influence grammar  learning outcomes.  It highlights the  importance of  instruc-
tional design and learner engagement when incorporating AI tools, suggesting that ChatGPT is most effective
when used as part of a guided, interactive learning environment.

1. Introduction

Mastering  verb  tenses  is  crucial  for  establishing  the  temporal
framework  of actions and states in language. Tenses  create a chrono-
logical sequence and enable clear communication of events and actions
in  relation  to  time.  They  illustrate  the  connections  between  different
actions and conditions, making their understanding essential to having a
good  command  of  the  language  (Allard  &  Mizoguchi,  2021;  Fuchs  &
Werner, 2018). However, significant differences often exist between the
tense  systems  of  various  languages,  posing  challenges  for  students
attempting  to  master  them.  Routine  practice  of  grammatical  aspects,
such as exercises requiring the filling in of correct verb forms, has not
produced highly effective results. In these exercises, students frequently
lack thorough explanations of why a given variant is correct, and what
the differences are when using other verb forms, or they find that iso-
lated  sentences  do  not  offer  the  necessary  context  for  choosing  the
appropriate option (Bielak & Pawlak, 2013, Lin et al., 2020).

Over  the  years,  teachers  have  developed  a  variety  of  innovative
methods to practice this grammar in classrooms, for example, the recent

successful  introduction  of  gamification  (Laura-De  la  Cruz,  2023).
However, students often lack the resources to practice this material on
their own effectively. In parallel, recent advances in machine learning
(ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) are reshaping educational practices
across  disciplines,  including  language  education.  Given  the  growing
potential of AI in education, we decided to explore how new AI-based
tools  can  be  used  for  these  purposes.  Tenses  are  particularly
well-suited for AI-mediated learning due to their structured, rule-based
nature and the need for immediate corrective feedback. These charac-
teristics  make  them  ideal  for  tools  like  ChatGPT,  which  can  explain,
correct, and elaborate on grammar points in real time.

Research  indicates  that  integrating  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  into
curriculum design and teaching methods offers substantial opportunities
for innovation in language learning (Wang, Wang, et al., 2024). While
ChatGPT's capabilities are promising, its effectiveness likely depends not
only on its design but also on the nature of student engagement (Heung
& Chiu, 2025; Xu et al., 2024; Zhan & Yan, 2025). Learners may differ
substantially in how they prompt the tool, interpret its responses, and
integrate  the  information  provided.  Understanding  the  relationship

To address these gaps, this study explores the impact of ChatGPT-
supported  instruction  on  university  students’  mastery  of  English  ten-

three instructional conditions: (1) ChatGPT use at home (independent,

and (3) traditional teacher-led instruction with no AI use. The study is
also  replicated  across  two  semesters  to  enhance  the  robustness  of
findings.

In addition to pre- and post-test comparisons, the study incorporates
student questionnaires to gauge learner perceptions of ChatGPT and a
qualitative  coding  framework  to  analyze  engagement  behaviors.  This
triangulated,  mixed-method  approach  provides  a  comprehensive
perspective  on  both  learning  outcomes  and  learner  experience.
Together, these elements aim to offer a multidimensional view of how
ChatGPT functions as a pedagogical tool in grammar education.

Research questions:

RQ2: How do students perceive the usefulness of ChatGPT in helping

1.1. Theoretical framework

This  study  is  grounded  in  established  theories  of  second  language
acquisition  (SLA),  educational  psychology,  and  technology-enhanced
learning.  Each  research  question  is  anchored  in  a  distinct  theoretical
perspective that provides the conceptual lens through which the data are
interpreted.

The first research question (RQ1) is informed by Sociocultural The-
ory (Vygotsky, 1978) and by principles of Computer-Assisted Language
Learning (CALL), particularly the role of learner autonomy described by
Benson  (2011).  From  a  sociocultural  perspective,  learning  is  most
effective  within  the  learner's  Zone  of  Proximal  Development  (ZPD),
supported by guidance from a more knowledgeable other. In this study,
students  using  ChatGPT  with  teacher  guidance  receive  pedagogical
scaffolding that helps them interpret and apply AI-generated feedback,
while  those  working  independently  must  rely  on  their  own  meta-
cognitive and self-regulatory strategies.

At the same time, ChatGPT itself can be understood as a mediational
tool that reshapes the ZPD. While it lacks the intentionality of a teacher,
it  provides  dialogic  scaffolding  through  explanations,  reformulations,
and responsive feedback. Here, support is learner-driven: the quality of
mediation  depends  on  how  students  prompt  the  system,  evaluate  its
output,  and  regulate  their  engagement.  This  expands  the  ZPD  into  a
socio-technological space where human and artificial mediators jointly
shape learning, foregrounding both the potential of AI to extend guid-
ance and the importance of learner agency in appropriating it.

The  second  research  question  (RQ2)  draws  on  the  Noticing  Hy-
pothesis (Schmidt, 1990), the concept of feedback literacy (Carless &
Boud,  2018),  and  theories  of  learner  autonomy  (Reinders  &  White,
2011).  The  Noticing  Hypothesis  suggests  that  conscious  attention  to
language  forms  is  necessary  for  acquisition.  ChatGPT  supports  this
process by providing immediate explanations and corrective feedback.
However, because the quality of its responses can be inconsistent, stu-
dents need to critically evaluate and selectively apply the feedback they

2

receive.  This  aligns  with  the  notion  of  feedback  literacy,  which  em-
phasizes  the  learner's  ability  to  make  productive  use  of  feedback.
Consequently, students' perceptions of ChatGPT's usefulness are closely
tied  to  their  capacity  for  autonomous  and  strategic  engagement  with
AI-generated responses.

The third research question (RQ3) is framed by Interactionist Second
Language Acquisition Theory (Long, 1996; Swain, 1995), the Noticing
Hypothesis,  and  theories  of  autonomy  in  computer-assisted  learning
environments  (Benson,  2011).  Interactionist  theory  emphasizes  the
importance  of  dialogic  engagement,  negotiation  of  meaning,  and
modified output in facilitating language learning. These processes are
reflected  in  student-ChatGPT  interactions,  particularly  when  learners
ask follow-up questions, challenge the tool's responses, or adapt expla-
nations to their own needs. Such engagement not only promotes deeper
noticing  of  grammatical  forms  but  also  fosters  autonomous  learning
behaviors. In this way, student engagement becomes a critical factor in
linking AI-supported interaction with tangible learning outcomes.

The following review of the literature expands on the practical and
empirical  research  underpinning  these  theoretical  foundations,  with
particular attention to prior studies on ChatGPT, grammar instruction,
and learner engagement.

2. Literature review

After  its  release  to  the  public  in  November  2022,  ChatGPT  (Chat
Generative  Pre-Trained  Transformer)  quickly  became  the  subject  of
numerous studies, and its significance across various fields continues to
grow. In terms of its use in education, various advantages and limita-
tions have been identified over the past two years. Its main limitations
have been identified as hallucination, originality, toxicity, privacy, and
sustainability (Hua et al., 2024). Related to this lies another of the main
problems associated with the use of ChatGPT in education, is the issue of
the quality of responses and bias in AI (Ali et al., 2024).

Regarding  the  issue  of  bias,  political  biases  are  often  highlighted
(Feng  et  al.,  2023),  which  is  not  significant  in  the  case  of  language
practice. In the context of language learning, it appears more problem-
atic that LLM models have been taught on a large variety of copyrighted
materials, with memorization rates associated with the frequency with
which passages from these books appear on the Internet (Chang et al.,
2023).  Thus,  mainstream  language  trends  are  promoted.  This  implies
that AI tools may ‘correct’  different language variations  and consider
them as errors, potentially undermining linguistic diversity. D'Agostino
(2023) warns  that  excessive  use  of  AI  in  training  writing  can  have  a
negative impact on language diversity and simultaneously discriminate
against students from certain social backgrounds.

Seo et al. (2021) state that one of the main concerns students have
when  using  AI  for  studying  is  that,  due  to  the  inconsistent  quality  of
responses, a student might learn an incorrect answer and subsequently
fail a test because of it. The problem of variable quality of responses, and
hallucination (a term used when ChatGPT generates semantically and
grammatically  correct  responses  that  contain  factual  errors)  can  be
handled with proper activity design. The need to constantly check the
information  provided  by  ChatGPT  should  not  be  considered  a  disad-
vantage. If both parties are familiar with this, critical thinking can be
practiced when the activity is designed correctly, turning a disadvantage
into a strength (Abramson, 2023). However, it is important to remember
when incorporating ChatGPT into instruction that ChatGPT works best
to practice phenomena with which students are already familiar. This
claim is supported by Meishar-Tal (2024) who argues that a high level of
critical thinking from both students and educators is necessary to ach-
ieve good results in training writing skills.

As mentioned above, the thoughtful design of the activity is key to its
successful  inclusion  in  the  lesson.  However,  in  practice  we  often  see
ChatGPT incorporated into lessons without prior thought. Studies show
that new technologies are only beneficial if they are implemented in a
manner consistent with sound pedagogy (Fütterer, Fischer, Alekseeva,

When it comes to problems related to the use of ChatGPT in teaching
from the student's perspective, some studies have highlighted concerns
about the potential impact of AI on the value of higher education. Issues
related  to  accuracy,  transparency,  privacy  protection,  and  ethics  are
prominent in discussions about AI integration in learning environments
(Chan &  Hu, 2023). Moreover, there are doubts about student auton-
omy; Akgun and Greenhow (2022) warn against jeopardizing autonomy
by allowing life to be guided by predictive systems. On the other hand,
AI tools like ChatGPT have been shown to have a positive impact on
certain  aspects  of  student  motivation  (Du  &  Alm,  2024).  They  offer
personalized learning experiences and immediate feedback, which can
enhance engagement and facilitate learning. However, the use of AI in
education  also  brings  challenges,  particularly  regarding  the  mainte-
nance of social bonds and the risk of excessive reliance on AI tools.

Despite  these  challenges,  AI  has  the  potential  to  revolutionize
educational  assessments  by  enhancing  our  understanding  of  learners,
not only what they know but also how they learn, and which pedagogies
work  best  for  them.  For  example,  Su  and  Yang  (2023) show  that the
main  advantage  of  using  ChatGPT  is  the  possibility  of  a  personalized
learning experience for students and taking the administrative tasks of
teachers off the table.

Many studies address the challenges or benefits of using LLM or AI in
general in teaching (Tahiru, 2021; Kohnke et al., 2023; Labadze et al.,
2023). However, at the moment, there is a lack of materials with ac-
tivities for teachers to use, and if so, with the main focus on the use of
ChatGPT  for  training  writing  skills  in  general  (Imran  &  Almusharraf,
2023; Ghafouri et al., 2024; Fokides & Peristeraki, 2024; Liu, Zhang, &
Biebricher,  2024)  or  specifically  academic  writing  (Mahapatra,  2024;
Shi et al., 2025; Song & Song, 2023), where the main advantage of using
AI  lies  in  its  ability  to  provide  students  with  formative  feedback.
Computer-mediated  feedback  can  successfully  replace  the  teacher,
especially in cases of large numbers of students in class, when individual
feedback from the teacher is not possible due to time constraints.

Additionally, blended learning models have also been explored for
facilitating  grammar acquisition. In  one Chinese university  context, a
combination  of  online  grammar  exercises  and  face-to-face  instruction
led to notable gains in students’  grammar proficiency as well as high
learner  satisfaction,  highlighting  the  benefit  of  integrating  digital  re-
sources  with  traditional  teaching  (Wang  et  al.,  2024).  This  positive
outcome indicates that blending technology-driven practice with class-
room  interaction  can  effectively  support  grammar  learning  in  higher
education settings.

even without a human instructor's direct input, intelligent systems can
facilitate learners' awareness of grammatical mistakes and contribute to
better writing performance.

Beyond  technology-mediated  approaches,  Murtisari  et  al.  (2020)
highlight the benefits of peer tutoring for grammar instruction in higher
education EFL contexts, noting that this approach can clarify complex
grammatical concepts and enhance learners’ accuracy. However, their
study also identifies persistent challenges facing peer grammar tutors,
including  difficulties  in  maintaining  learner  motivation,  managing
mixed  proficiency  levels  among  tutees,  and  the  risk  of  ‘cognitive
dependence’  in  less  proficient  learners.  To  address  these  issues,  the
authors recommend integrating grammar-focused peer tutoring into a
broader  communicative  language  teaching  framework  as  part  of  an
eclectic approach (Murtisari et al., 2020).

Complementing these findings, assessment-driven approaches have
likewise been examined as a complement to grammar instruction. For
instance,  Hadeli  et  al.  (2023) reported  that  students  who  underwent
regular  formative  tests  alongside  communicative  grammar  lessons
showed  significantly  greater  improvements  in  their  English  oral
response ability compared to those without such formative assessment.
This  finding  underscores  the  value  of  integrating  frequent  formative
testing  with  interactive  grammar  teaching  to  reinforce  learning  and
build learners’ confidence in using new structures.

Collectively,  these  studies  highlight  the  benefits  of  interactive,
feedback-rich, and technology-enhanced approaches in grammar peda-
gogy.  It  is  within  this  evolving  context  that  generative  AI  tools  like
ChatGPT are now being considered for language instruction. However,
empirical research has yet to fully examine ChatGPT's specific impact on
the  mastery  of  complex  English  grammatical  features  (such  as  tense
usage) at the university level, a gap which the present study seeks to
address.

3. Methodology

We divided our study into two parts. All participants were students
from  a  technical  university  who  had  successfully  passed  an  English
proficiency examination at the B1-B2 level according to the CEFR, which
served as the entry requirement for enrollment in the course and ensured
a comparable baseline of language knowledge across the sample. Within
each semester, students were randomly assigned at the individual level
to  one  of  three  instructional  conditions  (independent  ChatGPT  use,
ChatGPT  with  teacher  guidance,  or  traditional  instruction)  using  a
random number generator in Excel.

In  the  first  part,  we  worked  with  30  first-year  students  who  had
selected English grammar as an elective subject to enhance their profi-
ciency. These students were randomly assigned to three groups (1a/1,
1b/1, 2/1). Over a four-week period (19 February-15 March 2024), we
focused  on  improving  their  understanding  of  English  tenses.  To
strengthen the reliability of the findings given the limited sample size,
the study design was replicated with 36 students (groups 1a/2, 1b/2,
and 2/2) in the following semester (23 September-18 October 2024),
using identical procedures and materials.

In the first semester, the groups consisted of 10 students, and in the
second semester, 12. A group size of 10–12 students is regarded as the
standard enrollment for a language course. For this reason, it was not
possible to increase the number of students per group, as doing so would
no longer align with the established standard. In a teacher-led setting,
for instance, a larger group size could reduce the speaking opportunities
available to each student and limit the amount of individual feedback,

Although all participating students provided informed consent at the
beginning of the study, we emphasized that their participation remained
entirely voluntary throughout the research process. Ensuring participant
comfort and upholding the principle of voluntary participation were our
highest  priorities.  Therefore,  students  were  reminded  that  they  could
withdraw  from  the  study  at  any  point  without  any  negative  conse-
quences. We believe that maintaining this option was essential to pre-
serving the integrity of voluntary participation.

As a result, six students chose not to complete the study during the
first semester due to the demanding nature of their coursework, and two
students  withdrew  in  the  second  semester.  Although  all  students  re-
ported  these  general  reasons  for  withdrawal,  the  highest  number  of
dropouts occurred in the group that used only chat-based interaction at
home. This may have been caused by students realizing, over time, that
this system did not suit them, even though they did not explicitly report
this as a reason. The final sample included 24 students in the first se-
mester and 34 in the second semester.

combination  of  grammar  assessments,  ChatGPT  conversation  tran-

instructional groups using normalized gain scores. RQ2 was addressed
through thematic analysis of questionnaire responses on students' per-
ceptions  of  ChatGPT.  RQ3  was  explored  through  engagement-level
coding of ChatGPT transcripts and its correlation with grammar gains.

3.4. Groups description

3.4.1. Use of ChatGPT in groups 1a/1 and 1a/2

Students in groups 1a/1 and 1a/2 worked with ChatGPT as a self-
study  tool  for learning  English tenses.  To  ensure  equal  conditions for
all participants, only the free version of ChatGPT, 3.5 model was used
during the experiment. The goal of this design was to explore whether
ChatGPT  could  effectively  take  on  the  role  of  a  virtual  “teacher,”
providing explanations, feedback, and practice opportunities indepen-
dently of direct instructor involvement.

At the beginning of the intervention, students received an English
text  with  underlined  verb  forms  and  were  instructed  to  analyze  and
review the tenses used with the help of ChatGPT. To guide their inde-
pendent  work,  they  were  also  provided  with  a  set  of  standardized
prompts  (see  Table  4)  and  a  list  of  sample  questions  to  ask  ChatGPT
related to grammar, vocabulary, and reading comprehension (see Ap-
pendix A). These questions were designed to encourage interaction with
the AI on multiple linguistic levels.

Students were specifically instructed to:

• Ask ChatGPT to explain and compare different English tenses,
• Explore the practical usage of these tenses in everyday contexts,
• Practice  using  the  tenses  in  various  sentence  structures  and

dialogues,

• Request corrections and feedback on their usage, and
• Seek further clarification when they encountered confusion or errors.

During  the  following  weeks,  students  worked  independently  with
ChatGPT from home. This structure was intentionally designed to shift
the primary instructional responsibility to ChatGPT, allowing it to serve
as the students’ main source of grammatical guidance and feedback.

Although all students began with the same set of base prompts, they
were encouraged to personalize their learning experience by adapting
their interactions with ChatGPT to suit their individual needs, interests,
and  difficulties.  This  flexible  approach  aimed  to  foster  autonomous
learning and test the capacity of ChatGPT to respond meaningfully to a
variety of learner inputs.

At the end of the study, each student submitted a recording of their
ChatGPT  conversation,  which  served  as  both  documentation  of  their
engagement and a source of qualitative data for analysis.

did not use ChatGPT or any AI tools during the study.

(English  Grammar  in  Use,  Murphy,  2019),  helping  them  analyze  tense
usage,  refine  their  understanding,  and  apply  rules  in  context.
Throughout, the teacher:

• Delivered explicit explanations of tense structures, rules, and usage
contexts,  often  using  examples  and  comparisons  to  clarify  distinc-

• Modeled correct usage of tenses in spoken and written English,
• Provided  controlled  practice  activities  sourced  from  the  course
textbook  (English  Grammar  in  Use,  Murphy,  2019),  ensuring  that
students had structured opportunities to apply newly learned rules,
• Monitored students' work, offering immediate corrective feedback,

and addressing common errors during class,

• Answered  questions  and  clarified  ambiguities,  helping  students

connect form and meaning,

• Facilitated group reflection, occasionally revisiting grammar points

when patterns of misunderstanding emerged.

Over  the  following  weeks,  students  continued  to  practice  tenses
through  exercises  in  the  textbook,  with  the  teacher  guiding  them
through  each  activity,  correcting  errors,  and  reinforcing  grammatical
accuracy.  This  approach  was  designed  to  ensure  consistency  in  in-
struction  and  to  provide  a  clear  comparison  group  for  evaluating  the

4. Results

4.1. Learning gains across instructional modes (RQ1)

Before and after participating in the activities, students were given a
pre-test and a post-test designed to evaluate their ability to use various
English tenses within a given text. These assessments aimed to quanti-
tatively measure the effectiveness of the interventions on the students'
grammatical proficiency. The maximum possible score on both the pre-
test and post-test was 100 points. The results of each student's pre-test
and  post-test,  along  with  the  engagement  level  determined  through
analysis  of students'  conversations with  ChatGPT (groups  1a/1, 1a/2,
1b/1, and 1b/2), are presented in Appendix C.

Although  all  participants  entered  the  study  having  successfully
completed an English proficiency examination at the B1-B2 level of the
CEFR,  thereby  demonstrating  comparable  general  competence  in  En-
glish, the restriction of analysis to a single linguistic feature (i.e., the use
of verb tenses) revealed, through the pre-test results, the formation of
mixed-ability groups. This outcome may be attributable to a range of
factors,  including  misalignment  between  the  examination  format  and
individual  learner  preferences,  the  incidental  occurrence  of  linguistic
phenomena  not  yet  mastered,  or,  in  the  context  of  voluntary  and
anonymous  participation,  the  absence  of  prior  preparation  on  the
assumption  that  mastery  of  tenses  would  be  achieved  during  the
instructional process.

Given that the potential for progress varies depending on students'
starting  points,  we  employed  the  normalized  gain  formula  to  express
learning  gains  relative  to  the  maximum  possible  improvement.  This
approach  allows  for  a  more  equitable  comparison  across  students  or
groups. Considering how much each student can realistically improve,
the method reduces bias caused by students who scored high on the pre-
test and have less room to grow.

The majority of students, 87.5%, found working with ChatGPT more

3.  The  third  question  was  how  effective  they  find  working  with

Compared to traditional learning methods, 68.75% rated it as more

as less effective.

4.  Fourth, we asked about rating the ease of use of ChatGPT

The ease of use was another positive aspect highlighted by the stu-

5.  In  the  fifth  question,  students  had  an  open-ended  question  where

When asked about the most helpful aspects of using ChatGPT, stu-

and return to challenging concepts as needed. Other beneficial aspects
mentioned  included  assistance  with  writing  letters  and  emails,  the

5. Discussion

This  study  examined  students’  acquisition  of  English  verb  tenses
under three instructional conditions: independent ChatGPT use, teacher-
guided  ChatGPT  use,  and  traditional  instruction.  The  results  provide
insight into how instructional design, learner engagement, and AI tool
behavior interact to influence language learning outcomes.

In response to RQ1, the study confirmed that ChatGPT can effectively
support  grammar  learning,  particularly  when  combined  with  teacher
guidance. The teacher-guided groups achieved the highest normalized
gains and demonstrated the most consistent progress. This finding aligns
with blended learning research emphasizing that digital tools are most
effective when integrated with teacher mediation (Wang et al., 2024). In
this configuration, ChatGPT delivered immediate, individualized feed-
back,  while  the  teacher  provided  scaffolding,  verified  accuracy,  and
encouraged reflection. This dual form of support explains why ChatGPT
with teacher guidance outperformed not only independent AI use but
also traditional instruction, which, despite being structured, lacked the
personalized immediacy of AI feedback.

By contrast, the independent ChatGPT groups produced highly var-
iable outcomes, with some students even showing negative gains. These
regressions  can  be  explained  by  students'  limited  ability  to  evaluate
ChatGPT's  feedback  critically  or  to  sustain  autonomous  learning  be-
haviors. Previous research has highlighted that unguided AI use carries
the risk of learners uncritically accepting inaccurate or inconsistent re-
sponses  (Seo  et  al.,  2021;  Meishar-Tal,  2024).  In  our  study,  this  was
compounded by difficulties in prompt formulation, reported by 25% of
students.  From  the  perspective  of  feedback  literacy  (Carless  &  Boud,
2018), this points to a deficit in AI literacy: without the skills to craft
precise queries or evaluate the quality of responses, learners were un-
able to transform AI output into genuine learning opportunities.

Engagement  emerged  as  a  decisive  factor.  While  enjoyment  levels
were uniformly high, 87.5% of students rated ChatGPT as more enjoy-
able than traditional instruction, effectiveness varied depending on how
actively  students  engaged  with  the  tool.  Engagement  functioned  not
merely as a correlate but as a mediator: higher engagement levels were
strongly predictive of higher normalized gains. This is consistent with
interactionist SLA theory (Long, 1996; Swain, 1995) and the Noticing
Hypothesis (Schmidt, 1990), which emphasize the importance of active,
reflective interaction for learning. In the guided groups, teacher medi-
ation  further  promoted  noticing  by  encouraging  students  to  compare
ChatGPT's explanations with textbook rules, articulate distinctions be-

5.1. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that should be considered when
interpreting the findings. First, all participants were from a single uni-
versity  and  shared  a  similar  academic  background  and  English  profi-
ciency level. Although at the beginning of the research we deliberately
selected students of technical fields to ensure that the results were not
distorted by any inability to work with technology, at no point in the
study  did  participants  need  to  rely  on  their  technical  knowledge.
Working with ChatGPT proved to be so intuitive that it can be easily
transferred  to  students  across  different  disciplines,  and  even  for  high
school  students,  the  manipulation  of  the  technology  itself  should  not
pose  a  barrier,  the  real  challenge  may  rather  lie  in  cases  requiring
autonomous student work. Further research may confirm this premise.
Finally,  the  formulation  of  suitable  prompts  appears to  be  crucial for
successful  outcomes,  where  students  from  linguistics  or  related  pro-
grams  may  even  hold  an  advantage.  Replicating  this  design  across
multiple institutions, and particularly in humanities or non-STEM con-
texts, would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of whether
the observed effects extend across different learner profiles.

Second, the same instructor led all groups and was involved in the
research.  Although  care  was  taken  to  anonymize  data  and  minimize
bias, this dual role may have subtly influenced classroom dynamics or
student performance. Independent replications would help mitigate this
limitation and confirm the robustness of the findings.

Third,  the  relatively  small  sample  sizes  within  each  instructional
group constrained the statistical power and generalizability of the ana-
lyses. Although effect sizes and confidence intervals can provide addi-
tional insights, their stability and interpretive value diminish in small-
sample designs with high within-group variability (Plonsky & Oswald,
2014). At the same time, as outlined in the methodology, we maintain
that preserving a class size comparable to a standard course is essential
for  making  adequate  comparisons  across  instructional  methods.
Increasing the number of students per group could distort the results, as
classroom  dynamics  differ  substantially  in  larger  cohorts.  For  this
reason,  rather  than  expanding  group  sizes,  we  opted  to  replicate  the
experiment in a subsequent semester.

Consequently,  this  study  relied  on  descriptive  statistics  and
normalized gains to highlight instructional trends, rather than making
broad generalizations. This approach is consistent with methodological
guidance for classroom-based SLA research (Mackey & Gass, 2015) and
aligns  with  APA  recommendations  for  small-N  exploratory  studies
(Wilkinson,  &  APA  Task  Force  on  Statistical  Inference,  1999).  Future
research  should  seek  to  replicate  these  findings  in  larger  and  more
diverse samples to enhance statistical stability and external validity.

Fourth,  while  ChatGPT  prompts  were  standardized,  students’
phrasing and follow-up interactions varied considerably. A more fine-
grained  analysis  of  prompt  quality  and  interaction  dynamics  would
help clarify how different engagement styles shape learning outcomes.
Moreover, the study did not track long-term retention of tense mastery;
longitudinal studies following learners beyond the intervention would
provide  valuable  insights  into  the  durability  of  AI-supported  learning
gains.

With regard to the problematic areas of using chat tools mentioned in
the introduction, no bias was observed in activities focused on practicing

language models (e.g., GPT-4/5) may reveal whether recent advances
improve  the  accuracy  and  consistency  of  feedback,  thereby  reducing
some of the limitations observed here.

6. Practical implications

This  study  yields  several  practical  recommendations  for  educators
and curriculum designers seeking to integrate AI tools like ChatGPT into
language learning:

• Teacher  guidance  enhances  reliability  and  learning  outcomes.
ChatGPT  is  most  effective  when  integrated  into  teacher-supported
instruction.  Teachers  help  students  recognize  inaccurate  AI  feed-
back,  clarify  ambiguous  explanations,  and  formulate  purposeful
prompts.

• Students  need  explicit  training  in  AI  literacy.  Instruction  should
include strategies for asking precise questions, verifying responses,
and recognizing when AI-generated feedback is insufficient or inac-
curate.  Students  should  also  learn  how  to  request  clarification,
evaluate  vocabulary  suggestions,  and  compare  tense  choices
critically.

• Autonomy must be scaffolded, not assumed. While ChatGPT enables
self-directed  learning,  this  autonomy  is  productive  only  when
learners possess the metacognitive skills to evaluate feedback and the
motivation  to seek  improvement.  Without  guidance,  students may
either disengage or follow AI suggestions uncritically.

• Variation  in  output  must  be  managed.  Given  that  ChatGPT  may
generate different responses to the same prompt, educators should
help  students  interpret  these  inconsistencies  and  provide  mecha-
nisms to discuss them in class. Shared reflection on AI behavior can
deepen students' understanding of language use and increase their
critical awareness.

• Collaborative  AI  use  can  enhance  reflection.  Encouraging  peer-to-
peer  discussion  around  ChatGPT  responses,  such  as  comparing
different feedback outputs, evaluating examples, or co-constructing
questions,  can  reduce  passive  reliance  on  AI  and  promote  deeper
understanding.

• Feedback  literacy  should  be  embedded  into  grammar  instruction.
Students  benefit  from  learning  how  to  analyze  the  logic  behind
corrections  and  identify  when  explanations  are  insufficient  or
misleading.  Encouraging  students  to  question  ChatGPT  and  seek
justification improves their critical language awareness.

• Explicit training in artificial intelligence (AI) literacy is essential for
educators.  To  effectively  mediate  students'  use  of  tools  such  as
ChatGPT,  teachers  themselves  must  possess  the  necessary  compe-
tencies in AI literacy. Consequently, professional development pro-

human feedback. Moreover, educators should be made aware of the
ways in which students with different profiles may respond variably

By  designing  AI-enhanced  learning  environments  that  emphasize
engagement, reflection, and teacher mediation, educators can maximize
the benefits of tools like ChatGPT while addressing their limitations.

7. Conclusion

This study examined the role of ChatGPT in supporting university
students’ mastery of English tenses across three instructional conditions:
independent AI use, AI use with teacher guidance, and traditional in-
struction.  The  analysis  combined  quantitative  measures  of  learning
outcomes with qualitative insights from student interactions and ques-
tionnaire responses.

Findings  show  that  ChatGPT  can  effectively  enhance  grammar
learning, particularly when paired with teacher support. Students in the
guided  groups  consistently  outperformed  those  using  ChatGPT  inde-
pendently or relying solely on traditional methods. Teacher involvement
was crucial in helping students interpret AI-generated feedback, address
unclear  examples,  and  manage  inconsistent  or  inaccurate  responses.
These  results  support  theories  emphasizing  the  role  of  scaffolding,
noticing, and dialogic interaction in second language acquisition.

Importantly, the study found that student engagement and critical
interaction  with  ChatGPT  were more  predictive  of  learning  outcomes
than tool usage alone. Learners who asked meaningful questions, sought
examples, and reflected on feedback demonstrated the greatest gains. In
contrast, students who used ChatGPT passively or failed to question its
output often showed limited or negative progress.

Student  perceptions  of  ChatGPT  were  largely  positive,  with  many
appreciating its immediacy, encouragement, and ability to tailor feed-
back to their needs. However, students also reported challenges, such as
inconsistencies  in  explanations,  misinterpretation  of  vague  questions,
and  the  tool's  occasional  failure  to  correct  or  justify  revisions.  These
mixed experiences highlight the need for digital feedback literacy and
explicit instruction in how to interact productively with AI tools.

In conclusion, ChatGPT  is  a  valuable educational resource, but its
effectiveness is shaped by how it is implemented and used. It performs
best  not  as  a  stand-alone  tutor  but  as  part  of  a  broader,  teacher-
facilitated instructional approach. Future research should explore how
similar  findings  apply  across  other  grammar  topics,  skill  areas,  and
learner  populations,  and  investigate  how  AI  tools  can  be  refined  to
support  more  consistent,  pedagogically  sound  feedback.  Building  AI
literacy and reflective engagement into language learning curricula will
be essential for realizing the full potential of these tools.
