Introduction
The rapid advancement of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) has significantly transformed
the landscape of higher education by introducing new possibilities for personalized learning,
academic support, content creation, problem-solving, and student-centered instruction. GenAI
tools such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, and other AI-based learning assistants
are increasingly being used by students to generate ideas, summarize academic content, improve
writing, solve complex problems, receive instant feedback, and enhance their understanding of
difficult concepts. Unlike traditional digital learning tools, GenAI can produce human-like
responses, adapt to user queries, and provide customized academic assistance according to
students’ learning needs [1]. As a result, GenAI has become an important technological innovation
that influences how students interact with knowledge, complete academic tasks, and develop
learning skills in modern educational environments. In higher education, student learning
outcomes are considered one of the most important indicators of academic effectiveness. Learning
outcomes refer to the knowledge, skills, abilities, conceptual understanding, critical thinking
capacity, academic performance, and problem-solving competence that students gain through the
learning process. The use of GenAI has the potential to improve these outcomes by supporting
students in different academic activities, including assignment preparation, research writing,
examination preparation, coding, language improvement, and independent learning. When students
use GenAI effectively, they may experience faster access to information, improved clarity of
concepts, better academic confidence, and enhanced productivity. However, the actual impact of
GenAI on learning outcomes is not automatic. It depends on how students use these tools, how
deeply they engage with learning materials, and how capable they are of evaluating AI-generated
information. Student engagement plays a vital role in determining the success of technology-
supported learning. Engagement refers to students’ active participation, motivation, attention,
interaction, emotional involvement, and commitment to academic activities [2]. In a GenAI-
supported learning environment, students may become more engaged because AI tools provide
instant responses, personalized explanations, interactive learning experiences, and continuous
academic support. GenAI can encourage students to ask more questions, explore different
perspectives, revise their work, and participate more actively in learning tasks. Therefore, student
engagement may act as a mediating factor between GenAI usage and student learning outcomes.
This means that GenAI may improve learning outcomes more effectively when it increases
students’ academic involvement and motivates them to participate in the learning process.
Another important factor in the successful use of GenAI is AI literacy. AI literacy refers to
students’ ability to understand, evaluate, use, and ethically apply artificial intelligence tools in
academic contexts. Students with higher AI literacy are more likely to use GenAI responsibly,
verify AI-generated content, identify inaccurate or biased responses, avoid plagiarism, and apply
AI support for meaningful learning rather than simple task completion. In contrast, students with
low AI literacy may become overly dependent on AI-generated answers, copy content without
critical evaluation, or misuse AI tools in ways that reduce learning quality. Therefore, AI literacy
is an essential competency that can influence the relationship between GenAI usage and learning
outcomes. It helps students transform AI assistance into genuine academic improvement. Although
many studies have discussed the growing role of artificial intelligence in education, limited
attention has been given to the combined mediating roles of student engagement and AI literacy in
the relationship between GenAI usage and student learning outcomes [3]. Most existing studies
focus either on the direct benefits of AI tools or on general student attitudes toward technology.
However, there is a need to understand the internal mechanisms through which GenAI contributes
to learning improvement. Specifically, it is important to examine whether GenAI improves
learning outcomes directly or whether this effect occurs through increased engagement and

Generative Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education:
Generative Artificial Intelligence has emerged as one of the most influential technologies in
modern higher education. It refers to advanced AI systems that can generate human-like text,
explanations, summaries, images, codes, learning materials, and academic responses based on user
prompts. Tools such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Grammarly, and other AI-
based learning assistants are increasingly being used by university students to support academic
writing, research activities, problem-solving, language improvement, idea development, and
concept clarification. Unlike traditional educational technologies that mainly provide fixed
content, GenAI offers interactive, adaptive, and personalized responses according to the learner’s
academic needs. In higher education, GenAI is changing the role of students from passive
receivers of information to active learners who can interact with intelligent systems for academic
support. Students can use GenAI tools to ask questions, generate outlines, simplify complex
theories, improve assignments, prepare presentations, and receive instant feedback [5]. This makes
learning more flexible and accessible because students can receive academic assistance at any time
without being limited to classroom hours. GenAI also supports self-directed learning by allowing
students to explore topics independently and revise their understanding through repeated
interaction.

Mediating Role of Student Engagement:
Student engagement plays an important mediating role in explaining how Generative Artificial
Intelligence influences student learning outcomes. Although GenAI tools can provide academic
support, instant feedback, writing assistance, and personalized explanations, these benefits do not
automatically improve learning outcomes unless students actively participate in the learning
process. Student engagement connects GenAI usage with meaningful academic improvement by
increasing students’ motivation, attention, participation, and self-directed learning behavior.
Therefore, engagement acts as a behavioral pathway through which GenAI support is transformed
into improved academic performance and learning achievement [7]. In GenAI-supported learning
environments, students can interact with AI tools to ask questions, revise assignments, clarify
concepts, generate ideas, and receive immediate academic guidance. These interactive features can
make students more involved in their studies because they receive continuous support outside the
classroom. For example, a student who uses GenAI to understand a difficult topic may become
more confident, participate more actively in class, and complete academic tasks more effectively.
In this way, GenAI encourages students to become active learners rather than passive receivers of
information.

Statistical Analysis and Model Testing Strategy:
The statistical analysis and model testing strategy of this study was designed to examine the
relationship between Generative Artificial Intelligence usage, student engagement, AI literacy, and
student learning outcomes. Since the study followed a quantitative research framework, the
collected data were analyzed using statistical procedures that helped identify the strength,
direction, reliability, and significance of relationships among the study variables. The analysis
focused on both direct and indirect effects to determine whether GenAI usage influences student
learning outcomes directly and whether student engagement and AI literacy mediate this
relationship. The data were analyzed using statistical software such as SPSS and SmartPLS. SPSS
was used for preliminary analysis, including data screening, descriptive statistics, reliability
testing, and correlation analysis. SmartPLS was used for structural equation modeling because it is
suitable for testing complex mediation-based models. This combination of tools helped ensure that
the data were properly examined before testing the proposed conceptual framework [19]. The
analysis process was carried out in different stages, beginning with data cleaning and ending with
hypothesis testing and mediation analysis. Before conducting the main analysis, the dataset was
screened to remove incomplete, duplicate, and invalid responses. This step was important to
improve the accuracy and reliability of the findings. After data screening, descriptive statistics
were calculated to summarize the demographic profile of respondents and their general pattern of
GenAI usage. Measures such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were used to
describe the characteristics of the sample and the distribution of responses across the major
constructs.
Reliability analysis was conducted to examine the internal consistency of the measurement items.
Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability values were used to determine whether the items used
to measure each construct were consistent and reliable. A Cronbach’s alpha value above 0.70 was
considered acceptable, indicating that the items had satisfactory reliability. Composite reliability
was also assessed to confirm that the constructs had strong internal consistency. This step ensured
that GenAI usage, student engagement, AI literacy, and student learning outcomes were measured
accurately. Validity analysis was performed to ensure that the research instrument measured the
intended constructs. Convergent validity was assessed through average variance extracted, where a
value above 0.50 indicated that the construct explained sufficient variance in its indicators.
Discriminant validity was examined to confirm that each construct was distinct from the others.
This was necessary because the study included related but different variables, such as student
engagement and AI literacy [20]. Establishing validity helped improve the credibility of the
measurement model. Correlation analysis was used to examine the initial relationships among the
variables. This analysis helped identify whether GenAI usage, student engagement, AI literacy,
and student learning outcomes were positively associated with one another. A positive correlation
among these variables indicated that students who used GenAI more frequently were likely to
report higher engagement, stronger AI literacy, and better learning outcomes. However, correlation

Proposed Mediation-Based Research Model:
The proposed mediation-based research model of this study was developed to explain how
Generative Artificial Intelligence usage influences student learning outcomes through the
mediating roles of student engagement and AI literacy. The model is based on the assumption that
GenAI does not improve learning outcomes only through direct academic support; rather, its effect
becomes stronger when students are actively engaged in learning and possess sufficient AI literacy
to use AI tools responsibly and effectively. Therefore, the proposed model combines technological,
behavioral, and cognitive dimensions of student learning in higher education. In this study,
Generative Artificial Intelligence usage is considered the independent variable. It represents

students’ use of AI-based tools such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Grammarly,
QuillBot, and other GenAI platforms for academic purposes. Students may use these tools for
assignment writing, concept clarification, research support, presentation development, coding
assistance, language improvement, and feedback generation. GenAI usage is expected to support
students by providing personalized explanations, instant responses, and flexible learning assistance
[23]. However, the academic benefit of GenAI depends on how students interact with these tools
and how critically they apply AI-generated information. Student engagement is included as the
first mediating variable in the model. Engagement refers to students’ motivation, participation,
attention, interaction, interest, and self-directed learning behavior. When students use GenAI tools
to ask questions, revise their work, explore ideas, and understand difficult concepts, they may
become more involved in the learning process. This increased engagement can improve their
academic confidence, classroom participation, learning persistence, and task completion.
Therefore, student engagement acts as a behavioral pathway through which GenAI usage
contributes to improved learning outcomes.
AI literacy is included as the second mediating variable in the proposed model. AI literacy refers to
students’ ability to understand, evaluate, and ethically use AI tools in academic contexts. Students
with strong AI literacy are better able to identify inaccurate AI-generated responses, avoid
plagiarism, verify information from academic sources, and use AI as a learning assistant rather
than a replacement for personal effort. In this way, AI literacy acts as a cognitive and ethical
pathway that helps students transform GenAI support into meaningful academic improvement.
Student learning outcomes are treated as the dependent variable in this study [24]. Learning
outcomes include students’ academic performance, conceptual understanding, problem-solving
ability, assignment quality, learning confidence, and knowledge improvement. The model assumes
that GenAI usage can directly improve these outcomes by supporting students in academic tasks.
At the same time, GenAI can indirectly improve learning outcomes by increasing engagement and
strengthening AI literacy. Therefore, the model explains both direct and indirect effects of GenAI
on student academic success.

Future Work
Future research can extend the present study by examining the long-term impact of Generative
Artificial Intelligence on student learning outcomes across different educational levels, institutions,
and academic disciplines. Since the present study focused on undergraduate and postgraduate
students in higher education, future studies may include school students, vocational learners,
distance education students, and professional training participants. This broader educational
coverage would help researchers understand whether the influence of GenAI on learning outcomes
differs according to academic level, learning environment, and student maturity [28]. A
longitudinal research design may also be used to observe how students’ engagement, AI literacy,
academic performance, and problem-solving ability change over time with continuous GenAI
usage. Such an approach would provide stronger evidence regarding whether GenAI produces
short-term academic support or long-term learning improvement. Future studies may also compare
the effects of different GenAI tools on specific learning outcomes. For example, researchers can
compare ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Grammarly, QuillBot, and other AI-based
academic platforms in terms of writing quality, conceptual understanding, assignment
development, research productivity, coding performance, language improvement, and examination
preparation [29]. Different tools may support different types of learning tasks, and their
effectiveness may vary according to students’ academic disciplines. For instance, computer science
and engineering students may benefit more from AI tools that provide coding and problem-solving
assistance, while social science and business students may benefit more from tools that support
academic writing, case analysis, and report development. Therefore, future research should
examine tool-specific and discipline-specific effects of GenAI in higher education. Another
important direction for future work is to include teachers’ perspectives, institutional readiness, and
academic policy support. Although this study focused mainly on students, teachers play a central
role in guiding the responsible and meaningful use of GenAI. Future researchers can investigate
teachers’ attitudes toward GenAI, their readiness to integrate AI tools into teaching, and the
challenges they face in designing AI-supported learning activities. In addition, universities need
clear policies for academic integrity, ethical AI use, assessment redesign, and AI literacy training
[30]. Future studies may examine how institutional policies and teacher guidance influence the
relationship between GenAI usage and student learning outcomes. This would help higher
education institutions develop practical strategies for responsible GenAI adoption.
Future research may also explore additional mediating and moderating variables that can provide a
deeper explanation of GenAI-supported learning. The present study examined student engagement
and AI literacy as mediating variables, but other factors may also influence the effectiveness of
GenAI. Future studies may include digital competence, self-regulated learning, academic
motivation, critical thinking, ethical awareness, technology acceptance, teacher support, learning
style, and student creativity. These variables may help explain why some students benefit more
from GenAI than others. For example, students with strong self-regulated learning skills may use
GenAI for reflection and improvement, while students with low academic motivation may use it
only for quick answers. Including such variables can make future research models more

usage in education. While GenAI provides academic support, it may also lead to overdependence,

behavior if used improperly. Future studies can investigate how excessive GenAI use affects
students’ independent learning ability, creativity, and problem-solving skills. Researchers may also

Conclusion
This study examined the impact of Generative Artificial Intelligence on student learning outcomes,
with student engagement and AI literacy as mediating variables. The findings showed that GenAI
usage has a positive and significant effect on students’ academic performance, conceptual
understanding, assignment quality, problem-solving ability, and learning confidence. The results
indicate that GenAI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Grammarly, and other AI-
based platforms can support students by providing personalized explanations, instant feedback,
academic writing assistance, research support, and flexible learning opportunities. Therefore,
GenAI can be considered an important academic support tool in modern higher education. The
study also confirmed that student engagement plays a meaningful mediating role between GenAI
usage and student learning outcomes. When students use GenAI tools actively for concept
clarification, idea development, revision, and feedback, they become more motivated, involved,
and confident in their learning process. This increased engagement helps transform AI-based
academic support into improved learning performance. Similarly, AI literacy was found to be an
important mediator in the relationship between GenAI usage and learning outcomes. Students who
understand how to evaluate, verify, and ethically use AI-generated content are more likely to
benefit from GenAI tools in a responsible and meaningful way. Overall, the findings suggest that
GenAI alone is not sufficient to guarantee better academic outcomes. Its effectiveness depends on
how actively students engage with learning tasks and how well they understand the responsible use
of AI. The study highlights the need for universities to develop AI literacy programs, ethical AI-
use guidelines, and student-centered GenAI learning strategies. Teachers should also guide
students to use GenAI as a learning assistant rather than a shortcut for completing academic work.
By promoting active engagement and responsible AI literacy, higher education institutions can
