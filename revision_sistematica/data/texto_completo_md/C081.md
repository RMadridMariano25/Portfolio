1. Introduction

Peer feedback plays a key role in fostering students’ critical thinking in higher education, yet its
effectiveness is often limited by feedback quality. This study introduces a Generative Artificial
Intelligence  (GenAI)-supported  peer  feedback  approach  to  enhance  preservice  teachers’  (PSTs)
learning  outcomes  and  critical  thinking.  A  quasi-experimental  design  was  conducted  with  199
PSTs  in  an  online  peer  assessment  task.  Results  show  that  the  GenAI-supported  approach
significantly  improved  feedback  quality,  artifact  revision,  and  PSTs’  critical  thinking.  Further-
more,  the  application  of  self-feedback  moderated  the  relationship  between  GenAI-supported
feedback and critical thinking. These findings highlight the potential of GenAI to enhance peer
feedback and provide implications for integrating GenAI into teacher education.

As an essential skill for the 21st century, critical thinking is not only considered a core goal of higher education but also a key factor
in the professional development of individuals (Barahona et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Although a universally accepted definition is
still lacking, critical thinking is generally described as an attitude, a logical process, and a purposeful reflective and developmental
practice (Chen et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2013). Extensive research has established that critical thinking is closely associated with stu-
dents’ learning performance (Chang & Huang, 2025; Jiang et al., 2023) and plays a positive role in individual achievement in learning,
work, and social participation (Zhan et al., 2023). Therefore, cultivating and developing students’  critical thinking in educational
contexts is of great significance.

Although the necessity of cultivating university students’  critical thinking has been widely acknowledged, enhancing this skill
remains a challenging task for educators (Miri et al., 2007; Zhan et al., 2023). In recent years, online peer assessment (OPA) has
emerged  as  a  promising  pedagogical  approach to  facilitate  the  enhancement  of  critical  thinking (van  Popta et  al.,  2017;  Zeng  &
Ravindran, 2025). Within OPA, learners act as assessors by applying established criteria to review the work of their peers and by
offering constructive feedback, while at the same time they take the role of assessees when being assessed by others (Fang et al., 2021).
Several meta-analyses underscore the beneficial role of peer assessment in cultivating critical thinking (Li et al., 2020; van Popta et al.,
2017; Zhan et al., 2023). Nevertheless, this approach is not without drawbacks, such as the risk of superficial or low-quality feedback
(Kerman,  Banihashem  et  al.,  2024).  Previous  studies  indicate  that  critical  comments  that  include  reasoning  and  suggestions  are

To enhance the caliber of peer feedback, scholars have suggested multiple strategies, such as interactive peer assessment (Lin et al.,

in large classes (Guo, 2024). In response, scholars have begun to investigate the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in peer assessment for

that this system markedly improved the quality of peer feedback. However, empirical research on how Generative Artificial Intelli-
gence (GenAI)–supported peer review influences both academic outcomes and critical thinking among preservice teachers (PSTs) is
still scarce in teacher education. This study introduces an innovative approach using GenAI to guide PSTs in generating high-quality
peer feedback during OPA, aiming to evaluate its effectiveness in enhancing learning outcomes and critical thinking skills.

2. Literature review

2.1. Feedback quality, feedback uptake and critical thinking

Peer assessment involves learners reviewing the work or performance of their peers (Topping et al., 2000). Studies indicate that this
approach can significantly improve students’ comprehension of subject matter (Li et al., 2020), promote higher-order thinking (Zhan
et al., 2023), boost learning motivation (Zheng et al., 2020), and develop feedback literacy (Ketonen et al., 2020). Yet, its contribution
to meaningful learning is largely determined by the quality of the feedback provided (Er et al., 2021; Guo, Pan et al., 2024). In general,
high-quality peer  feedback  should include  both an  objective  evaluation  of  the work  and  actionable  suggestions for  improvement
(Banihashem et al., 2022; Sichterman et al., 2025; Valero Haro et al., 2024). Actionable and specific peer feedback can strengthen the
assessees’  engagement  with  the  feedback  and  encourage  them  to  make  substantive  revisions  to  their  artifacts.  In  contrast,  when
feedback contains negative emotions or lacks specific and actionable suggestions for improvement, assessees may find it difficult to use
effectively (Guo, Pan et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2021). Therefore, high-quality peer feedback requires learners to objectively analyze the
work based on explicit criteria and to provide actionable suggestions that clarify the next steps for improvement (Banihashem et al.,
2024; Prilop & Weber, 2023). Previous studies have indicated that offering specific and constructive suggestions to peers contributes to
improving the quality of peer feedback (Lahza et al., 2025; Valero Haro et al., 2024). In other words, the key to enhancing peer
feedback quality lies in guiding learners to provide constructive feedback.

Feedback uptake reflects how well students absorb and implement the suggestions they receive in later versions of their work
(Alemdag & Yildirim, 2022). This process typically occurs at three levels: ignoring, copying, and applying (Van De Pol et al., 2019). In
OPA, learners act as both assessors and assessees (Zhang et al., 2021). Specifically, as assessees, learners decide whether to adopt their
peers’ suggestions and modify their work accordingly, which represents peer feedback uptake (Li et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2024). As
assessors, learners often reflect on their own work using the same criteria applied to others. By examining the strengths and weaknesses
of peers’ work, assessors can incorporate valuable elements into their own work while avoiding or correcting similar flaws (Cho & Cho,
2011; Zhang et al., 2024). This process is referred to as self-feedback uptake. Earlier research has demonstrated that feedback uptake
significantly impacts students’ learning outcomes (Wood, 2022). For example, Zhang et al. (2024) identified that feedback uptake
functioned as a key mediator, explaining how interactive peer assessment led to enhanced quality in PSTs’ artifacts. Consequently,
feedback uptake is crucial for comprehending how various forms of peer assessment affect PSTs’ academic success.

Critical thinking is universally acknowledged as a fundamental skill in contemporary society (Li et al., 2023). Within higher ed-
ucation, particularly in digital learning settings, it is integral to decision-making and knowledge construction (Hartwell & Aull, 2023).
Consequently, fostering students' critical thinking has been a primary objective of higher education (Zou’bi, 2021). Previous research
has emphasized the unique value and promise of peer feedback in nurturing this skill (Zeng & Ravindran, 2025; Zhan et al., 2023).
According to Zeng and Ravindran (2025), the reasons lie in two aspects: (a) peer feedback increases opportunities to encounter diverse
perspectives,  as  giving  and  receiving  feedback  naturally  stimulates  dialogue  among  students;  and  (b)  peer  feedback  requires  the
cognitive processes  involved  in  critical thinking,  making  it  more likely  that  learners  will  reflect  upon and  internalize  knowledge
through  social  interaction.  Several  studies  also  validate  that  high-quality  peer  feedback  positively  influences  the  development  of
critical thinking (Ekahitanond, 2014). For instance, Jiang et al. (2023) introduced a knowledge-construction-based peer feedback
strategy and demonstrated its effectiveness in enhancing undergraduates’ critical thinking. Similarly, Shao et al. (2024) showed that
peer feedback supported with regulatory scripts effectively promoted students’  critical thinking. Taken together, current research
suggests a strong link between high-quality peer feedback and the enhancement of critical thinking. However, studies focusing on how
peer feedback cultivates this ability among PSTs remain limited (Zeng & Ravindran, 2025; Zhan et al., 2023).

In summary, existing research suggests that high-quality peer feedback is essential for promoting feedback uptake and developing
critical thinking. In the context of teacher education, how to support PSTs in generating constructive and actionable high-quality
feedback remains an urgent challenge. Therefore, exploring new approaches that can effectively enhance the quality of PSTs’ feed-
back and empirically examining their impact on learning outcomes and critical thinking have become pressing and significant research
issues.

2.2. GenAI-supported peer feedback

Although peer assessment offers numerous advantages and educational potential, it faces the persistent challenge of low peer
feedback quality (Banihashem et  al., 2024). Effective  feedback can offer learners meaningful  insights that support  deeper under-
standing and continued improvement of their work (Zhang et al., 2025). Thus, enhancing the quality of peer feedback has been a
central concern in peer assessment research. To address this challenge, prior studies have proposed monitoring and guiding student
feedback during peer assessment. Interventions such as instructor commentary on student-generated feedback (Han & Xu, 2020), the
use of structured scripting to direct peer reviews (Shao et al., 2024), and interactive models of peer assessment (Zhang et al., 2024)
have been introduced. While these approaches can improve feedback quality, their effectiveness is constrained by individual and
contextual factors. First, generating high-quality feedback is intrinsically labor-intensive and time-consuming, which poses challenges
for instructors aiming to deliver detailed evaluations of student-generated feedback (Guo, Zhang et  al., 2024). Second, the effec-
tiveness of regulatory scripts and interactive feedback depends on learners’ prior knowledge and their ability to process and act upon
feedback (Guo, Pan et al., 2024). Finally, most of these approaches are labor-intensive and time-consuming, particularly in large-class
settings (Guo et al., 2025; Guo, Pan et al., 2024).

With the growing integration of GenAI into education, researchers have begun to explore incorporating AI into peer assessment to
automatically generate peer feedback. For instance, Usher (2025) compared the grades and feedback provided by AI chatbots, peers,
and instructors on student projects and found that chatbots often delivered higher-quality feedback than peers, offering more detailed
insights and concrete suggestions for improvement. Similarly, Banihashem et al. (2024) used ChatGPT to provide feedback on stu-
dents’ argumentative essays and assessed its quality against peer-generated responses. Their findings revealed significant differences:
ChatGPT often produced more descriptive evaluations, while peers more frequently highlighted critical issues in the work. Although
GenAI demonstrates strong potential for delivering immediate and detailed feedback, it may also generate irrelevant or contradictory
information that requires student intervention (Usher, 2025). This challenge primarily stems from the “hallucination” phenomenon, in
which GenAI may misinterpret learners’ inputs and produce inaccurate feedback (Feldman-Maggor et al., 2025; Jukiewicz, 2024). To
address this, researchers have proposed integrating AI-generated and human feedback to leverage their complementary strengths
(Banihashem et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). For example, Guo, Pan et al. (2024) proposed an approach where AI assesses peer feedback
to elevate its quality, observing notable gains in both feedback caliber and the writing abilities of English majors. Nevertheless, within
teacher education, it remains unclear how effectively GenAI can facilitate peer feedback and advance PSTs’ critical thinking.

Therefore, this study introduces a GenAI-supported peer feedback approach, the core of which lies in constructing a human-AI
collaboration model. This model does not seek to replace students’ central role in the cognitive process but aims to improve feed-
back quality through AI empowerment. The study will empirically examine the effectiveness of this approach and explores the un-
derlying mechanisms through which GenAI facilitates improved learning outcomes and the development of critical thinking among
PSTs.

3.2. Technical settings

This study employed the Chaoxing Learning Platform (https://www.chaoxing.com/) to support OPA. The platform consists of four
core  modules:  courseware,  discussion,  assignments,  and  course  mapping.  All  OPA  tasks  were  carried  out  within  the  assignment

feedback group and  the other for the  traditional feedback group. According to their group assignment, PSTs uploaded their arti-
facts to the appropriate forum. During the peer assessment stage, students were asked to download and evaluate their peers’ artifacts,
then offer feedback by replying in the forum. Each feedback entry was required to include both a score and written comments (see
Fig.2). After receiving feedback from peers, students made revisions to their artifacts and submitted the updated versions within the
assignment module.

This study employed the Doubao platform (https://www.doubao.com/chat/), a popular GenAI tool in China, to implement GenAI-
supported peer feedback. Doubao provides functions such as a chatbot, a writing assistant, and an English learning assistant. Students
in the experimental group were required to use Doubao to critique their own comments, revise them based on the GenAI feedback, and
then provide the improved comments to their peers. Conversely, students in the control group directly provided their comments to
peers without using any GenAI tool to refine them. In the experimental group, we adopted the ICIO prompting framework to facilitate

3.3. Procedure

The experimental workflow of this study is depicted in Fig.4. During the first week, the instructor presented the concepts of TEL
design to all classes and assigned a learning task via the Chaoxing learning platform, requiring each PST to submit a TEL design. All
participants were asked to complete their artifacts and upload them to the platform within the first week.

In Week 2, both groups first completed the critical thinking tendency questionnaire in class. The instructor then provided the rubric
for evaluating TEL designs and explained the indicators of each dimension. Through the use of illustrative cases, PSTs were further
guided to understand and apply the rubric effectively.

In Week 3, students from both groups engaged in peer assessment based on the provided OPA rubric. The experimental group
utilized the GenAI-supported peer feedback, whereas the control group used the traditional peer feedback. To ensure active partici-
pation, each PST was required to provide feedback to three peers based on their student ID numbers (e.g., Student 1 provided feedback
to  Students  2,  3,  and  4,  and  so  on).  This  design  ensured  that  every  student  both  provided  feedback  to  three  peers  and  received
evaluations from three peers in return.

In Week 4, participants in both groups refined their artifacts using the feedback they had received and resubmitted them to the

Chaoxing platform. Subsequently, all PSTs were asked to complete the critical thinking tendency questionnaire again.

3.4. Instruments

3.4.1. Feedback quality

This study adopted an existing coding framework for feedback quality (Cheng et al., 2015) to analyze feedback content across three
dimensions: affective, cognitive, and metacognitive, as outlined in Table 1. The rationale for selecting this coding framework is that, in
higher education, online peer feedback content is generally classified into three categories: cognitive, affective, and metacognitive
(Gao et al., 2024). In this study, each individual feedback comment served as the unit of analysis. Previous studies indicate that
cognitive feedback is more beneficial than affective feedback for students’ knowledge acquisition, and metacognitive feedback is more
effective than affective feedback in promoting higher-order thinking (Lin et  al., 2021). Accordingly, cognitive and  metacognitive
feedback were defined as high-quality feedback, whereas affective feedback was defined as low-quality feedback. To ensure coding
reliability, two trained coders (the second and third authors of this study) independently coded 150 peer feedback comments (25% of
the total dataset). Inter-rater reliability, assessed using Cohen’s kappa, was 0.848. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through
discussion until consensus was reached. After this, each coder independently coded half of the remaining data.

emphasizing instead the dynamic interaction among these three domains (Schmid et al., 2021). Owing to this theoretical advantage,
the TPACK framework has been validated as a reliable and comprehensive approach for assessing TEL design quality (Schmid et al.,

Improvement in TEL design was calculated as the post-feedback artifact score minus the pre-feedback artifact score.

3.4.3. Feedback uptake

To examine how students engaged with peer and self-feedback (i.e., feedback they provided to others but subsequently integrated
into their own work revisions), this study categorized feedback uptake into three types: Ignore, Copy, and Apply (Zhang et al., 2021).
“Ignore” indicates that learners made no use of the feedback in their revisions. “Copy” refers to instances where learners reproduced or
inserted feedback verbatim without meaningful adaptation. “Apply” describes revisions where learners thoughtfully integrated and
elaborated upon the feedback. To ensure coding reliability, the same reliability procedure was employed. The results showed a Cohen’s
kappa coefficient of 0.859.

3.4.4. Critical thinking tendency questionnaire

The critical thinking tendency questionnaire adapted for this study, based on Lin et al. (2021), measures students’  capacity to
critically analyze and evaluate information (Liu et al., 2018). The instrument consists of six items, including “During peer assessment, I
determine which facts are reliable” and “I support my opinions with reasons and evidence in peer assessment.” Participants rated each
item on a five-point Likert scale. The total score for each participant represented the sum of all six items. The modified version of the
questionnaire demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.885.

4. Results

Table 2 displays the frequency and percentage distribution of each feedback type across the experimental and control conditions.

4.3. Feedback uptake

To address RQ3, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to examine feedback uptake, and the findings are summarized in Table 4. For
peer feedback uptake, significant differences emerged between the experimental and control groups in the ignore (p < .001) and apply
(p < .001) categories, whereas no difference was found for copy (p = .829). Both ignore and apply demonstrated large effect sizes.
Specifically, PSTs using GenAI-supported peer feedback applied peer suggestions more frequently and disregarded them less often than
those using traditional peer feedback.

Concerning self-feedback uptake, significant group differences were identified across all three categories: ignore (p < .001), copy (p
< .001), and apply (p = .003). The effect size for apply was moderate, while those for ignore and copy were large. Specifically, PSTs in
the experimental condition demonstrated higher frequencies of both copying and applying self-feedback relative to the control group.
Overall, these results indicate that PSTs supported by the GenAI-based approach were more inclined to integrate both peer and self-
feedback into their TEL designs and were less likely to overlook peer suggestions than those in the control condition.

4.4. Critical thinking tendency

Descriptive statistics for critical thinking tendency are summarized in Table 5. To examine how group assignment and testing time
influenced critical thinking tendency, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted, and the outcomes are shown in Table 6. A
significant interaction effect emerged between group and time (F = 3.92, p = .049, ηp

2  = 0.02).

As illustrated in Fig.5, the control group recorded marginally higher scores at the pretest stage (M = 21.97, SD = 4.35) compared

the experimental condition demonstrated greater improvements in critical thinking tendency across time than those in the control

4.5. Moderation analyses

To investigate whether feedback uptake moderates the connection between peer feedback approach and critical thinking, we first
conducted Pearson correlation analyses (see Table 7). The findings indicate that the peer feedback approach was positively correlated
with copying self-feedback, applying self-feedback, and applying peer feedback, while it was negatively correlated with ignoring self-
feedback and ignoring peer feedback. However, no significant correlation was observed between the peer feedback approach and
copying peer feedback. Regarding critical thinking tendency, a significant positive correlation was found with applying self-feedback,
whereas no significant correlations were detected with other variables, including the peer feedback approach, ignoring self-feedback,
copying self-feedback, ignoring peer feedback, copying peer feedback, and applying peer feedback.

The moderating effect was further tested using PROCESS (Model 1; bootstrap resamples = 5000) (Hayes, 2018). The independent
variable was the feedback approach (coded as 1 for experimental, 0 for control), the dependent variable was the posttest score of
critical thinking tendency, and the pretest score of critical thinking tendency served as a covariate. Moderator variables included
significantly different forms of feedback uptake (i.e., ignoring peer feedback, applying peer feedback, ignoring self-feedback, copying
self-feedback, and  applying self-feedback).  The results  indicated  that  none of  the  following  variables  significantly moderated  the
relationship between peer feedback approach and critical thinking tendency: ignoring self-feedback (B = –1.277, SE = 0.654, p =
.052), copying self-feedback (B = –0.222, SE = 2.785, p = .937), ignoring peer feedback (B = –0.657, SE = 0.549, p = .233), or applying
peer feedback (B = –0.237, SE = 0.930, p = .799).

As indicated in Table 8, applying self-feedback did not exhibit a significant direct effect on critical thinking tendency (B = –0.186,
SE = 0.337, p = .582). However, the interaction term (peer feedback approach × applying self-feedback) showed a significant positive
influence (B = 1.629, SE = 0.684, p = .018). The overall moderation model reached statistical significance, F (4, 194) = 22.656, p <
.001, explaining roughly 31.8% of the variance in critical thinking tendency (R² = 0.318). These findings highlight that applying self-
feedback strengthens the positive effect of peer feedback approach on critical thinking tendency.

5. Discussion

This  research  introduced  a  GenAI-supported  peer  feedback  approach  aimed  at  improving  the  quality  of  peer  feedback  and
advancing the development of critical thinking among PSTs. The results support the effectiveness of this approach in improving peer
feedback quality, aligning with findings from previous research (Guo, Pan et al., 2024). More importantly, from a broader interactive
learning perspective, the significance of this study lies not only in showing that GenAI can improve the quality of peer feedback text,
but also in demonstrating its potential role as a cognitive scaffold, an evaluative mediator, and a trigger for self-monitoring (Chien
et al., 2024; Tu et al., 2026; Zhan & Yan, 2025).

mechanism that enables assessors to interact with the GenAI until they are satisfied with the feedback quality (Guo, 2024). Supported
and guided by GenAI, assessors are inclined to examine peer artifacts more meticulously and supply more precise, actionable rec-
ommendations (i.e., more guiding types of cognitive feedback). In other words, when students use GenAI to support peer feedback,
they tend to offer more constructive comments (Noroozi, Haddadian et al., 2025). With respect to metacognitive feedback, no marked
differences emerged between experimental and control groups, a result that echoes earlier work (Lin, 2018). Across both conditions,
reflective feedback emerged as the most common form, likely because assessors, guided by the evaluation rubric provided in this study,
offered more comments on TEL design artifacts to stimulate peers’ self-reflection. In terms of affective feedback, no significant dif-
ferences were detected between groups, and both groups produced relatively high frequencies of supportive feedback. This may be
attributed to the tendency of GenAI to produce neutral or direct feedback (Noroozi, Haddadian et al., 2025). In addition, this could be
explained by the Confucian cultural context, where students may prefer to compliment their peers and avoid openly challenging them
to  prevent  embarrassment  (Zhan,  2019,  2021).  Taken  together,  these  findings  suggest  that  GenAI  more  clearly  strengthened  the
cognitive dimension of peer feedback, whereas its influence on affective and relational dimensions remained relatively limited. Recent
studies have emphasized that meaningful learning depends not only on the quality of information exchange, but also on the relational
and discursive processes through which learners negotiate meaning together (Cheng et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024;
Zeng & Ravindran, 2025). From this perspective, the present GenAI-supported approach mainly strengthened the cognitive produc-
tivity  of  the  existing  peer  feedback  workflow,  while  social  interaction,  emotional  support,  and  relational  development  remained
relatively under-addressed.

In terms of improving TEL design artifacts, the GenAI-supported approach was more beneficial than conventional peer feedback.
This finding is consistent with previous studies (Chen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). Moreover, the experimental group outperformed
the control group across all seven TPACK-related subdimensions, suggesting that the value of GenAI-supported peer feedback lies not
only in improving comments themselves, but also in supporting the effective transformation of evaluative information into revision
(Zhang et al., 2024). Previous research has shown that whether feedback promotes learning depends not only on whether students
receive feedback, but also on whether they can understand, judge, and productively use it (Carless & Boud, 2018; Wood, 2022). The
present findings  further  support  this view,  indicating  that GenAI  may  help  learners  convert  evaluative information  into  concrete
revision actions by improving the quality and clarity of feedback (Cheng et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). However, this
value still appears to be expressed mainly in the improvement of task performance, rather than in the reorganization of the broader
interaction ecology. In teacher education, PSTs need not only strong academic performance, but also the ability to participate in
learning processes that are pedagogically meaningful, ethically informed, and relationally sensitive (Lu et al., 2024). For this reason, AI
in education is better understood not merely as a productivity tool, but as part of a broader rethinking of teaching, learning, and
assessment (Panday-Shukla, 2025).

Regarding feedback uptake, the findings showed that PSTs in the GenAI-supported condition were more likely to incorporate both
peer and self-feedback into the refinement of their TEL design artifacts, and less likely to merely copy or ignore feedback, compared
with those in the traditional setting. This outcome aligns broadly with earlier work using interactive peer review to monitor PST
feedback (Zhang et al., 2024), indicating that GenAI-supported peer feedback encourages higher-level uptake (i.e., “application”)
while reducing lower-level uptake (i.e., “copying”). Prior research has indicated that feedback features and quality are critical de-
terminants of feedback uptake (Wu & Schunn, 2020a). As feedback quality improves, students are more likely to apply feedback rather
than ignore or copy it (Wu & Schunn, 2020b). In addition, GenAI-supported peer feedback provides PSTs with more comprehensive
and clearer feedback accompanied by concrete suggestions (Zhu et al., 2025). In our study, PSTs using GenAI-supported peer feedback
produced more high-quality feedback than those relying on traditional approach, which in turn facilitated their higher-level uptake of
feedback. More importantly, this result suggests that the educational value of GenAI lies not only in helping students produce better
comments, but also in prompting them to internalize evaluative criteria and apply them to the revision of their own work. From this
perspective, the role of GenAI in interactive learning is better understood as scaffolding learners’ processes of evaluation, interpre-
tation, revision, and self-monitoring (Chang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025). In this sense, GenAI may support the development of PSTs’
feedback literacy and reflective practice (Nguyen & Nguyen, 2025; Zhan & Yan, 2025).

With regard to critical thinking tendency, this study demonstrated that GenAI-supported peer feedback promoted the development
of PSTs’  critical thinking tendency, consistent with  prior research. For instance, Liu et  al. (2025) proposed a ChatGPT-generated
content approach supported by concept mapping and peer assessment, demonstrating that this approach not only enhanced PSTs’
learning motivation but also markedly elevate their critical thinking tendency. Similarly, in EFL writing contexts, peer assessment
approaches  integrating  automated  assessment  significantly  improved  students’  critical  thinking  skills  (Liu  et  al.,  2023).  Critical
thinking represents a higher-order cognitive skill that involves reflecting on one’s own and others’  work, evaluating multiple per-
spectives, and employing logical reasoning to form judgments (Lin et al., 2021; Rezaei & Beheshti Shirazi, 2024). GenAI-supported
peer feedback provided PSTs with opportunities to engage in deeper reflection on both peers’ artifact and the quality of their own
feedback, thereby effectively supporting the growth of their critical thinking. However, this improvement appears to be grounded
primarily in the enhancement of cognitive activity within an existing peer feedback task. In interactive learning more broadly, the
development  of  critical  thinking  depends  not  only  on  the  quality  of  opinions,  but  also  on  wider  social  conditions,  including  the
negotiation of multiple perspectives, relational safety, responsibility taking, and value judgment (Hwang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025).
Moreover, students need to develop GenAI literacy in order to maintain critical reflection on feedback generated by GenAI and to
prepare for a future society characterized by human–AI coexistence (Bozkurt et al., 2024; Noroozi, Haddadian et al., 2025).

More importantly, the study reveals the underlying mechanism by which GenAI-supported peer feedback fosters PSTs’  critical

6. Conclusions

This study employed a quasi-experimental design to investigate the effects of GenAI-supported peer feedback on PSTs’ learning
outcomes and critical thinking. The results demonstrate that the GenAI-supported feedback approach significantly enhanced feedback
quality, facilitated artifact improvement, promoted feedback uptake, and effectively fostered the development of critical thinking
among PSTs. Notably, the application of self-feedback served as a significant moderator in this process, effectively strengthening the
positive relationship between GenAI-supported peer feedback and critical thinking. These findings not only confirm the efficacy of
GenAI  in  enhancing  peer  feedback  among  PSTs  but  also  provide  practical  insights  for  integrating  GenAI  into  teacher  education
contexts.

The empirical contributions of this study are twofold. First, it marks the initial effort to evaluate the efficacy of GenAI-supported
peer feedback within teacher education. A trailblazing investigation by Guo, Pan et al. (2024), set in an English as a Foreign Language
writing  context,  illustrated  that AI-assisted  peer  feedback  can elevate  both  feedback  quality  and  writing  performance.  Our  study
further validates that the GenAI-supported peer feedback approach effectively improves the quality of PSTs’ TEL design and fosters
their critical thinking, thereby demonstrating the successful transferability of this approach to teacher education. Second, the study
identifies the underlying mechanism, revealing that the application of self-feedback positively moderates the relationship between
GenAI-supported  peer  feedback  and  critical  thinking.  As  the  level  of  self-feedback  application  increases,  the  predictive  effect  of
GenAI-supported peer feedback on critical thinking becomes stronger.

The findings offer several practical implications for educational practices involving peer feedback. First, compared with traditional
peer feedback approaches, GenAI-supported peer feedback helps cultivate PSTs’ critical thinking. This finding can be directly applied
in teacher education to foster PSTs’ higher-order thinking skills. Second, GenAI-supported peer feedback enables PSTs to generate
higher-quality  feedback  and  significantly  increases  feedback  uptake.  This  innovative  approach  presents  a  practical  strategy  for
improving peer feedback in higher education, as it can be easily implemented using existing GenAI tools and extended to diverse
learning contexts such as medical training, language learning, and writing instruction. Third, the moderation effect indicates that the
application of self-feedback plays a crucial role in fostering PSTs’ critical thinking. Therefore, instructors should encourage students to
engage in reflective practices, consistently evaluating whether similar issues exist in their own work. This practice not only improves
feedback uptake but also enhances critical thinking. Finally, from a theoretical standpoint, the results emphasize the capacity of GenAI
technologies to supervise peers and enhance feedback quality, offer new avenues for teacher intervention. In higher education con-
texts, GenAI can provide adaptive learning support, particularly in large-scale classrooms.

Although this study offers meaningful insights, it is not without limitations. First, the experimental period for GenAI-supported peer
feedback was relatively short, and the results may have been influenced by novelty effects (Lu & Ba, 2025). Second, in this study,
GenAI was tasked with providing feedback on PSTs’ comments, rather than evaluating the original TEL design artifacts themselves. As
such, GenAI-generated feedback may not fully capture the accuracy and comprehensiveness of PSTs’ evaluations of the original ar-
tifacts. Third, although quantitative methods confirmed that GenAI-supported peer feedback enhances PSTs’  feedback quality and
critical thinking, the specific behavioral differences between experimental and control groups during peer interactions remain un-
explored. Fourth, the assessment of critical thinking relied primarily on self-reported measures, which may not accurately reflect
actual behaviors (Noroozi, Alqassab et al., 2025). Moreover, the statistical analysis did not account for potential data clustering effects.
Building upon these findings, several directions for future research are proposed.

(1) Future studies could extend the experimental duration, for example across an entire semester, to better evaluate the influence of
GenAI-supported peer feedback on learners’ feedback quality and critical thinking.
(2) To enhance the effectiveness of GenAI-generated feedback on learners’  comments, future work might consider integrating
peers’ original artifacts into prompt design and utilizing more advanced large language models (e.g., GPT-5) to enhance GenAI’s
feedback generation capabilities.
(3) To explore learners’ behavioral patterns during GenAI-supported peer feedback activities, future studies may adopt temporal
modeling approaches such as sequential pattern mining and epistemic network analysis (e.g., Moon, McNeill et al., 2024; Moon,
Yeo, et al., 2024) to uncover underlying behavioral dynamics, thereby clarifying the mechanisms through which GenAI facilitates
learning performance.
(4) Finally, subsequent studies may adopt more diverse data collection methods, including physiological indicators, behavioral
observations,  and  interviews,  to  enable  triangulation  and  offer  a  more  comprehensive  and  objective  understanding  of  the  re-
lationships among variables and their underlying mechanisms.
