This  study  examines  the  perceived  influence  of  Generative  Artificial  Intelligence  (GenAI)  on
Higher  Order  Thinking  Skills  (HOTS)  in  higher  education  through  a  multi-actor  analysis  at
Universidad de los Andes, Colombia. Using a concurrent mixed-methods approach, we conducted
nine semi-structured interviews with academic administrators, twelve focus groups with sixty-five
faculty members, and surveyed 529 students. Qualitative data were coded for four HOTS: critical
thinking, creative  thinking, reasoning, and metacognition. Findings reveal that concerns  about
creative thinking were the most frequently mentioned (31%), followed by general cognitive skills
(25%) and critical thinking (24%), while reasoning (11%) and metacognition (9%) received less
attention. Notably, patterns of concern varied significantly across stakeholder groups: students
expressed greatest worry about creative thinking (50% of coded entries), fearing loss of creative
capacity through AI dependency; faculty showed more balanced concerns and openness to using
GenAI  as  a  creativity  catalyst;  academic  administrators  prioritized  critical  thinking  (30%)  and
reasoning  (26%),  linking  these  to  digital  literacy  and  institutional  quality.  Results  suggest  the
need for developmental frameworks that consider when and how AI assistance is pedagogically
appropriate,  multi-level  institutional  responses  addressing  divergent  stakeholder  concerns,  and
ongoing  dialogue  about  balancing  efficiency  gains  with  cognitive  development.  The  research
contributes  to  understanding  GenAI’s  complex,  sometimes  contradictory  impacts  on  thinking
skills in higher education.

monitoring of one’s own learning have been recognized as essential lifelong learning skills (Schraw et al., 2011; Halpern, 2013).
Moreover, thinking skills have been considered fundamental for responding to current social and technological challenges, guiding
professional, technical, and technological training (Fan et al., 2025).

Several ways of conceiving thinking skills exist, each with particular advantages and downsides. Two frameworks stand out. On one
side, Webb’s Depth of Knowledge (DOK) framework categorizes learning tasks based on cognitive complexity and depth of mental
processing (Martin, 2016). Unlike difficulty-based or Bloom-style classifications, DOK examines what happens in a student’s mind,
organizing tasks into four levels: recall and reproduction, skills and concepts, strategic thinking/reasoning, and extended thinking
(Martin, 2016).

On the other side, frameworks such as Bloom’s taxonomy distinguish between Lower Order Thinking Skills (LOTS), which include
skills such as memorizing, recalling, and understanding (Tikhonova & Kudinova, 2015), and Higher Order Thinking Skills (HOTS),
which  involve  mental  engagement  with  ideas,  concepts,  and  situations  in  an  analogical,  elaborative,  inductive,  deductive,  and,
otherwise, transformational ways indicative of knowing, as a complex, effortful, generative, evidence-based, and reflective activity
(Alexander et al., 2011). Both LOTS and HOTS stem from Bloom’s original taxonomy of cognitive skills (knowledge, comprehension,
application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation), and they are interconnected since LOTS serve as the foundation for more complex
HOTS (King et al., 1998). Beyond these dominant frameworks, there are also other alternative conceptions of thinking skills and their
relationships (e.g., Ellerton et al., 2022; Newton et al., 2020), offering different perspectives on cognitive development and learning.
In this paper, we employ the classical LOTS and HOTS taxonomy insofar as it enables sustained conversation within this well-
established tradition and aligns with the categories identified by our participants.1 We focus on HOTS, given their importance in
understanding  the  impact  of  generative  artificial  intelligence  (GenAI)  in  higher  education  (Fan  et  al.,  2025).  Additionally,  we
grounded our study on Fan et al. (2024), who contextualize HOTS in higher education within the current challenges of GenAI. They
considered four categories: critical thinking, creative thinking, problem-solving, and metacognitive skills. Drawing from this frame-
work and from our analysis, we identified the following four key thinking skills: critical thinking, creative thinking, metacognition, and
reasoning.

While we consider that these four skills encompass the core of HOTS as understood by different authors, we acknowledge that the
choice is to some extent arbitrary, and we leave open the door for further research on other HOTS. Alexander et al. (2011), for instance,
explain that intellectual exercise for epistemic purposes is both reflective and analytical. They argue that higher-order thinking is
oriented toward ideas and objects of understanding and reflection, with the purpose of generating knowledge (Alexander et al., 2011).
The skills selected here represent these two purposes of analysis (critical thinking and reasoning) and reflection on one’s own thinking
(metacognition). Pellas (2025) also highlights the importance of critical thinking and creativity as core HOTS.

Critical thinking has been broadly conceptualized as the ability to systematically evaluate information to make decisions, reach
sound judgements or develop a justified position on an issue. According to Ennis (1987), this skill refers to reflective thinking focused
on deciding what to believe or do. In addition, critical thinking can be also considered as a set of dispositions, such as “simplicity,
comprehensiveness, value, comprehensibility, conformity of its language to our everyday language, and the fitting of subordinates
under  superordinate”  (Ennis,  1996,  p.  180),  and  skills,  such  as  focusing,  analyzing  arguments,  formulating  questions,  evaluating
evidence, defining terms, identifying assumptions, and comparing possible outcomes (Schraw et al., 2011; Halpern, 2013).

Creativity  encompasses  a  natural  set  of  behaviors  that  can  be  taught  and  learned,  rather  than  an  innate  ability  (Gl˘aveanu  &
Beghetto, 2021). It involves divergent and convergent thinking to produce new ideas, using innovative approaches for evaluating
accurately shortcomings, and developing appropriate solutions when discovering and solving problems (Runco & Jaeger, 2012; Runco,
2023). Just the act of generating solutions to problems requires the creative process of going beyond previously learned concepts and
rules (King et al., 1998). Creative processes involve both cognitive capabilities (fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration (Sun
et al., 2022) and adaptive behaviors, such as selecting the relevant aspects of a problem, integrating information into a coherent
system that allows searching for new information and developing novel solutions (Crowl et al., 1997; Nijstad et al., 2010).

Reasoning responds to a process that mediates knowledge inputs and cognitive outcomes (Schraw et al., 2011). Reasoning skills
encompass deductive, inductive, abductive, and probabilistic processes, each playing a vital role in higher-order thinking. Deductive
reasoning involves constructing sound arguments with valid structure and true premises, guaranteeing the truth of the conclusion this
way. In contrast, inductive reasoning generalizes known observations to unknown cases and is fundamental for scientific inquiry, as
well as expanding categorical knowledge (Engelman et al., 2024). While deduction is constrained by existing evidence, induction
extends  beyond  it,  allowing  for  conjectures  about  the  unknown.  Abductive  reasoning  involves  formulating  the  most  plausible
explanation for a specific set of observations or facts, focusing on creating a probable hypothesis that best explains the particulars of a
situation. Probabilistic reasoning is based on degrees of belief, and conclusions can be more or less likely, requiring the retrieval of
prior  knowledge  from  memory.  Reasoning  types  are  foundational  to  logical,  moral,  legal,  and  probabilistic  thinking,  enabling

Metacognition is considered as a way of self-regulating, monitoring, and being aware of one’s own thinking (Schraw et al., 2011).
Two dimensions are considered in this process: (i) knowledge of cognition, which involves declarative knowledge or knowledge about
us as learners and factors influencing our performance, and (ii) regulation of cognition, which involves planning (goals and time),

Studies linking GenAI to HOTS presuppose that GenAI use influences thinking skills and teaching-learning processes, with effects
that may be either beneficial or detrimental. Furthermore, the development and performance of HOTS is crucial for the use of GenAI
(Fields, 2024; Pellas, 2025; Runco, 2023). On the one hand, some of the negative effects of the use of GenAI that college students have
identified are related to creative thinking: students report that GenAI is taking over the thinking process and thereby stifling human
creativity (Anderson et al., 2024). Also identified as a risk factor is the potential reduction of students’ self-efficacy (Fields, 2024).
Other studies have identified that excessive use of GenAI in the writing process significantly limits students’ ability to produce original
ideas. It carries a risk of technological dependence when creative thinking is required in writing (Bal & Arseven, 2026). Such effects on
learning processes also put into question the use of traditional pedagogies in the classroom (Fields, 2024).

On the other hand, the positive effects of the use of GenAI in higher education include adaptability to the learning style of each
student, allowing students to encourage and question their own ideas and enhance their reasoning skills. For this reason, adequate
guidance from professors and clarity in the development of HOTS in students is fundamental in higher education (Fields, 2024).
Likewise, other studies confirm that HOTS development using GenAI among college students depends significantly on whether it is
used intentionality and pedagogically or not (Huang et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Bal & Arseven, 2026). Some of these studies agree
that cultivating HOTS, such as critical thinking and problem solving, is generated when students develop the skill of constructing
arguments in the process of constructing knowledge. To this aim, it is necessary to personalize teaching strategies according to the
levels and needs of the learners (Huang et al., 2024).

Studies show that GenAI can be used as support for the cognitive development of HOTS, in addition to generating a positive attitude
towards its use (Fields, 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Pellas, 2025). Furthermore, quantitative research finds that higher levels of academic
achievement are associated with greater creativity, which in turn positively influences attitudes toward GenAI, and critical thinking
and problem  solving, which are  crucial to enhance the positive  association between academic  performance and attitudes  toward
machine learning (Pellas, 2025). GenAI allows academic processes to be developed more effectively, which in turn can strengthen the
development of HOTS (Huang et al., 2024). It has further been found that the use of GenAI promotes, to a certain extent, metacognitive
skills such as self-regulated learning (Guo et al., 2025). Recommendations found in the literature for the proper inclusion of GenAI in
higher education highlight developing critical thinking, including ethical discussions, and fostering creative thinking (Fields, 2024;
Pellas, 2025).

Given  the  importance  of  HOTS  for  learning,  together  with  the  emergence  of  GenAI  as  a  disruptive  technology  in
education—especially regarding its impact on thinking skills in higher education—the question emerges how GenAI impacts HOTS in
higher education. This paper addresses this question in terms of the perception of impact of GenAI on HOTS at Universidad de los
Andes, Colombia. The aim is to contribute to this pressing issue regarding the use of GenAI in higher education.

2. Methodology

The results presented in this paper are part of a broader study, which aims at understanding the perception and use of GenAI at
Universidad de los Andes, Colombia (Monta˜na, 2026). This paper presents a secondary qualitative analysis of the data of the previous
study, using semi-structured interviews with academic administrators and focus groups with full-time faculty, as well as answers to
open-ended questions from the student survey. This paper does not use any quantitative data. This qualitative study was designed to
capture  both the  depth  of  expert perspectives  from administrators  and  faculty and  the  breadth  of  student  experiences  across  the
institution.

The qualitative data collected encompassed in-depth semi-structured interviews with academic administrators and focus group
discussions with full-time faculty members. Interview and focus group protocols were developed based on the research objectives and
piloted  before  implementation.  Questions  explored  participants’  experiences  with  GenAI,  and  their  perceptions  of  its  impact  on
teaching and learning. A call to participate in the study was disseminated through emails administered via the Office of the Academic
Vice-Chancellor. The survey instrument included open-ended questions asking students to identify the advantages and disadvantages
of GenAI tools in their academic work. This survey employed a convenience sample and was disseminated across digital communi-
cation platforms, electronic mail, the institution’s learning management system, and physical campus venues where the University
mascot distributed QR code links to prospective respondents.2 The convenience sampling methodology limited representativity by
school. Participation varied across institutional units, with some schools showing lower response rates.

Qualitative  data  collection  occurred  during  September  2024,  whereas  the  survey  data  were  gathered  across  the  period  from
September 2024 through January 2025. The research team conducted 9 semi-structured interviews with academic administrators,
including  department  chairs,  academic  deans,  and  vice-chancellors,  lasting  45-60  minutes  each.  In  addition,  12  focus  groups

The student survey collected 212 open responses from participants with a mean age of 21 years, a median academic progression of

the same period.

Data analysis proceeded in two phases. Initially, all qualitative data (interview and focus group transcripts, as well as open-ended
survey responses) were coded thematically to identify major concerns and perspectives regarding GenAI use in higher education. This
initial coding revealed that thinking skills, HOTS in particular, emerged as a central preoccupation across all actors. After data analysis
of the study’s main goal, it became clear that HOTS were of central concern for all actors. Accordingly, the research group conducted a
re-codification of the original qualitative data, initially introducing codes for four HOTS, i.e., creative thinking, critical thinking,
reasoning, and metacognition. An emergent code for general cognitive skills was then added, in order to capture participants’  re-
flections on cognitive skills that were not precisely articulated. First, two members of the research team independently coded a subset
of the data according to coding criteria and book codes to ensure coding consistency. Then, disagreements were resolved through
discussion  and  consensus  by  three  members  who  came  from  different  disciplines  and  trajectories.  This  strategy  allows  a  cross
perspective on coding processes that result in research members’ triangulation (Sampieri et al., 2014). Consensus coding enabled the
research team to discuss differing interpretations, refine the application of the coding categories, and reach agreement on the final
coding decisions.

In this paper we present the results of data analysis after this re-codification of the qualitative data, taking into account data
gathered in interviews, focus groups, and open-answer questions of the student survey. Thus, the primary analytical focus of this paper
remains on the rich qualitative accounts of GenAI’s perceived impacts on HOTS.

The analysis has several limitations. First, the rapid pace of technological change and its applications in academia and higher
education limit the generalizability of findings from a single time point. Our study represents a snapshot of the University’s evolving
engagement  and  regulation  of  GenAI.  Second,  participants  constituted  a  convenience  sample  drawn  from  all  schools  within  the
institution; however, they do not represent all perspectives in the academic community. Third, comparisons across groups are limited
by the use of different data collection techniques: open-ended survey questions, focus groups, and interviews. To enable comparisons,
the codebook was applied consistently across all three corpora, and the analysis focused on identifying which categories appeared
more  or  less  frequently  within  each  group  and  on  contrasting  the  observed  patterns.  Finally,  the  combination  of  deductive  and
inductive analysis used during the recodification process introduces a potential confirmation bias among research team members.

3. Results

We found evidence related to all four HOTS as well as thinking skills in general (see Table 1). Our analysis of qualitative data across
all three participant groups (students, faculty, and academic administrators) revealed distinct patterns of concern regarding GenAI’s
impact on different types of thinking skills. Contrary to expectations, creative thinking was the skill most mentioned by participants
(31%), followed by general thinking skills (25%) and critical thinking (24%). Reasoning (11%) and metacognition (9%) were the least
mentioned. Perceptions from academic administrators, professors, and students varied, as detailed in Table 1.

3.1. Students

In the survey’s open questions, students were asked to identify the main advantages and disadvantages of GenAI tools. Of the 529
survey respondents, 347 (66%) provided responses to the open-ended questions, yielding rich qualitative data about their experiences
and concerns. Most of the identified disadvantages relate to HOTS. Their principal concern is related to creative thinking, with 50% of
codified entries mentioning this skill. They are far less concerned with the impact on critical thinking (25%) and thinking skills more
generally  (21%)  and  not  significantly  concerned  with  reasoning  (4%)  and  metacognition  (0%),  though  this  may  reflect  limited
awareness of these concepts rather than absence of concern.

Students have experienced firsthand how GenAI can help them become more efficient, completing tasks without the effort they
previously required. However, they see as an important limitation that GenAI hinders creative thinking, and they worry about losing
their ability to think creatively:

It’s a tool that’s too powerful. It allows you to improve efficiency and academic performance impressively. It’s a way to significantly reduce
the time spent on tasks. However, sometimes I feel that I stop thinking or being creative when using these tools. I would like to learn how not to
lose my faculties while optimizing my time. (Student, survey respondent S68)

This tension between efficiency and cognitive development emerged as a recurring theme in student responses. Negative impacts
on critical thinking were also frequently mentioned. Again, students mention the trade-off between efficiency and developing critical
thinking: “[GenAI tools] allow you to be more efficient with time, find quick solutions, however, they must be viewed critically, so they
don’t go from being helpful to doing the work alone” (Student, survey respondent S96). In fact, many consider that both creative and
critical thinking suffer when delegating multiple tasks to GenAI: “It reduces critical thinking ability and creativity since we settle for
the first thing that appears in the GenAI” (Student, survey respondent S96).

Regarding thinking skills more generally, students also consider that GenAI tools compromise their learning experience. GenAI
makes things easier: it allows them to delegate difficult tasks and comply with their academic work but at the cost of compromising
their learning goals. As one student recognizes: “There are tasks in which it no longer seems necessary to think or analyze, so I think I
have lost a certain analytical capacity or have stopped making an effort, which is why there are topics I don’t master, even though I got
good grades” (Student, survey respondent S18). This disconnect between grades and actual learning represents a significant concern
among students who are aware of GenAI’s limitations. More radically, another student only identifies negative consequences from the
use of GenAI:

It has no advantages, only disadvantages. It is environmentally irresponsible, contributes to mediocrity and anti-intellectualism. No one sits
down to read and understand a text or write an essay on their own anymore; they do everything with AI. This way no important skills for
academic life are developed. People who use AI are stupid, lazy, and mediocre. (Student, survey respondent S97)

While this represents an extreme position, it reflects broader anxieties about GenAI’s impact on intellectual development that

appeared more moderately throughout student responses.

Finally,  students  express  little  concern  with  respect  to  reasoning  skills  (only  one  student  mentioned  it)  and  no  concern  at  all
regarding metacognition. Although one could also argue that the exercise in itself was a metacognitive one, insofar as they had to
reflect on the impact of GenAI on their own learning, none mentioned this thinking skill explicitly in their responses.

3.2. Faculty

Faculty perspectives were gathered through twelve focus group sessions involving 65 full-time instructors from across the insti-
tution. Taking into account their teaching practices and their perceptions of student use, professors were asked to identify the role that
GenAI tools have had in higher education in general. Regarding their views on HOTS, in particular, creative thinking (29%) and
general cognitive skills (29%) were the most frequently mentioned, followed by critical thinking (22%). Metacognition (12%) and
reasoning (8%) were the least mentioned.

On the one hand, faculty members recognize that GenAI can be used to explore new images and as a trigger of new ideas, which can

enhance student learning through the use of tools that seek to develop their creative thinking:

I need to represent this in different ways, that’s an exercise we do in visualization, I have a data set and we have it visualized, I also want to
propose other visualizations where I want to highlight this category, for example, it comes out immediately, we have things that hadn’t even
occurred to me, like wow! this is good. So, it seems to me like a creativity trigger for us, also a powerful thing. I’m passionate about the topic, I
love it and I do very cool things with that. (Professor, focus group 6 participant D35)

Faculty participants working in fields such as engineering, design, and architecture were particularly enthusiastic about GenAI’s

potential to generate novel ideas and accelerate creative exploration.

On the other hand, professors are concerned with the repercussions of GenAI on the development of students’ cognitive skills:
This type of tool cannot be used in the initial cognitive developments of a student. Introducing this when a student is just beginning to write a
document is a terrible idea because we are truncating the proper development that a student should have. (Professor, focus group 4 participant
D25)

This  concern  about  developmental  appropriateness  was  particularly  pronounced  among  faculty  teaching  general  education
courses, who emphasized the importance of building foundational skills before introducing AI assistance. And, in particular, faculty
expressed concerns about the repercussions of GenAI on the development of critical thinking:

So,  let’s  say,  those  harmed  are  in  my  opinion,  those  harmed  are  all  those  who  use  artificial  intelligence  tools  unconsciously,  without
analyzing, without sufficient critical skills and well, and use artificial intelligence tools to… replace their knowledge, not to enrich it. (Professor,
focus group 12 participant D65)

Regarding metacognition, professors’ concerns were two-fold. First, they pointed to the ability to understand one’s own learning: “I
feel that the student should be able to learn how to learn with artificial intelligence, that is, it’s a very rich tool, it’s an impressive tool”
(Professor, focus group 10 participant D77). Second, they emphasized the importance of human “intervention” in the use of the tool,
which in turn leads to the process of understanding how the tool is used in the learning process. Several faculty members discussed
designing assignments that explicitly required students to reflect on their GenAI usage patterns and evaluate the tool’s contributions to
their learning. As an engineering professor states:

[…] spatial recognition skills, in logical inference, are absolutely flawed because the system isn’t trained to do that. And precisely to be able
to […] remedy those algorithmic limitations, there always has to be human intervention. Humans are the only ones with metacognitive capacity,

show concern with how an indiscriminate use can obstruct the learning process, they are also open to experimenting with these tools,
and to implementing pedagogical strategies for using them successfully in the classroom. Many faculty participants described actively
redesigning their courses and assessments in response to GenAI availability, suggesting a period of active pedagogical experimentation
and adaptation.

3.3. Academic administrators

Academic administrators’ perceptions on the use of Gen AI were gathered from nine individual interviews with department chairs,
academic deans, vice-chancellors, and the University president. As with the other actors, the information was coded based on the
identification of HOTS. Critical thinking (30%) was most mentioned, followed by reasoning (26%), and cognitive skills (18%). Creative
thinking (15%), and metacognition (11%) were the least mentioned. This pattern suggests that administrators, given their institutional
oversight responsibilities, tend to focus on skills they perceive as most foundational to academic integrity and student success.

During interviews, academic administrators expressed the most concern about critical thinking emphasizing that it is fundamental

in professional training for the 21st century and as part of achieving digital literacy:

Artificial intelligence is now generating everything at a much faster pace—the cycles of fake news, videos that imitate politicians, soon
professors. A whole series of false information. Therefore, education directed at young people will become increasingly critical, particularly
regarding critical thinking, but a critical thinking that takes into account that yes, in the media and the world of marketing, all of this is already
circulating and competing for their attention. And careful, key questions must be asked and training must occur—not so much of the machines,
but training of the new generations. Understanding how to coexist with machines in a much more strategic manner, right? What is truly at stake
is  human  consciousness,  yes,  because  very  soon one  could, pardon  the word,  also  colonize  the minds of  young  people  with all these  new
technologies. (Academic Administrator, interview I3)

This concern about information integrity and the erosion of shared epistemic standards appeared frequently in academic admin-

istrators interviews, often framed in terms of institutional responsibility to prepare students for a rapidly changing labor market.

Regarding reasoning skills, academic administrators expressed some concern about the possibility that AI will replace skills such as
logical reasoning. Particularly in areas of knowledge where this type of thinking is mostly used in numerical operations, administrators
worried about students losing fundamental analytical capacities. Some suggest putting the myth aside and focusing on developing
these skills during courses. Therefore, the design of activities in teaching practice should focus on strengthening and applying these
skills rather than transmitting information, so  that students  can demonstrate their reasoning. Moreover, academic administrators
showed concern regarding the teaching and learning of cognitive skills, in general, and how these may be impacted by GenAI. Different
tasks and processes that students must undertake were mentioned:

Yes, well, I think the main thing for me is that we have not had a deep, philosophical and pedagogical discussion about what the human brain
needs to develop critical thinking skills, discernment skills—that is, to what extent can you delegate those tasks that seem so basic to us to
artificial  intelligence?,  or  whether  it  is  necessary  for  growth  and  development,  especially  for  undergraduate  students,  the  development  of
cognitive skills, how much must be done by hand, how much must be done analogically? I think we have not had that conversation: learning to
read, learning to summarize, learning to describe what argument the other is trying to make, to synthesize, to group—there are a series of tasks
that belong to the human brain that occur in practice. (Academic Administrator, interview I4)

Other administrators echoed this call for deeper institutional reflection on cognitive development, noting that the emergence of
GenAI has forced fundamental questions about pedagogical purpose and method that the institution had not yet adequately addressed.
Although academic administrators mentioned creative thinking less frequently (15%), they showed some concern with authorship
and authenticity issues, especially in areas with an artistic component: “So, where is the creative element? If this thing can generate
things for you, what is your creative contribution in the creative fields? So, architects, designers, artists, musicians…”  (Academic
Administrator, interview I1). These concerns often extended to questions about how to evaluate student work in creative disciplines
when AI can generate sophisticated outputs.

Although metacognition was rarely mentioned explicitly, participants made references to reflective capacities when talking about
the basic skills needed for interacting with machines, which can be interpreted as metacognitive in nature. Academic administrators
recognize that GenAI is a powerful metacognitive tool that contributes to the learning process, allowing both teachers and students to
recognize this use and monitor it.

On the one hand, interviews with academic administrators reflect an interest in how to introduce necessary curricular changes to
incorporate the use of GenAI tools. On the other hand, there is also the concern that students will not develop sufficient cognitive skills
using GenAI. In this regard, most academic administrators recognize that these skills cannot be replaced by machines and that faculty
and students must be clear about this. In addition, they should rely on tools that facilitate their teaching practices, as well as the student
learning process. Several administrators discussed ongoing institutional initiatives to develop AI literacy programs and revised aca-
demic integrity policies, reflecting active institutional responses to the challenges posed by GenAI.

3.4. Comparison among actors

In general terms, we found that the HOTS most frequently mentioned among all participant groups was creative thinking, followed
by general cognitive skills and critical thinking. In contrast, metacognition was scarcely mentioned; mostly indirectly and in relation to
the possibilities of using GenAI for learning. From a comparative perspective among actors, we find the following relevant findings.
First, academic administrators were more concerned with reasoning than other actors (26% of codified entries compared to 8% among
faculty and 4% among students). They also showed more interest in GenAI’s impact on critical thinking than other actors (30% of
codified entries compared to 25% among students and 22% among faculty), although critical thinking was a significant concern across
the board. Second, students are considerably more concerned about the impact of GenAI on their creative thinking (50% of codified
entries compared to 29% among faculty and 15% among academic administrators), although the frequency of mentions among faculty
regarding this skill was also higher than expected. Third, faculty showed the most balanced distribution of concerns across different
HOTS, with creative thinking and general cognitive skills tied at 29%, suggesting a more holistic perspective on GenAI’s cognitive
impacts.

Bear in mind that these comparisons are very much limited by how these percentages are generated. While comparisons assume
that  there  is  full  agreement  among  coders  when  coding,  in  reality,  consensus  was  reached  through  discussion  among  coders,  a
technique normally used in qualitative research. However, we believe these comparisons are still useful since the comparisons serve
primarily a descriptive and exploratory function rather than a confirmatory one. Our goal was to identify patterns and relative dif-
ferences in how participant groups engaged with HOTS concepts, not to establish statistically precise distributions. The consensus
coding process, while negotiated, strengthened the reliability of identified themes across groups.

These divergent patterns of concern across actors suggest that different institutional positions shape how GenAI’s impacts are
perceived and prioritized. Students, experiencing GenAI’s effects most directly in their own creative work, focus heavily on creative
thinking. Administrators, responsible for institutional quality and program integrity, emphasize critical thinking and reasoning as
foundational to academic standards. Faculty, mediating between these perspectives while managing practical pedagogical challenges,
show more distributed concerns across all HOTS categories.

4. Discussion

The study’s multi-actor analysis reveals a complex and sometimes counterintuitive landscape of concerns regarding GenAI’s impact
on  HOTS  in  higher  education.  Three  major  themes  emerge  from our  findings  that warrant  careful  consideration:  the  unexpected
prominence  of  creative  thinking  concerns,  the  differential  engagement  with  reasoning  and  metacognition  across  actors,  and  the
broader implications for pedagogical practice in the age of AI.

4.1. Creative thinking: a shared but differentiated concern

From our initial exploration of the relevant literature, we were not expecting creative thinking to be such a pressing concern
regarding the use of GenAI tools among students and faculty. Existing research has predominantly focused on critical thinking as the
primary HOTS at risk from GenAI adoption (Fields, 2024; Pellas, 2025), yet our findings suggest that creative thinking deserves equal,
if not greater, attention, at least from the perspective of those most directly engaged in learning and teaching processes. Students in
particular seem to be especially sensitive to the negative impacts of GenAI in this respect, with half of their coded responses addressing
creative  thinking  concerns. This  is  consistent with  other  studies, showing  that creative  identity in  higher  education is  a  relevant
dimension of the learning process at this level (Dollinger et al., 2005) or finding a significant relationship between the promotion of
creative thinking and students’ self-perception and self-efficacy regarding their learning and thinking skills (Snyder et al., 2025).

Students’  worries center on a perceived erosion of creative capacity through dependency, manifesting as a decline in creative
ability. It develops when the effort required for original thinking is consistently outsourced to AI tools. Surprisingly, faculty members
seem more open to using GenAI to enhance and buttress their creative process. This divergence may reflect differential cognitive
abilities and stakes: students are still developing their creative capacities and identity as thinkers, while faculty members possess
established creative expertise that they can leverage AI to extend rather than replace. Faculty may also be more aware of GenAI’s
limitations  and  therefore  better  positioned  to  use  it  as  a  tool  rather  than  a  crutch  (Bellomo,  2023).  This  rather  unexpected  but
important finding of the study shows that there can be areas of student concern regarding the use of GenAI that are, however, more
positively seen by instructors, contrary to the overall trend showing overly concerned instructors and oblivious students when it comes
to the use of GenAI in learning processes (Huang et al., 2024).

4.2. Critical thinking: universal concern with varied emphasis

Another interesting finding relates to critical thinking. While the study is consistent with the generalized concern over critical
thinking that has emerged over the use of GenAI in higher education insofar as critical thinking is seen as crucial for digital literacy by
all actors, faculty members showed the least concern regarding this skill. Again, this is surprising given the initial assumption that
instructors are the most worried about the incursion of GenAI in higher education, while other actors are perceived as less critical and
more open (Guo et al., 2025). However, this pattern may reflect pragmatic adaptation rather than lack of concern. Faculty participants
frequently described actively redesigning assessments and pedagogical approaches to work with GenAI rather than against it. For
instance, using AI-generated content as material for critical analysis rather than attempting to prevent its use entirely. This suggests a
shift from alarm to strategic engagement that has not yet fully permeated student or academic administrator perspectives.

Academic administrators’ heightened concern with critical thinking (30% of coded entries) appears closely linked to institutional
apprehensions about information integrity, academic standards, and student preparedness for navigating an increasingly unpredict-
able job market. This institutional perspective, less visible in faculty and student responses, highlights how GenAI challenges not only
pedagogical practice but also the University’s role as gatekeeper of intellectual discernment.

4.3. Reasoning and metacognition: a curious absence

Discussion surrounding reasoning skills was less easy to identify during data analysis. This might be due to the fact that many
participants seem to take “critical thinking” to encompass all reasoning as well, not properly differentiating between these two HOTS.
Additionally, reasoning is also poorly differentiated from cognitive skills generally. This significant conceptual blurring suggests that
even among educated actors, the theoretical distinctions between different HOTS may have limited practical currency. If faculty,
administrators, and students do not clearly differentiate between critical thinking and reasoning in their everyday discourse, this raises
questions about whether taxonomies developed in the academic literature adequately capture how thinking skills are understood and
valued  in  actual  educational  practice.  Perhaps  due  to  these  limitations,  faculty  and  students  did  not  show  significant  concerns
regarding reasoning skills, as found in the study by Guo et al., (2025). Notice however that academic administrators did pay attention
to reasoning skills, e.g., logical reasoning and synthesizing, which might suggest closer familiarity with GenAI impacts or, alterna-
tively, greater exposure to institutional discussions about learning outcomes and curricular design where these distinctions are more
explicit.

Although the impacts on metacognition of GenAI use were less explicitly mentioned in the study, they were indirectly discussed in a
positive light by academic administrators and professors, both of whom emphasized the great potential of using GenAI as a tool for
personalized learning, thus making students more attuned and actively intentional to the particularities of their own learning process.
This finding aligns with recent studies suggesting that AI tutoring systems can improve self-regulated learning (Huang et al., 2024).
The  absence  of  explicit  metacognitive  discourse  among  students  is  particularly  notable.  Even  as  they  engaged  in  metacognitive
reflection by analyzing their own learning processes in the survey, they did not name it as such. In the end, however, whether GenAI
could potentially strengthen students’ reflective skills and become a tool for developing metacognition or not, is a question that our
study cannot fully answer. Perhaps the use of alternative cognitive skills frameworks (e.g., Hattie, 2009; Marzano, 2007) could be used
for better tackling this issue.

4.4. Toward a developmental framework for GenAI integration

Finally, the study found an overall concern with the potential impact on the development of cognitive skills, generally speaking,
stemming from the use of GenAI in higher education. The team decided to add an emergent code for general cognitive skills given that
many actors referred to them generally, without specifying whether they had in mind HOTS, LOTS or both. What emerges from our
data is an implicit developmental concern that cuts across all institutional actors: the question of when and how much AI assistance is
pedagogically appropriate. Faculty teaching general education courses emphasized the dangers of introducing GenAI “too early,”
before foundational  skills were established, while  administrators  worried  about the  long-term consequences  of  skipping essential
cognitive steps. This suggests the need for a developmental framework that considers not only which skills might be affected but at what
stage  of  cognitive  development  GenAI  intervention  is  beneficial  versus  harmful.  Furthermore,  the  use  of  HOTS  as  a  theoretical
framework may limit the scope of the findings and alternative frameworks, such as the Depth of Knowledge framework (Martin, 2016),
may capture and categorize additional dimensions of cognitive skills relevant to education research.

4.5. Implications for theory and practice

Our  findings  suggest  several  implications  for  both  theory  and  practice.  Theoretically,  the  study  highlights  the  limitations  of
applying psychological taxonomies of thinking skills directly to educational contexts without considering how these categories are
understood and operationalized by educational actors. The conceptual blurring we observed between critical thinking, reasoning, and
general cognitive skills suggests that stakeholders work with more holistic, practice-based understandings of thinking than academic
taxonomies capture.

In practice, the divergent concerns across actors point to the need for multi-level institutional responses. Students need explicit
guidance  on  using  GenAI  in  ways  that develop  rather  than replace  their  creative  and  critical  capacities.  They  need an  academic
environment that develops these abilities through pedagogical and investigative practices that deliberately require original thinking

rather than delegating it to AI tools. Faculty need support in redesigning their pedagogies, particularly in reconceiving the tools and
methods used to support and evaluate creative and critical processes, in assessing student dependency on GenAI, and in incorporating
pedagogical elements that respond to the changes GenAI is introducing in higher education. Administrators need frameworks for
policy development that prompt dialogue with other actors about the possibilities of formal curriculum reform that explicitly rec-
ognizes HOTS as essential developmental goals across all disciplinary programs, regardless of professional training. Most importantly,
all three groups need structured opportunities for dialogue about GenAI’s role in education; conversations that according to our data
are only beginning to occur. This applies to classroom practices and relationships, but also to the formal and curricular levels within
educational institutions.

The tension between efficiency and cognitive development that permeates our findings represents perhaps the central pedagogical
challenge of the GenAI era. As one student poignantly noted, the question is “how not to lose my faculties while optimizing my time”
(S68). Finding this balance will require ongoing experimentation, assessment, and adjustment at individual, pedagogical, and insti-
tutional levels.

5. Conclusions

This study examined the perceived influence of GenAI on HOTS in higher education through a multi-actor analysis encompassing
academic administrators, faculty, and students at Universidad de los Andes, Colombia. Our findings reveal a complex landscape of
concerns and opportunities that varies significantly across different educational actors and HOTS categories.

The research identified creative thinking as the predominant concern among participants, particularly students, who expressed
worry about losing their creative capacities while simultaneously benefiting from GenAI’s efficiency. While critical thinking remained
a significant worry especially among academic administrators (who linked it to digital literacy and AI-generated misinformation),
faculty  demonstrated  surprising  openness  to  adopting  GenAI  as  a  creative  assistant.  This  divergence  suggests  that  perceptions  of
GenAI’s impact on creative thinking are highly contextual and role-dependent, warranting further investigation into how different
actors conceptualize creativity itself and its relationship to AI assistance.

Our  analysis  also  revealed  important  gaps  in  how  different  HOTS  are  understood  and  discussed.  Reasoning  skills,  though
emphasized by academic administrators, received limited attention from faculty and students, possibly due to conflation with critical
thinking or general cognitive abilities. Metacognition, while rarely mentioned explicitly, emerged indirectly as a potential strength of
GenAI use, with administrators and faculty recognizing its promise for personalized learning and improved self-awareness of learning
processes. However, we also find here limitations of the HOTS framework, which could be overcome using other alternatives.

The study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, data collection occurred during the early phases of
widespread GenAI adoption in higher education (September 2024 to January 2025), meaning perceptions may evolve substantially as
familiarity with these tools deepens. Second, our analysis struggled to differentiate clearly between impacts on HOTS versus LOTS, as
participants often discussed cognitive skills in general terms without distinguishing between foundational and higher-order processes.
This limitation points to a broader conceptual challenge: the boundaries between different thinking skills remain fluid and contested,
even among educational professionals. Third, while we achieved representation across eleven of twelve academic Schools, our study
focused on a single institution in Latin America, limiting generalizability across diverse educational contexts, institutional types, and
cultural settings. In particular, our survey relied on a small, self-selected convenience sample. This introduces a potential selection
bias, as students who volunteered for the study may be those with a special interest in AI or who are uniquely motivated by their own
struggles with generative AI. Consequently, our findings may overrepresent the perceived negative impacts of AI on student work.
Fourth, related to the consensus coding limitation discussed earlier, the absence of formal inter-coder agreement measures constrains
the precision of comparative analyses across participant groups. Finally, the study captured perceptions rather than measuring actual
impacts on cognitive skill development, leaving open questions about whether actors’ concerns accurately reflect learning outcomes.
Future research should address these limitations through several complementary approaches. Studies tracking students’  actual
development of HOTS alongside their GenAI usage patterns would provide empirical grounding for the concerns expressed by our
participants. Comparative studies across institutions, educational systems, and cultural contexts would illuminate how institutional
policies, pedagogical traditions, and cultural values shape both GenAI adoption and its perceived impacts on thinking skills. Addi-
tionally, research differentiating between impacts on LOTS and HOTS would clarify whether GenAI primarily affects basic cognitive
processes  or  whether  it  directly  influences  complex  thinking  abilities.  Experimental  studies  examining  specific  pedagogical  in-
terventions designed to harness GenAI for HOTS development while mitigating potential harms would provide practical guidance for
educators navigating this transition.

As GenAI becomes increasingly integrated into higher education, understanding its multifaceted impacts on thinking skills remains
crucial. This study contributes to that understanding by revealing the complex, sometimes contradictory perceptions held by different
educational actors and by highlighting both the threats and opportunities GenAI presents for cognitive skill development in the 21st
century university.
