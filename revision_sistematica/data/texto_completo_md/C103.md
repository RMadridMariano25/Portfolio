Given the rapid advancements, and notable failures, in large language model generative AI (genAI), there are
elevated expectations that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) AI chatbots will revolutionize higher education
by  offering  individualized,  always-available  tutoring  based  on  validated  content.  However,  experimental  evi-
dence on their effectiveness remains scarce. Using a randomized controlled field experiment, this study examines
the effects of a genAI chatbot on key precursors to learning success (i.e., interest, self-efficacy, and engagement)
and academic achievement for ≈500 undergraduate students across two modalities (in-person and asynchronous
online). We completed a semester-long controlled experiment with pre- and post-treatment surveys and tests.
Despite  expectations,  we  found  the  genAI  chatbot  had  no  statistically  significant  impact  on  any  measured
outcome. These early results challenge assumptions about AI's instructional effectiveness and suggest universities
should  further  investigate  the  pedagogical  value  of  AI  chatbots  before  making  substantial  investments  or
committing to long-term contracts. We recommend future research to increase the generalizability of the findings
and to discover methods to improve efficacy of AI chatbots in higher education.

1. Introduction

For  decades,  digital  education  promised  personalized

lear-
ning—education  that  meets  each  student's  specific  needs  rather  than
targeting the broader population (Gibbons, 1970; D. W. Johnson et al.,
1978). Despite computers becoming increasingly powerful, smaller, and
now in many students' pockets, this dream always remained just beyond
reach.  However,  the  advent  of  large-language  models  (LLMs)  and
generative artificial intelligence (genAI1) renewed enthusiasm for indi-
vidualized,  accessible  digital  education.  Internationally,  the  potential
role of genAI is an important research topic, with scholars across various
educational contexts exploring its viability (Ding et al., 2023; Frankford
et al., 2024; Hauske & Bendel, 2024).

From a practical standpoint, universities must decide whether genAI
has matured enough to justify broad investment. GenAI chatbots incur
costs  to  develop,  requiring  expertise  from  the  discipline,  educational
design, and information technology, along with ongoing costs to oper-
ate. On the theoretical side, genAI might justify itself based solely on its

support for enhanced engagement and achievement—critical concerns
for universities seeking to have students graduate on time (De Boer &
Van  Rijnsoever,  2023).  A  fundamental  question  for  a  university  is,
therefore,  what  measurable  improvements  does  a  genAI  investment
provide?

Functionally, education is a service provided by an instructor to a
student, where the student's participation is essential for creating value.
Thus,  co-creation  requires  engagement  from  both  the  instructor  and
student; and without both participating no value is produced (Vargo &
Lusch, 2008). Co-creation is pivotal in all education but more precarious
in  higher  education  where  students  enjoy  greater  freedom  and  bear
increased individual responsibility for their learning. New technologies
like  genAI  can  foster  co-creation  through  student  empowerment  and
institutional  innovation  but  demand  careful  management  to  mitigate
risks  such  as  inconsistent  participation  and  quality  control  issues
(Zarandi et al., 2024).

2.1. Model of Domain Learning (MDL)

Understanding  how  humans  learn  has  been  fundamental  to  suc-
cessfully  facilitating  learning.  While  several  models  theorized  the
mechanisms at work in learning, a model that described the dimensions
of learning at different learning stages would facilitate the assessment of
an adaptive genAI experience. The Model of Domain Learning provided
such a theoretical backdrop (Alexander, 2003, 2004; Fives & Dinsmore,
2017).

The MDL (Fig. 1) described students using and building three com-
ponents as they progressed through three stages of learning. Across these
stages, students developed interest, knowledge, and  strategic process-
ing, each with two types. For interest, individual interest appeared when
a student  had an underlying affinity for the domain, while situational
interest arose when a student wanted something—often novel—from a
course  of  study  that  catches  students’  attention,  encouraging  engage-
ment  with  it.  Knowledge  has  been  decomposed  into  broad  domain
knowledge (e.g., knowing that marketing runs the gamut from product
to promotion to price) and more specific topic knowledge (e.g., that there
are  multiple  types  of  promotion).  Building  topic  knowledge  provided
context  and  allowed  the  student  to  better  conceptualize  the  domain.
Finally, learning strategies were employed either as surface-level actions
to address an impending task or as deep-level actions that brought forth
thought-driven connections in the domain.

surface  to  deep  learning.  Engagement  is  multi-dimensional,  and  the
MDL framework emphasizes cognitive and motivational engagement, in
addition to behavioral actions (Mosenthal, 1999; Skinner et al., 1990).

students to continue contributing substantial learning effort and build

reinforces ongoing learning effort and increasing knowledge (Hidi et al.,
2006). Self-efficacy, the belief that one possesses the ability to be suc-

and perception of continued success (Bandura, 1978; Bandura &  Wes-

2.2. Traditional human tutoring practices

Individualized teaching—matching instruction to a student's specific
needs—has  been  contrasted  with  uniform  curricula  presented  to  all
students (Landrum & McDuffie, 2010). Although often a luxury for the
well-to-do (Balwant & Doon, 2021), some higher education sectors (e.g.,
in  the  United  Kingdom,  and  supplemental  instruction  in  the  United
States)  have provided  individual tutors as a  central source of student
support. While this approach has been more common for students with
special  education  needs,  its  benefits  extended  to  the  broader  student
population  when  costs  could  be  reduced  (Bloom,  1984;  Rytivaara  &
Vehkakoski, 2015; Sneyers &  De Witte, 2018). Early efforts sought to
replicate these benefits through technology-produced intelligent tutor-
ing  systems  that  modeled  human  tutoring  dialogue,  including
question-asking, feedback, and scaffolding (Graesser et al., 2004), but,
comparatively, these systems were limited in scale and adaptability. The
emergence of LLM-based genAI chatbots represented the latest iteration
of this trajectory, raising expectations that the individualized benefits of
human tutoring could finally be delivered at scale.

2.3. Transition to AI chatbots: expectations versus established abilities

Coincidental  to  the  resource  pressures  faced  in  higher  education,
advances  in  genAI  have  been  look  upon  as  a  possible  alternative  for
individualized student learning. Recent surveys have shown AI use has
grown  in  many  colleges  and  classrooms,  especially  in  STEM-based
courses, with two-thirds of college instructors having said they use AI
for planning, content creation, or student assignments (Morrone, 2024;
Quizlet, Inc., 2024b). Faced with increasing class sizes and expectations
of improved graduation rates, faculty and universities have been seeking
solutions  to  improve  students’  learning  outcomes  (S.  R.  Johnson  &
Stage,  2018;  Wang  &  Calvano,  2022);  and  vendors  have  responded.
Outside  the  classroom,  AI  enhanced  study  tools  have  become  wide-
spread  with  AI  embedded,  for  example,  into  Kahn  Academy,  Quizlet,
and Studyable (Khan Academy, 2025; Quizlet, Inc., 2024a; Studyable,
2025)  and  incorporated into  offerings by  publishers, such  as those  at
Pearson and McGraw Hill (Pearson, 2024; Reed, 2024).

Given the millennia-long traditions of faculty-student discourse and
guidance, AI chatbot developers have faced daunting expectations. The
expectation has been that an AI chatbot would match a skilled human
tutor  in  proficiency  and  personality,  but  always  available,  scalable,

quick  to  respond,  competent,  transparent,  motivating,  emotionally
engaging, private, and equitable (Jin et al., 2025; Kestin et al., 2024;
Shao et al., 2025). However, despite impressive chatbot demonstrations,
genAI's notable limitations have often been overlooked. Because LLMs
are semantic, word- and concept-based models designed to return the
next best word, they have struggled with mathematics (Xu et al., 2024),
hallucinated—in cases fabricating information at rates well over 20%
(Chelli  et  al.,  2024),  provided  overly  general  responses  (B´echard  &
Ayala, 2024; M. Liu et al., 2025), often faltered when instructions were
lengthy or complex (N. F. Liu et al., 2024), and had little support for
privacy and equitable access (Jin et al., 2025).

Empirical  research  on  genAI  in  education  is  rapidly  emerging,
though findings are contradictory. Bastani et al. (2025) conducted an
RCT  with  approximately  1000  high  school  mathematics  students,
finding  unfettered access  to  GPT-4  significantly  harmed  learning out-
comes,  as  students  used  the  AI  as  a  ‘crutch’  rather  than  engaging  in
productive struggle; these negative effects were largely mitigated when
the  AI  included  pedagogical  guardrails.  Similarly,  Yang  et  al.  (2025)
conducted a quasi-experimental study with 153 high school program-
ming students and found integrating ChatGPT resulted in lower levels of
flow  experience,  self-efficacy,  and  learning  achievement  compared  to
conventional  instructional  methods.  Conversely,  Kestin  et  al.  (2025)
found  a  custom  AI  tutor  incorporating  structured  pedagogical  best
practices outperformed in-class active learning for college physics stu-
dents.  These  contradictory  findings  may  reflect  differences  in  imple-
mentation design, subject domain, or student population. Notably, our
literature  review  found  no  published  RCTs  examining  RAG-based  AI
chatbots  in  undergraduate  education  over  a  full  academic  term.  This
study  contributes  additional  empirical  evidence  to  this  nascent  but
important area of research.

From an educational researcher's perspective, questions have arisen
about what genAI might contribute to student learning. Both instructors
and  students  recognized  that  personal  attention  from  a  thoughtful
instructor has been key to overcoming learning barriers. Yet, increasing
class  sizes  and  the  prevalence  of  asynchronous  online  courses  have
reduced  the  availability  of  such  individualized  support  (Nieuwoudt,
2020; Ratan et al., 2022). While genAI has shown it can deliver correct

not consistently met these standards. Among the various approaches to
AI chatbots, two main types have emerged: 1) general chatbots and 2)
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) chatbots.2 Earlier models such as

of communicative AI, but OpenAI's ChatGPT 3.5—released in November

Modern chatbots are built on top of large language models (LLMs)
that  are  trained  to  predict  the  next  word  (“token,”  actually)  in  a
sequence  (OpenAI,  2024;  S´anchez  Cuadrado  et  al.,  2024).  As  these
models  have  grown  in  scale,  the  semantic  meaning  embedded  in  the
models produced surprising results with thoughtful, context-aware re-
sponses  that can appear  to  demonstrate  reasoning  (Wei  et  al.,  2022).
Whether or not LLMs truly “reason” is debatable (Mitchell et al., 2023),
but  their  capacity  for  generating  nuanced  and  contextual  output  has
been a desirable attribute in an AI chatbot.

Chatbots serve as intermediaries between the user and the LLM. In a
typical interaction (see left frame in Fig. 2), a student submits a question
the chatbot records as the “user prompt.” This prompt, along with any
previous conversation from the session, is forwarded to the LLM, with
the chatbot managing parameters such as response randomness (called
“temperature”) and response length. Once the chatbot determines the
LLM has generated a suitable reply, the chatbot formats and delivers the
reply back to the user often appended to a continuous text-message-like
thread showing the back-and-forth conversation. LLMs are pretrained on
vast, diverse datasets,3 making them ideal to generate responses based in
general  knowledge  and  reasoning.  This  makes  them  well-suited  for
handling  open-ended  queries,  summarizing,  categorizing,  and  more,
without  requiring  highly  specialized  knowledge.  Moreover,  the  avail-
ability of many validated general chatbots—such as ChatGPT or Gem-
ini—offers easy, low-cost access to robust responses across a wide range
of topics.

However,  there  are  disadvantages.  Aside  from  hallucinations  (i.e.,
where genAI heads down a random path fabricating a false response),
when academic terms differ from everyday use, a perfectly functional
general chatbot may provide information far enough from the desired
context as to be counterproductive. For example, ChatGPT 4o's defini-
tions  for  “need,”  “want,”  and  “value”  reflect  common  usage  and  are
incongruent  with  marketing  theory.  These  terms  are  foundational  to
understanding marketing theory, so it is important students learn the
correct academic definitions.

Addressing this limitation falls solely on the creator of the AI chatbot.
Students are, by nature of their status, ignorant of the correct content
and, without alternatives, may uncritically accept answers they cannot
evaluate.  Based  on our  observations, this  effect  results  in  a  ‘certainty
cliff,’  a  paradox  where  non-experts  confidently  accept  a  chatbot's
response,  only  to  have  a  sudden  revelation  (the  cliff),  where  their
learning effort is dashed due to their reliance on incorrect information.
This scenario could be potentially damaging to trust in, and reducing
reliance on, the AI chatbot because the user cannot know when they have
been given incorrect information (Tossell et al., 2024).

This  argues  strongly  for  faculty,  as  the  subject  matter  experts,  to
provide expert content to augment the chatbot's knowledge, integrating
it into the curriculum, and helping to ensure the validity, reliability, and
currency of the response (Holmes et al., 2019).

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) chatbots provide this very
opportunity. To avoid creating a certainty cliff, faculty-provided expert
content is used as the primary source to respond to students' queries. In
the “RAG-based Chatbot” frame of Fig. 2, the student asked a question
and, as before, the chatbot captured that question as the “user prompt.”
But the RAG-based chatbot used the question to retrieve the most rele-
vant expert content from that provided by the faculty expert and used it

In the first step, retrieval, the student's query is semantically matched
against pre-built “chunks” (short text passages of the of expert content)
stored  in  a  specialized  database.  Each  text  chunk  was  encoded  as  an
embedding  vector—a  large  numerical  array  capturing  its  mean-

relevant information. Chunking of the expert text and creating of the
vectors was done when the RAG chatbot is constructed.

In  the  second  step,  the  retrieved  expert  content  was  added  to  the
system message and previous discussion, creating a system prompt and
augmenting  the  general  knowledge  of  the  LLM.  Typically,  the  system
message included instructions to favor the expert content over that of
the general model.

Finally, generation occurred at the LLM and is no different than how
the LLM would normally be activated except it had been primed with
specific  expert  content  and  instructions  that  were  used  to  create  its
response. Note that the expert content and instructions do not alter or
“train” the model but direct the LLM toward the favored expert content.
AI chatbot advantages should lead to improved learning opportu-
nities  for  students.  While  the  MDL  describes  stages  learners  progress
through,  and  attributes  developed  to  support  students  as  they  move
through the stages, practical learning opportunities offered by AI chat-
bots should assist in building these attributes. These practical benefits
include personalization at scale, real-time feedback, 24/7 availability,
and low-stakes practice. We describe the creation of the AI chatbot in the
materials and methods section, presented below.

2.5. Learning experience and outcomes: Interest, self-efficacy,
engagement, and achievement

Given the theoretical elements of the MDL and the potential for a
RAG-based AI chatbot to improve students' progress along the path to-
ward proficiency, our formal research question asks: Controlling for the
covariates  of  gender,  age,  race,  employed  hours  worked,  and  course
delivery mode, what is the experimental difference for students’  indi-
vidual interest, self-efficacy, engagement, and course test performance
between the pre-treatment measurement point (T1) and post-treatment
measurement point (T2) across the control and treatment conditions?

Here  we  break  down  four  interrelated  constructs  in  this  learning
cycle,  interest,  self-efficacy,  engagement,  and  achievement  and  offer
individual hypotheses.

Individual interest—best described as the desire to re-engage with
an  object,  whether  a  book,  video,  acrobatics,  or  physics—has  been
central to continued engagement (Renninger &  Hidi, 2015). Although
interest  was long described dichotomously as either situational or in-
dividual,  educational  researchers  have  increasingly  employed  the
development  Four-Phase  model  of  interest  (Hidi  et  al.,  2023;  Hidi  &
Renninger, 2006; Renninger &  Hidi, 2011), which divided situational
interest into two phases and added emerging interest as a transition. The
interest phases were identified and started as stimulated (situational);
maintained (situational); emerging; and ended in individual interest.

This  model  portrayed  an  individual's  interest  as  beginning  with
fleeting situational interest, which, if continually reinforced as the stu-
dent moved from acclimation to proficiency in the MDL, developed into
more durable individual interest. At its most developed, individual in-
terest  became  a  source  of  motivation  that  students  carried  between
engagements  and  throughout  their  lives.  We  measured  individual  in-
terest  due  to  its  self-reinforcing  nature  and  contribution  to  long-term
learning and, as hypothesized, expected growth over the test period.

Self-efficacy  is  an  individual's  belief  they  have  the  ability  to  be
successful in a specific task or series of related tasks such as a course of
study  (Bandura,  2013;  Bandura  &  Wessels,  1997).  It  was  noted  as  a
critical  source  of  motivation  for  students  facing  challenging,  lengthy
learning tasks and as the resource they drew upon when they stumbled
and  failed  to meet  their achievement  expectations. Thus, self-efficacy
has  been  identified  as  an  important  outcome  of  engagement  in  any
course. Self-efficacy and interest have been strongly linked (Bandura &
Shunk, 1981; Fryer & Ainley, 2019). Causally, initial interest has been
suggested to drive engagement, creating an opportunity for students to
develop  self-efficacy,  which  in  turn  supported  engagement  and,  in  a
virtuous cycle, provided an opportunity for further interest development
(Renninger & Hidi, 2011).

H2. Controlling  for  gender,  age,  race,  employed  hours  worked,  and
class delivery mode, AI chatbot students have higher self-efficacy when
compared to control students at T2 as compared to T1.

Engagement was defined to include direct measurement of behav-
ioral  activity.  Directly  measured  behavioral  engagement  recognized
students’ efforts and devotion of time and energy to learning (Fredricks
et al., 2004; Kuh, 2003; Skinner et al., 1990). Engagement also rounded
out the virtuous cycle that included interest and self-efficacy.

Reciprocal growth between interest and engagement led to improved
self-efficacy  and  conversely  self-efficacy
improved  engagement
(Renninger et al., 2024; Renninger & Hidi, 2011). One major question
for  genAI  researchers  is  whether,  and  how,  genAI  contributs  to  this
reciprocal  cycle,  which  is  foundational  to  students’  learning  in  the
present  and  going  forward.  Early  counter-balanced  experimental
research  with  chatbot  partners  suggested  that  while  students  experi-
enced some initial interest compared to a human partner, this interest

was a novelty effect that did not last (Fryer et al., 2017, 2019).

Most studies exploring the relationship between the amount of self-
regulated  learning  activities  and  having  a  tutor  (human  or  virtual)
found  that  students  who  use  a  tutor  exhibited  higher  self-directed
engagement  (Duffy  &  Azevedo,  2015;  Fırat  &  Kuleli,  2024;  Kestin
et al., 2024).

H3. Controlling  for  gender,  age,  race,  employed  hours  worked,  and
class  delivery  mode,  AI  chatbot  students  have  higher  behavioral
engagement (eBook usage) when compared to control students at T2 as
compared to T1.

Achievement  is  described  as  the  natural  outcome  of  the  virtuous
cycle where interest leads to engagement, which enhances self-efficacy,
and ultimately supports higher achievement levels (Fryer et al., 2021).
For  universities,  at  least  one  measure  of  achievement  has  been  the
fundamental  question,  “do  students  graduate  on  time?”  Antecedent
measures for this outcome are numerous but logically include successful
completion of classes required for the degree, and class achievement is,
in turn, often based on high-stakes tests.

H4. Controlling  for  gender,  age,  race,  employed  hours  worked,  and
class delivery mode, AI chatbot students have higher standardized test
scores when compared to control students at T2 as compared to T1.

2.6. Covariates

Covariates  are  included  in  studies  to  control  for  potential  con-

independently of the intervention or main predictor. Including appro-

dependent  variable  (Hair  et  al.,  2018).  Recognizing  that  different
student  groups  may  have  varied  responses,  we  included  gender,  age,
race, average weekly hours of outside employment, and class delivery
mode  as  covariates  in  our  analysis.  Multiple  studies  have  either  used

3. Methodology

3.1. Participants

This study was performed at a state university in the southeastern
United  States  across  three  principles  of  marketing  classes,  one  asyn-
chronous online class of 207 students, one small face-to-face class of 43
students, and one large face-to-face class of 204 students. These counts
exclude  16  students  who  dropped  the  course  and  three  who  were
repeating this course so had previous exposure to the experiment. All 19
of  these  students  were  excluded  from  the  study's  results,  leaving  454
students  in  the  experiment,  231  in  the  control  group  and  223  in  the
treatment group.

Due  to  the  introductory  nature  of  the  course  and  the  fact  it  is  a
prerequisite for all business majors, enrollment tended to be primarily
sophomores and juniors, of young ages, and primarily from the business
college. The demographic profile (Table 1) of the students in this study
has  been  generally  comparable  to  what  is  seen  in  the  principles  of
marketing class across past Fall and Spring terms. In addition to these
demographics, 47% of students reported working at their employer less
than 40 h per week and 12% reported working 40 or more hours a week
with 211 students reporting working with a mean of 27.2 h per week
(10.6 SD).

3.2. Design and procedure

Broadly, our goal for this study was to investigate the potential for
genAI to create a new learning environment that would provide crucial
learning experiences and outcomes that support students engaging in,
and  persisting  in,  undergraduate  courses.  To  this  end,  this  study
experimentally tested the effects of integrating AI chatbots into a Prin-
ciples of Marketing course. Fig. 3 gives an overview of the experimental
design across the 16-week course.

3.2.1. Pilot studies

This  study  built  on  two  pilot  studies.  Our  first  study,  an  effort  to
integrate an AI chatbot into a marketing course (n ̃480), showed the
novelty  of  having  access  to  a  RAG  AI  chatbot,  though  cutting-edged,
months-old technology at the time, was insufficient to attract students
to use it on a regular basis (Huang et al., 2025). This mirrors the effect
noted by Fryer et al. (2017, 2019). Arguably, awarding points to use the
AI  chatbot  provides  an  external  motivator,  but  usage  rates  decline
without even low-stakes participation points. These and other findings
guided  our  second  pilot  study.  In  the  second  study  (Fryer  &  Thoeni,
2024),  treatment  students  were  awarded  participation  points  to  com-
plete “study sessions” with the AI chatbot at least once a week. Control
students received the same points for completing study sessions using
custom Quizlet flash cards. Although the second study was conducted
with a smaller sample (n ̃100), it provided additional insights leading to
questions on student engagement and study effort. Anecdotally, findings

suggested students might become overconfident if the AI chatbot was
too encouraging, potentially leading to reduced studying. In the current
study, we addressed this ‘excessive encouragement’  with improved AI
chatbot system instructions.  We also expanded our data  gathering on
engagement,  study  time,  student-reported  study  time,  relative  study
effort, and short weekly qualitative statements.

3.2.2. Current study

This  study  was  designed  as  a  randomized,  controlled  field  experi-
ment with two groups, a control and treatment groups, with measured
dependent  variables  and  a  manipulated  independent  variable  (the  AI
chatbot). The study was conducted over a 16-week fall semester—with
the treatment period spanning 12 calendar weeks (11 active weeks due
to the university's “fall break”). The student sample was comprised of
three  classes  of  a  Principles  of  Marketing  course,  all  with  the  same
instructor and content, which allowed direct control and comparison of
activities  and  outcomes  (e.g.,  test  scores).  Students  were  randomly
assigned within each class section to one of the two test groups, control
or treatment. Assignment at the class level helped ensure class size or
delivery format did not present an initial skewing of the test groups and
also  allowed  for  later  post-hoc  analysis  if,  for  example,  exploration
within the distance learning class was needed. Random assignment took
place after the first week of class (once drop/add was completed and the
final registration list known). That weekend, all students were offered a
small amount of extra-credit points (about 0.25% of course grade) for
completing the pre-survey, which gathered responses on AI familiarity,
demographic  data,  and  the  constructs  measured:  interest  and  self-
efficacy.

The  first  weeks  of  the  class  (leading  up  to  test  #1)  operated  as  a
normal class, identical for all students. To establish a baseline for test
score performance, all students had the same access to the full course
materials and completed the first test with no treatment difference be-
tween the control and treatment groups—that is, the AI chatbot was not
available  to  anyone  during  this  period.  Randomization  checks  were
done using results of test #1 at this point and no significant differences
were found between the control and treatment groups. One of the class's
features was free access to a custom set of flashcards hosted at Quizlet.
All  students  had  access  to  Quizlet  and  were  awarded  small-stakes
participation points (about 1% of course grade) for study sessions with
Quizlet.

The  course  and  textbook  (eBook)  were  divided  into  four  sections
with three or four chapters per section. For all students, each chapter
had assigned readings in the eBook, an accompanying lecture (live for
face-to-face  class  or  video  recorded  for  online  class),  a  Quizlet  (see
below)  for  the  chapter,  and  access  to  Cornell  note  templates  for  the
chapter's key concepts. At the end of each section of chapters, a proc-
tored test was given with 50 multiple-choice questions that have been
previously tested, are mixed across the chapters for that section, covered
broad and detailed topics, and designed to produce an average grade of
about 75%. The tests were machine scored by a third party.

The day after test #1, all students received an announcement in the
learning management system (LMS) notifying them of the group they
were in and gave instructions for the assignments they were to complete
for  the  rest  of  the  term.  The  control  group  continued  the  course  as
normal and was awarded low-stakes participation points for continued
use of Quizlet. The treatment group was instructed in how to access the

Before beginning the study, a proposal was prepared and submitted
to the university's internal review board (IRB). Because the study was

3.3. Materials and measures

3.3.1. AI chatbot creation and testing

As  described  above,  the  advantage  sought  by  using  a  RAG-based
chatbot as an AI chatbot required the creator(s) provide three key ele-
ments:  1)  expert  content,  2)  system  instructions,  which  provide  the
functions  to  the  AI  chatbot  and  3)  rigorous  testing  for  validity  and
reliability. Such content production and validation typically require a
subject-matter expert working with an instructional designer and/or IT
professional with RAG experience.

The first element, expert content, was provided by the first author of
this paper specifically for this experiment. The copyrighted content was
organized  into  one  document  per  chapter  and  contained  topics,  con-
cepts, glossary (similar to Quizlet definitions), learning objectives, and a
full transcription of each chapter's lecture. Notably, the content did not
contain any test questions or answers or any content directly from the
course's required textbook (which is copyrighted separately).

The second element included system instructions that guide the AI
chatbot when it is responding to the student's question, which included a
‘personality’  statement (see summary in Appendix 10.1), list of goals,
and functions the AI chatbot can perform. There were several functions
explicitly built into the system instructions. A quiz feature dynamically
generated  short  quizzes  (three  multiple  choice  and  one  open-ended
question)  on  any  topic,  chapter,  or  test  the  students  requested.  After
answering,  the  AI  chatbot  indicated  which  multiple-choice  questions
were correct and incorrect, provided a bit of context for each and also
assessed the open-ended question giving more context if the student did
not  do  well  on  the  question.  Students  were  instructed,  and  this  was
reinforced through announcements during the semester, to ask the AI
chatbot for explanations about questions they got wrong.

The  AI  chatbot  also  gave  summaries  of  topics,  engaged  in  discus-
sions, answered simulation (a separate activity in all the classes), mar-
keting, and general business questions. In addition, for students whose
native language was not English, the AI chatbot engaged the student in
their language of choice. At least one Korean student found this feature
helpful.

The content and instructions were created, they were implemented

into a system designed to work as a RAG chatbot, as described above.

For  this  research,  we implemented  the  AI  chatbot using  Microsoft
Copilot with ChatGPT 4o as the underlying model. We selected Copilot
because it was scalable on demand (for a cost), secured the copyrighted
content, and limited access to the AI chatbot to only those students in the
treatment group through university login credentials. We selected the
ChatGPT  4o  model  because,  at  the  time,  it  had  the  highest  accuracy
ratings  of  any  chatbot  application  Microsoft  supported  that  also

accepted user-supplied content.

After establishing the AI chatbot, we tested it for validity and reli-
ability  using  a  test  and  correct  method  commonly  used  with  neural
networks called reinforcement learning from human feedback. In this
process  we  used  a  set  of  more  difficult  questions  and  evaluated  the
system's response for validity (were the answers correct?) and reliability
(were  the  answers  consistent).  During  two  previous  pilots,  we  also
reviewed  nearly  500  student-AI  chatbot  conversations  for  valid  and
reliable responses. Early on, when the AI chatbot would answer incor-
rectly or inconsistently, we would diagnose the response to determine if
the underlying content was unclear (or possibly being lost in context due
to RAG chunking) or the system instructions were leading the AI chatbot
to incorrect or inconsistent behavior. We then provided feedback to the
system by adjusting the content or instructions and retested until we had
eliminated the problem. This process was done over several months and
took an estimated 100 hours of testing.

At the end of the development and testing process, we asked the AI
chatbot  to  provide  answers  to  each  of  the  questions  students  would
receive on the tests and the AI chatbot missed only one question out of
200.  In  other  words,  while  the  AI  chatbot  was  not  provided  the  test
questions or answers, it was capable of answering them correctly and
consistently.

Finally,  a  note  on  reliability.  Most  LLMs  have  a  feature  called
“temperature”  that  can  be  adjusted  by  a  developer.  LLMs  are,  funda-
mentally,  probabilistic  engines  and  the  randomness  of  responses  are
controlled, in  part, by  the temperature setting  (Davis et  al., 2024). A
temperature  of  0.0  will  give  the  most  deterministic  (and  consistent)
output.  But,  at  this  level,  the  responses  show  little  variation  and  can
become monotonous. Higher temperatures, say 1.2 or above, give more
creative responses but risk coherency and reliability. Generally recom-
mended practice is around 0.7, which we used to achieve the results we
sought. We will  also  note that, beyond the  scope  of this paper,  other
parameters also can affect the output including top-p (restricts possible
response tokens to smallest number that sums to the selected probabil-
ity), number of past turns (i.e., messages) to consider, frequency penalty
(reduces redundancy of a token) and presence penalty (reduces use of a
token). In the end, the probabilistic nature of LLMs can produce unde-
sirable results even after thorough reliability testing.

3.3.2. Measures

Below are the measures used for data collected in this study along
with the means of collection. All items are provided in the appendix.
Where noted, data were collected both pre-treatment (T1, second week,
after  drop/add  week)  and  post-treatment  (T2,  after  the  fourth  test).
These  data  were  gathered  using  online  surveys  and  students  were
awarded  low-stakes  extra  credit  (equivalent  to  0.25%  of  the  course
grade) for completing the surveys.

Individual interest was measured using a 5-item scale rated on a 6-
point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree, α =
0.874) adapted from Fryer et al. (2016) at the beginning of the term, T1,
and repeated at T2.

Self-efficacy was measured using a 4-item scale rated on a 6-point
Likert-type  scale  (1  = strongly  disagree  to  6  = strongly  agree,  α  =
0.868) adapted from Midgley et al. (2000) at the beginning of the term,
T1, and repeated at T2.

Engagement, as noted, was measured using two summary observed
behavioral measures of eBook usage provided by the publisher. Usage
data were timestamped and included pages read and time spent in the
eBook. We converted these data to a minutes-per-week measure for the
individual weeks prior to T1 and prior to T2 for each student.

Achievement was measured using two tests with the first test given
at about a month into the course before any student had access to the AI
chatbot (T1). The last test (fourth in the class) was given at the end of the
term (T2) and was non-comprehensive. All tests were 50 questions with
a  mixture  of  multiple-choice  and  true/false  questions.  The  questions
were  drawn  from  a  test  bank  of  about  1800  questions  that  had  been

categorized by chapter and importance and had scores based on over
500 previous students answering the questions. The tests were randomly
generated  to  provide  a  mix  across  all  chapters  covered,  topic  impor-
tance, and previous score by question with the aim of the average score
for  the  test  being  between  74%  and  77%.  All  students  in  this  study
received the same tests and were reused from pilot studies 1 and 2 where
they were validated to meet the expected score range.

Since the tests covered different material at T1 and T2 they were not
directly comparable even though their target mean is in the same range.
Therefore,  to compare  the results,  we standardized the  test scores (z-
score) by test and across all classes, which converted the data for each
test into a set with zero mean and unit standard deviation. This elimi-
nated bias due to scale differences (Hair et al., 2018).

Because the T1 and T2 achievement tests assessed different course
content (early vs. late chapters), raw scores may not be equivalent in
difficulty and are therefore not directly comparable across time periods.
To enable comparison, test scores were z-standardized within each time
period, with all students (control and treatment) standardized together
at  T1  and  again  at  T2.  While  this  approach  does  not  allow  absolute
comparisons of improvement, it allows us to assess each student's per-
formance  relative  to  their  peers  at  each  time  point.  The  comparison
across  T1  to  T2  thus  reflects  whether  a  student's  standing  relative  to
peers  improved  or  declined—and  critically,  whether  this  change
differed between treatment and control groups. Note that at T1, prior to
any treatment, both groups were pooled for standardization; the same
procedure was applied at T2.

Control variables class mode, gender, age, race, and average weekly
hours of outside employment were gathered from surveys of the students
and  included  in  regression  analysis  as  covariates.  The  university's
research department provided incoming GPA which was used to test a
post-hoc analysis. Instructor characteristics were not included since only
one instructor was involved.

4.2. Exploratory analyses

Beyond  the  primary  hypotheses,  we  examined  AI  chatbot  usage

patterns, covariate relationships, and student attitudes.

AI chatbot usage patterns. Students tended to use the AI chatbot
just  before  weekly  and  test  deadlines.  In  the  online  class,  where  all
deadlines were on a Sunday, 48% of logins occurred on Sunday. In the
face-to-face  class,  which  had  Sunday  deadlines  but  Wednesday  tests,
36% and 21% of logins occurred on those days, respectively. In terms of
total activity, students logged in an average of 0.89 times per week (SD
= 1.06), slightly less than the assigned one time per week, with a mean
of 0.83 (SD = 0.85) for online and 0.94 (SD = 1.21) for face-to-face.
While the system recorded logins, we were unable to accurately deter-
mine session durations since students had no requirement to log off. A
text complexity analysis of student-side AI chatbot conversations (Lu,
2010; Tan, 2024) found the average number of words for each session to
be  68  (SD  = 88.4).  This  low  number  reflects  students  asking  simple
questions  (e.g.,  “define  need”)  or  answering  multiple  choice  quizzes
from the AI chatbot (e.g., “1a, 2b, 3c”).

Covariate relationships. Age showed a significant relationship with
behavioral engagement: an additional 7.2 min of eBook engagement per
week per year of age, controlling for other demographic attributes and
course mode (p < 0.0001, Cohen's ƒ2 = 0.216, a medium effect). Course
delivery mode was a significant covariate with test score. At T2, the face-
to-face classes had a significantly lower standardized test score ((cid:0) 0.16,
n = 247) compared to the online class (0.19, n = 207, p = 0.0003). This
represents a 0.35 standard deviation gap between the classes using the
same test, while the gap at T1 (0.09) was not significant (p = 0.1958).
AI familiarity and student attitudes. Student-reported familiarity
with AI was low. In the pre-survey, 80% of students reported they never
or only sometimes use AI for general use and 76% reported they never or
only sometimes use AI for school. In a post-course survey, students who
used the AI chatbot were asked if they enjoyed using it (M = 80.4, SD =
21.9, on a 0 to 100 slider scale), if it helped them understand the course

5. Discussion

Overall,  there  were  no  statistically  significant  interaction  effects  that
would support the AI chatbot had any effect for any of the measured
outcomes: interest, self-efficacy, engagement, or test scores. Based  on
the MDL framework, we discuss all four measured outcomes below.

5.1. Interest

Based  on  the  MDL,  for  students  acclimatizing  in  a  new  learning
domain,  situational  interest  is  a  precursor  for  success.  Our  findings
indicate there was little difference between the control and treatment
groups, on average, across periods. The interaction effect was not sig-
nificant (p = 0.195, d = 0.050), and the descriptive differences between
groups were minimal, providing no evidence the AI chatbot influenced
interest.

5.2. Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy increased from T1 to T2 across all students. Since self-
efficacy  is  an  individual's  belief,  that they  have  the  ability  to  be suc-
cessful,  perhaps  simply  getting  through  the  course  is  evidence  to  the
student they do have the ability to be successful. However, based on the
interaction effects being insignificant, access to the AI chatbot did not
enhance this further.

5.3. Engagement

As  theorized  by  the  MDL,  interest,  self-efficacy,  and  engagement
have  virtuous,  interrelated  effects.  Since,  for  the  treatment  group,
measured  interest  was  essentially  unchanged,  one  would  expect
engagement  to  have  a  similar  response.  The  significant  main  effect
showing engagement fell from T1 to T2 may be an artifact of measuring
engagement at the very end of the course—when students are “ready to
be done”—or other phenomena, which we did not measure, could be at
work.

5.4. Achievement

The MDL shows how students gain expertise through the stages of
acclimation,  competence,  and  proficiency.  Implicit  in  a  stage  of
competence or proficiency is the measured ability to be competent or
proficient. We found no significant main effects or interaction effects,
suggesting  the  AI  chatbot  did  not  improve  interest,  self-efficacy,  or
engagement sufficiently to alter the achievement outcome.

5.5. Covariate and attitudinal findings

Studies with graduate students have found improvements using AI
chatbots (Baillifard et al., 2025; Fazlollahi et al., 2022). Graduate stu-
dents  had  higher  motivational  strategies,  for  example  interest

enhancement and goal orientation, that led to behavioral engagement
when compared to undergraduates (Yun &  Park, 2020). We therefore
expected a level of maturity to coincide with individuals’ motivation for
productive  engagement.  Our  own  finding  that  age  was  significantly
related to engagement supports this interpretation.

We have no explanation for the significant drop in test scores for the
face-to-face class relative to the online class at T2 but note again that the
interaction effect with the AI chatbot was insignificant.

The positive attitudes toward the AI chatbot provide some encour-
agement that students are interested and willing to use an AI chatbot.
However, it is important to note that students' satisfaction with an AI
chatbot does not equal students learning from the AI chatbot. Students'
own self-assessments—that the AI chatbot did not help them get better
grades or increase their interest in marketing—mirror the experimental
results. This observation suggests that for learning outcomes, satisfac-
tion with the AI chatbot may be a hygiene factor—not a motivator when
present (e.g., students expect it to work) but a demotivator if absent (e.
g., demotivates if it doesn't work) (Herzberg et al., 1959).

5.6. Reconciling expectations with findings

Having found no significant effects in all measures associated with
the AI chatbot, the results surprised us, not meeting our expectations.
Rapid advances in AI, favorable press, and early research reports within
the “AI” literature suggested hypotheses for significant effects would be
supported.

The  rapid  emergence  of  genAI  (ChatGPT  in  November  2022  and
RAG-based  systems  in  late  2023)  complicates  direct  comparison  with
prior research. Earlier studies on intelligent tutoring systems relied on
rule-based, keyword matching, or heuristics NLP systems that bear little
functional  resemblance  to  current  large  language  models  (Duffy  &
Azevedo, 2015; Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019).
Though  a  clear  taxonomy  for  GenAI  research  does  not  yet  exist
(Weidlich et al., 2025), we think it is fair to separate previous tutoring
systems from genAI systems and to treat pre-2023 findings as historical
context rather than directly comparable evidence. Foundational ques-
tions  about  how  genAI  affects  learning  may  need  to  be  re-asked  and
evidence reassessed.

In addition, much of the near recent research was small, controlled
studies  or  were  not  controlled  studies  (e.g.,  Betancourt  Ramirez  &
Fuentes Esparrell, 2024; Eltahir &  Babiker, 2024). A meta-analysis of
ChatGPT experiments (Deng et al., 2025) advises caution in interpreting
the  positive  results  due  to  methodological  limitations.  Finally,  some
studies used more mature subject pools (e.g., graduate or medical stu-
dents) more likely to productively engage meaningfully with a digital
tutor.  Weidlich  et  al.  (2025) identify  three  considerations  for  inter-
pretable  effects:  What  exactly  the  AI  treatment  involved,  what  the
control  group  did,  and  whether  outcome  measures  reflect  actual
learning. All this is to say, large RCT-based studies are needed to build
out a robust set of findings in the literature.

However,  reviewing  the  larger,  more  robust  post-2023  empirical
research on genAI in education, we find the pattern may be coalescing,
though  contradictions  still  exist.  Research  suggests  when  AI  provides
answers directly, learning outcomes suffer—even when students believe
the tool is helping them. Unfettered GPT-4 access harmed mathematics
learning in a study of over 1000 high school students, and those students
did not realize their learning had been harmed (Bastani et al., 2025).

Neuroimaging  research  found  similar  effects:  LLM-assisted  writing
reduced  brain  connectivity  by  up  to  55%  compared  to  unassisted
writing, and 83% of LLM users could not recall content from essays they
had just written (Kosmyna et al., 2025). ChatGPT integration led to lower
flow,  self-efficacy,  and  achievement  among  high  school  programming
students (Yang et al., 2025). An RCT with 120 undergraduates  found
that unrestricted ChatGPT use significantly reduced long-term knowl-
edge  retention  45  days  after  learning,  consistent  with  cognitive  off-
loading  reducing  the  effortful  processing  needed  for  durable  memory
(Barcaui, 2025).

However,  when  AI  scaffolds  rather  than  answers,  outcomes  may
improve.  Adding  pedagogical  guardrails,  by  providing  scaffolding
without  direct  answers,  mitigated  the  negative  effects  in  the  Bastani
et  al.  study,  consistent  with  the  principle  that  effortful  practice
strengthens  retention  and  transfer  even  when  it  feels  harder  in  the
moment  (Bjork  &  Bjork,  2011).  A  custom  AI  tutor  with  structured
pedagogy  showed  positive  effects  in  college  physics  over  a  shorter
duration (Kestin et al., 2025). Our null findings align with studies where
AI  functioned  as  an  answer-provider.  Our  AI  chatbot  lacked  session
memory  and  personalization,  and  students  could  ask  questions  and
receive answers without struggle.

These findings suggest a mechanism: AI tools that provide answers
may remove the effortful engagement that drives learning; AI tools that
scaffold without answering may preserve it. Within the MDL framework,
the cycle of interest, engagement, and self-efficacy requires students to
work through challenges, that is, no progress can be made toward pro-
ficiency without active effort. Self-efficacy can develop when learners
succeed at appropriately difficult tasks—operating at the edge of their
current competence. When an AI chatbot provides answers, it may short-
circuit this cycle. Students experience no productive struggle, gain no
sense of accomplishment, and receive no boost to self-efficacy or inter-
est. The student feels momentarily satisfied but the cognitive work that
produces learning did not occur. This fits our finding that students re-
ported  positive  attitudes  toward  the  AI  chatbot  while  showing  no
learning gains, and the finding that students did not recognize when AI
harmed their learning (Bastani et al., 2025). A related concern: When AI
systems hallucinate, naive students accept and build understanding on a
faulty  foundation.  At  some  point  they  discover  the  clay  feet  of  their
knowledge  and  collapse  when  confident  but  incorrect  knowledge  is
revealed.

Beyond the theoretical explanations above, several methodological
and design limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn from
this study.

6.3. AI chatbot limitations

The AI chatbot used in this experiment lacked session memory—it
started each session without content or knowledge from any prior ses-
sions; an artifact of the Copilot system being used. In practice, this meant
the AI chatbot was unaware of anything the student had communicated
in prior sessions, including their demonstrated knowledge level, areas of
difficulty, or personal interests. This is a fundamental limitation of many
chatbots when compared to a human tutor who builds a mental model of
each student over time, while the AI chatbot treated every interaction as
a first encounter. This significantly constrained the system's ability to
offer  personalized  instruction,  which  may  have  reduced  its  potential
effectiveness. Anecdotally, several students asked if the AI chatbot could
save previous conversations and, after discussion, the students were less
interested in going back to a conversation but desired the AI chatbot to
remember things the students had told it, for example interests or to pick
up a prior conversation.

Though this did not reduce response accuracy, better social presence
could have improved engagement and rapport with the AI chatbot (Kim
et  al.,  2022).  If  the  goal  is  to  have  the  AI  chatbot  feel  more  natural,
maintaining a knowledge of previous conversations could allow the AI
chatbot to engage the students in a more personal context, increasing
social presence. Since social presence is a mediating variable to credi-
bility, improving social presence would likely improve trust and possibly
use of the AI chatbot (Kim et al., 2022).

While  qualitative  data  was  collected  through  open-ended  survey
questions, response rates and depth of their content were insufficient to
support  meaningful  thematic  analysis.  Future  research  with  more
structured qualitative protocols may provide richer insight into student
perceptions.

6.4. Inability to capture study habits

Finally, during this experiment we were limited to the student's self-
reported study habits. There is evidence that having a tutor has a posi-
tive effect on self-study (Hong &  Park, 2012; Seo, 2018), but we had
anecdotal evidence of at least some students reducing their study habits.
However, we were unable to capture data sufficient to understand how
(or if) students were shifting study habits due to using the AI chatbot or
to measure effects of a shift if it was occurring.

6. Limitations

6.5. Inability to capture strategic processing

6.1. A single course and instructor across three class sections

Due  to  the  nature  of  the  present  field  experiment,  this  RCT  was
limited to three sections of the same course with only a single instructor,
greatly  limiting  generalizability.  It  is  possible,  for  example,  the
instructor was sufficiently experienced in generating interest with stu-
dents so that effects of the AI chatbot were minimized or that the mar-
keting content is incompatible with advantages that could be had with
an AI chatbot.

6.2. Subject matter

While  the  content  (principles  of  marketing)  can  be  challenging  in
breadth, it may be in a dimension less conducive to the helpful assis-
tance of an AI chatbot. The course contains theory and concepts but, like
any introductory course, it is heavy in vocabulary, as understanding the
language of the discipline is required to engage in deeper conceptual
learning. The control group had Quizlet flash card assignments, but both
groups had access to the flash cards, and the tests covered concepts and
vocabulary.  It  is  possible  the  AI  chatbot  may  not  have  offered

The full MDL addresses interest, knowledge, and strategic process-
ing. While we were able to measure two of these (interest and knowl-
edge—as  test  performance),  we  opted  to  not  collect  self-reported
strategic  processing  data  due  to  concerns  about  reliability.  While  an
alternative would be to directly measure strategic processing, given the
scale  of  our  experiment  and  the  time  required  per  student,  this  was
deemed impractical.

7. Future research

The pattern from recent research points toward design features that
may determine whether AI tools help or harm learning. Future research
should  examine  AI  systems  with  persistent  learner  models  that  track
progress across sessions, adaptive scaffolding that calibrates challenge
and  support  to  individual  learners,  and  pedagogical  approaches  that
maintain effortful engagement rather than eliminating it. Such systems
would push students at the edge of their competence while providing
graduated support—engaging the MDL's pathways rather than bypass-
ing them. Research should also investigate how to protect students from
the certainty cliff when AI hallucinations go undetected.

The  counterintuitive  findings  leave  us  with  many  questions  and
suggest a need for further research. Reflecting the limits of this study,
especially in generalizability, we would recommend replicating similar
studies with other subjects across a spectrum of courses and at various
levels.  This  could  address  the  possible  limits  of  content  types  (the
quantitative of calculus vs. the qualitative of poetry), depth (introduc-
tion  vs.  advanced),  and  instructor  styles  and  effectiveness.  We  also
suggest  studies  be  conducted  comparing  pedagogical  tutors  based  on
earlier NLP techniques or heuristics to LLM-based tutors. Since one of
the goals of a RAG-based tutor is to offer a higher level of reliability,
deterministic models may offer a better solution. How students respond
to an AI chatbot, not just in session but the study choices they make after
using AI chatbots, is an open question. Exploration of how the AI chatbot
engages students, e.g., merely quizzes them or expects to be taught by
the student in a Socratic method, could have effects on students’  self-
regulated  learning  and  help  understand  whether  the  AI  chatbot  is  a
complement or a substitute to other modes of self-study.

Constructing  and  testing  an  AI  chatbot  that  maintains  individual
knowledge of each student's then-current ability level, as well as per-
sonal  interests,  and  uses  that  information  to  provide  guidance  with
salient examples could improve engagement as well as achievement.

Finally, we see discussion related to AI literacy, but much of it seems
to  fit  into  the  realm  similar  to  media  literacy,  that  is,  how  does  one
correctly assess and consume the content provided by an AI source. This
view is covered by Lintner (2024) who suggests AI literacy includes 1)
an understanding of AI systems, 2) how to evaluate their output, and 3)
how to use them. This definition is effective for broad and varied use of
AI—recognizing  that  non-moderated  content  requires  thoughtful  un-
derstanding and evaluation.

However, as work proceeds to understand how to improve students'
literacy  with  AI  chatbots  (e.g.,  Casal-Otero  et  al.,  2023;  Tseng  et  al.,
2024), from a co-creation view, how social presence happens, and trust
is built (derived?), should be a burden placed on the AI chatbot, not the
student. If students are socially literate enough to engage with a human
tutor, a well-constructed AI chatbot should not require additional “lit-
eracy” to understand the system, evaluate the output, or use it. It appears
AI is not ready for this level of interaction; but research in this area could
focus,  for  example,  on  the  integration  of  past  conversations  with  the
student  with the  aim to  improve the  AI chatbot's understanding each
student's  personality  and  their  current  academic  limitations.  Here  we
recommend future research in the intentional design of AI chatbots to
reduce the literacy burden on students.

8. Conclusion

Overall, the major findings of this study are that the AI chatbot did
not statistically increase individual interest, self-efficacy, engagement,
or test performance for students across course modalities.

Our  original  goal  was  seeking  evidence  to  frame  and  manage  ex-
pectations around AI chatbots’ benefits for students and to the univer-
sity. The outcomes measured in this study (i.e., interest, self-efficacy,
engagement, and test performance) are important indicators of student
learning  and,  hence,  graduation  success;  and  increases  in  student
engagement  and  achievement  could  justify  the  resources  required  to
curate  expert  content,  develop,  and  deploy  AI  chatbots  across  many
courses.  However,  we  find  no  evidence  to  support  this  expectation.
Though  we  still  believe  AI  chatbots  can  be  useful  learning  tools  for
students, the combination of factors we were able to test, though robust,
did not produce evidence of beneficial outcomes. Our results, considered
alongside a growing body of controlled studies (Barcaui, 2025; Bastani
et al., 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025), suggest the critical
question  may  not  be  whether  to  deploy  genAI  but  how  to  design  it.
Within  the  MDL  framework,  the  cycle  of  interest,  engagement,  and
self-efficacy requires productive struggle; when an AI chatbot provides
answers without requiring effort, it may short-circuit the very processes
that produce learning.

Deploying AI chatbots requires commitment from faculty and incurs
labor and operating costs. To justify these costs, a commensurate benefit
must be produced. Since a key goal for most universities using genAI
would be to improve students’ success, defining the expected outcomes
and assessing whether the chatbot preserves effortful learning should be
considered before substantial investment or commitment is made. Value
is co-created between students and AI chatbots when the chatbot expects
cognitive work from the student, not replaces it. Therefore, universities
considering genAI investments should ask not only what outcomes they
expect, but whether their chatbot scaffolds productive struggle or sup-
plants it.
