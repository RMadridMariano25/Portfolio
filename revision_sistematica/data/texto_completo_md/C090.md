This  study  investigated  the  perceptions  and  use  of  Generative  Artificial  Intelligence  (GenAI)
among  387  social  sciences  students  within  an  English-Medium  Instruction  (EMI)  context  at  a
Turkish  university. Employing  a quantitative cross-sectional  survey design,  the research exam-
ined how students' GenAI-related characteristics (usage time, experience, self-perceived compe-
tence) and modes of GenAI integration (complementary, substitutive, or hybrid) correlated with
their academic performance, which was measured through grade point average (GPA) and En-
glish language proficiency.  Additionally, open-ended  responses regarding tool  usage  were sub-
jected to quantitative content analysis to identify prevalence trends. Key findings indicated that
language  proficiency  positively  correlated  with  EMI  academic  performance  and  negatively
correlated with substitutive GenAI use, which suggested that students with lower proficiency may
use  GenAI  to  compensate  for  linguistic  challenges.  Conversely,  complementary  GenAI  use  was
positively  associated  with  academic  performance,  which  highlighted  its  benefits.  Frequency
analysis revealed that students predominantly used ChatGPT for translation, proofreading, and
idea generation, and frequently used other GenAI assisted tools like Grammarly and Duolingo.
These results offer crucial insights into GenAI's integration within EMI learning, which inform the
design of effective GenAI-supported pedagogical practices.

1. Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is reshaping how students in higher education engage with learning, from studying and
academic skill development to coursework completion. However, as GenAI becomes increasingly embedded in students' academic
endeavours, understanding its influence on study habits, learning processes, and ethical academic behaviour is increasingly important.
Whilst research has indicated that GenAI can enhance academic performance and support deeper learning (Nguyen, 2025; Zhang et al.,
2024), it can also introduce risks, when it is used to substitute rather than complement independent learning (Kohen-Vacs et al., 2025;
O'Dea, 2024). Despite the global expansion of English medium instruction (EMI) programs, little is known about how students in
non-Anglophone contexts perceive and use GenAI. In such settings, GenAI may offer both unique opportunities and challenges for
students' academic development. Notably, there is a gap in literature regarding the interplay between GenAI use, language proficiency,
and academic performance in university EMI courses particularly in the social sciences.

The  Turkish  higher  education  context  presents  a  compelling  case  for  investigating  the  role  of  GenAI  in  EMI.  This  is  because,
although many universities offer programs taught in English, students' academic language proficiency often falls short of the level
required for meaningful engagement with complex course content (Kamas¸ak & Sahan, 2024). Traditionally, students have relied on
translation-based strategies to read materials in English. They translate them into Turkish for comprehension and then re-translate
their  ideas  into  English  for  academic  writing.  This  process,  whilst  resourceful,  is  cognitively  demanding  and  may  slow  down
deeper learning (Zhou et al., 2023). The increasing availability of GenAI tools introduces new dynamics, as students may use them to
support, replace, or bypass these traditional practices. This shift raises important questions about how GenAI is shaping students'
academic experiences. Therefore, understanding students’  patterns of use is essential for developing pedagogical and institutional
strategies that promote ethical and effective engagement with GenAI, which ensures it enhances rather than undermines independent
learning.

This study investigates students' perceptions and patterns of GenAI use at a Turkish university, which focuses on EMI within social
sciences programs. Data were collected from 387 undergraduate students via a purpose-designed questionnaire, which was validated
through Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA). The research explores the relationships between students’ language proficiency, GenAI
usage patterns, which are categorized as complementary, substitutive, or hybrid, and academic performance. The aim is to generate
insights into  how  GenAI can be  effectively integrated into  EMI  contexts to support  student  learning while maintaining  academic
integrity and promoting independent study.

To  guide  this investigation, we addressed  the following  research  questions  (RQs), where  academic performance  is questioned

through the grade point average (GPA) measurement.

RQ1:  To  what  extent  do  EMI  students'  GenAI-related  characteristics  (usage  time,  experience,  self-perceived  competence,  and
perceptions of GenAI as complementary, substitutive, or hybrid) correlate with their academic performance (GPA) and language
proficiency?
RQ2: How do language proficiency, GenAI usage time, GenAI experience, self-perceived GenAI competence, and perceived GenAI
integration influence EMI students' academic performance (GPA)?
RQ3: What are the perceived functions of ChatGPT in students' academic activities, and what other GenAI tools are incorporated
into their study routines?

2. Literature review

2.1. English medium instruction

The increasing adoption of EMI in higher education reflects broader trends in globalisation and internationalisation (Galloway
et al., 2020; Rose, McKinley, et al., 2020). EMI is commonly defined as “the use of the English language to teach academic subjects
(other  than English  itself)  in  countries or  jurisdictions  where  the  first language  of  the majority  of  the  population  is  not English”
(Macaro, 2018, p. 19). Whilst EMI offers the dual advantage of fostering both disciplinary knowledge and English language proficiency
(Yuksel  et  al.,  2023a),  it  also  imposes  considerable  linguistic  and  cognitive  demands  on  students.  In  EMI  contexts,  instruction  is
delivered in English despite it not being the dominant language of the study environment (Dearden &  Macaro, 2016). Byun et al.
(2011) argue that EMI serves as a key mechanism for internationalisation, which positions students to better compete in global ac-
ademic and professional arenas. However, the challenges are significant for students. This is because they are expected to read aca-
demic  texts,  follow  lectures,  and  produce  extended  written  work  in  English,  which  they  learned  as  a  foreign  language  and  in
environments where typically there is no additional language support embedded in the curriculum (Yıldız et al., 2017). Unsurprisingly,
a frequently cited challenge for EMI students is their insufficient language proficiency levels (Belhiah & Elhami, 2015), a concern that
we explore in the following section.

2.2. EMI academic success

Academic success within EMI contexts has emerged as a critical area for research inquiry, with recent reviews highlighting a range
of factors that contribute to student achievement (Feng et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2025). Among these, English language proficiency,
which encompasses both general and subject-specific skills is frequently identified as a key predictor of academic performance in EMI
settings (Curle et al., 2020; Rose, Curle, et al., 2020; Xie & Curle, 2022). However, empirical findings remain inconsistent. Whilst some
studies report strong positive correlations between English language proficiency and academic performance outcomes (Thompson
et al., 2022), others suggest that language proficiency alone may not reliably predict success (Athirah, 2024; Curle et al., 2020). This
divergence underscores the likely influence of additional cognitive and psychological variables in shaping learning outcomes within
EMI environments. Beyond linguistic competencies, a growing body of research has highlighted the significant role of non-linguistic
factors in shaping academic success within EMI contexts. Variables such as learner mindsets, self-regulation, self-efficacy, aptitude,
and motivation have been shown to have considerable influence on students’ academic outcomes (Yuksel et al., 2023b; Curle et al.,
2024; Lin & Lei, 2021; Thompson et al., 2022; Soruç et al., 2024). These findings underscore the complex interplay of cognitive and
psychological constructs in the EMI learning experience, which suggest that academic achievement cannot be attributed to language
proficiency alone.

Despite this expanding literature, the specific role of students' engagement with GenAI in EMI settings remains underexplored.
Little  is  known  about  how  students'  GenAI-related  characteristics,  which  include  usage  time,  prior  experience,  self-perceived

2.3. An overview of GenAI and its impact on students' study habits

GenAI has rapidly permeated various sectors of society, including education (Tlili et al., 2023). Powered by large language models
(LLMs), GenAI enables users to engage with content through the generation of human-like responses to prompts and questions (Kasneci
et  al.,  2023;  Thorne,  2024).  Among  the  most  prominent  examples  are  Generative  Pre-trained  Transformers  (GPTs),  such  as  the
American OpenAI's ChatGPT, launched in November 2022, and DeepSeek-V2, developed in China and released in May 2024. These
tools have received global attention; ChatGPT alone reached 100 million active users and approximately 6 billion monthly website
visits within its first few months of inception (Lungren et al., 2024). Due to its capacity to generate extensive and coherent text,
ChatGPT has gained widespread popularity in educational contexts. It is now used across all levels of education for tasks ranging from
homework assistance to academic essay writing (Zafar et al., 2024). GPTs have been described as transformative for education, which
can  enable  unique  approaches  to  teaching,  learning,  and  interaction  (Hrastinski,  2023;  Kasneci  et  al.,  2023;  Tlili  et  al.,  2023).
However, the benefits are accompanied by concerns regarding academic integrity and the potential for over-reliance on GenAI content
(Sullivan et al., 2023). In response to this technological shift, universities have begun to explore, adapt, and integrate GenAI into
curricula. The move has prompted fundamental changes to traditional educational practices, which includes the expansion of hybrid
and distance learning models (Vrågård et al., 2024).

GenAI has also introduced new dimensions of flexibility into students' study habits, which enable access to learning resources
anytime and anywhere via mobile and digital devices (Zafar et al., 2024). Its continuous availability supports asynchronous learning
and fosters increased motivation and engagement (Chiu, 2024). Chatbots offer personalized and immediate academic support that
deliver  explanations  and  feedback  with  speed  and  clarity  (Zhai  &  Wibowo,  2023),  which  have  been  shown  to  enhance  students'
learning experiences and willingness to participate in academic tasks (Boˇzi´c & Poola, 2023; Chen et al., 2023). Through reflecting on
the perceived benefits, Domínguez and Sandberg (2024) found that students advocated for GenAI technology integration into aca-
demic curricula because of its relevance for future workforce demands, economic development, and global competitiveness. However,
there are many concerns about the potential drawbacks of GenAI use. These include reduced learner autonomy, diminished peer
interaction, and weakened leadership skills, alongside anxieties about job displacement (Zafar et al., 2024). The widespread adoption
of GenAI tools has prompted educators and institutions to examine students’ usage patterns more closely, particularly in relation to
academic integrity. East and Donnelly (2012) define academic integrity as “being honest in academic work and taking responsibility,”
a principle that now requires reinterpretation because of GenAI-assisted practices.

In a recent study, Johnston et al. (2024) surveyed 2555 students at a UK university to inform student-centred revisions to academic
integrity policies. Their findings revealed that Grammarly and ChatGPT were the most widely recognized GenAI or GenAI-assisted
tools,  with  over  half  of  respondents  reporting  their  use  in  academic  contexts.  Students  generally  viewed  minor  GenAI-assisted
edits, which included grammar checks, conceptual clarification, support for disabilities, and assistance for second-language English
learners as acceptable practice but unacceptable to produce entire assignments. The findings, supported by Amigud and Lancaster
(2019), also indicated that students with lower confidence in academic writing were more likely to rely on GenAI technologies.

2.4. Students’ GenAI usage in EMI settings

The  increasing  integration  of  GenAI  in  EMI  contexts  presents  significant  opportunities  for  supporting  students  in  overcoming
linguistic challenges (Kikuchi, 2024). Research has demonstrated the value of GenAI in promoting independent language learning in
English for Specific Purposes (ESP) writing, and in enhancing the use of authentic materials in Business and interdisciplinary contexts
(Elmotri  et  al.,  2025a,  2025b).  However,  concerns  about  students'  over-reliance  on  GenAI,  potential  reductions  in  academic
engagement, and broader ethical implications remain under addressed (Khosravi et al., 2022). Despite GenAI's increased prevalence in
higher education, empirical research that examines its impact on academic success in EMI contexts, where language proficiency is a
critical factor, remains limited (Adewale et al., 2024; Bannister et al., 2023). Students' perceptions of GenAI play a pivotal role where
positive attitudes may encourage strategic and effective use, while skepticism could hinder adoption and exacerbate existing academic
challenges.

A critical step in understanding GenAI's educational impact involves examining how students use the tools. Recent studies (Chiu,
2024; Priyadarshini & Priyadarshini, 2025) have categorized GenAI usage into three patterns. These are firstly, complementary use,
where AI supports but does not replace student effort, for example with grammar checks or vocabulary assistance. Secondly, substitute
use, where GenAI replaces substantial amounts of student input by generating full essays or solving complex problems. Finally, hybrid

3. Methodology

3.1. Research design

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional survey design (Creswell, 2014) to investigate the relationship between EMI
students’  GenAI  usage  patterns,  language  proficiency,  and  academic  performance.  The  cross-sectional  approach  was  selected  to
capture a snapshot of students' engagement with emerging GenAI tools at a specific point in their academic trajectory. While the study
relies primarily on structured numerical data (Likert scales and demographic variables), it also incorporates open-ended survey items.
To maintain a consistent quantitative methodological coherence, the data from these open-ended items were handled using a quan-
titizing strategy (Sandelowski et al., 2009; Teddlie &  Tashakkori, 2009). This involves the transformation of textual data into nu-
merical frequency counts, allowing the researcher to statistically identify the most prevalent tools and functions within the sample.

3.2. Setting and participants

This study drew upon data collected during the 2024–2025 academic year. The EMI students were enrolled in social sciences
programs at a Turkish university which offered face-to-face courses to its students. The institution adopts a partial EMI model, as
defined by Macaro (2018), where at least 30 % of courses are delivered in English, while the remaining instruction (up to 70 %) is
conducted in  Turkish, the primary  language of education.  Prior to admission, students  are required to  demonstrate their  English
language proficiency either by passing an internally administered examination corresponding to the Cambridge Preliminary English
Test (PET), which is set at the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) B1 difficulty level (Cambridge ESOL,
2014), or by submitting qualifying scores from standardized assessments such as TOEFL, IELTS, or a nationally recognized equivalent.
The  sampling  frame  for  this  study  consisted  of  512  students  studying  in  three  programmes  in  the  social  sciences  disciplines
(Economics, International Relations, and Labour Economics) at the university. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling,
with professors encouraging their students to participate in the study by sharing a link to the online survey. No incentives, monetary or
otherwise, were offered for completion. The participant cohort was comprised of 387 undergraduate students from these programmes
who completed the survey, representing a response rate of 75.6 %. The participants were enrolled onto Economics (n = 122), In-
ternational Relations (n = 138), and Labour Economics (n = 127) programs. The sample included students in their second (n = 150),
third (n = 123), and fourth (n = 114) years of study. At the time of data collection, all participants had completed a minimum of four
semesters of their programs, which encompasses at least 20 content courses delivered in Turkish (approximately 80 credit hours) and a
minimum of eight courses taught in English (approximately 40 credit hours). All necessary legal and ethical approvals were granted by
the  Turkish  university  prior  to  the  commencement  of  the  study.  The  university  acted  as  the  gatekeeper  throughout  the  research
process. The study was classified as ethically low risk due to its voluntary nature. Informed consent was secured through a digital form
presented at the beginning of the online survey. This form detailed the study's purpose, its voluntary nature, and participants' right to
withdraw at any time. The survey was designed to be fully anonymous, collecting no personal identifying information (such as names,
student IDs, or IP addresses) that could link responses back to individual students. All collected data was stored on a secure server
accessible only to the primary research team. Demographic details of the participants are presented in Table 1.

3.3. Questionnaire design and data collection

The questionnaire was designed to examine students’ use of ChatGPT within EMI contexts, with particular attention to identifying
substitute, complementary, and hybrid patterns of use (see Chiu, 2024 for details). Its development was informed by preliminary
classroom  observations,  relevant  literature  on  GenAI  in  education,  and  informal  consultations  with  both  students  and  teachers.
Drawing on these sources, we created a questionnaire. The first section gathered demographic information from participants, which
asked for their age, gender, and academic program. This foundational data was essential for contextualizing the findings and exam-
ining potential correlations between student characteristics and their engagement with GenAI tools.

The second section focused on quantifying participants' general engagement with ChatGPT, which addressed the frequency and
duration of use. It asked for average weekly usage (in hours) and the total length of time participants had been using the tool (in
months). In addition, the participants were asked to self-assess their proficiency with ChatGPT on a scale from 1 to 10, which provided

asking such questions, we aimed to gain a broader understanding of how ChatGPT is situated. The fourth section focused on inves-

validity and reliability. First, the constructs of Complementary Use, Substitute Use, and Hybrid Use were defined based on an extensive

generated to reflect the theoretical definition of each of these three constructs. To establish content validity, this item pool was then
submitted to a panel of three expert researchers specializing in educational technology and EMI. The experts provided feedback on the
clarity, relevance, and coverage of the items, leading to the refinement and removal of several statements. Next, the revised ques-
tionnaire was pilot tested with a small group of 12 undergraduate students (who were not part of the final sample) to check for
comprehension, ambiguity, and potential response issues. Minor wording adjustments were made based on their feedback to finalize
the instrument.

The fourth section focused on investigating if students’ ChatGPT usage could be categorized as a Complementary Use, Substitute
Use, or Hybrid Use. Each category was defined and followed by a series of these validated statements, which the students had to
indicate their agreement with (see Appendix 1). The statements were thus designed to reflect the extent to which ChatGPT was used as
a replacement for traditional learning activities (substitute use), as a means of enhancing or extending learning (complementary use),
or  as  a  combination  of  both  (hybrid  use).  The  questionnaire  used  closed-ended  statements,  which  were  suitable  for  quantitative
analysis.

Participants' English language proficiency data were obtained from institutional records provided by the University's Registry. The
data were collected during matriculation which used an institutionally adapted version of the Cambridge Preliminary English Test
(PET) (Cambridge ESOL, 2014), which assesses reading, writing, speaking, and listening skills.

The reliability and validity of the adapted PET have been reported in articles published a series of studies in language testing: the
writing test (Shaw & Weir, 2007), the reading test (Khalifa & Weir, 2009), the speaking test (Taylor, 2011), and the listening test
(Geranpayeh & Taylor, 2013).

3.4. Data analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 (SPSSv26). Initially,
descriptive statistics were calculated to provide an overview of the participants and the distribution of key variables. These involved
firstly computing the means, standard deviations, and ranges for participants' EMI course grade point averages (GPA), which reports
academic performance. Then, computing English language proficiency scores, ChatGPT usage time (in hours per week), duration of
experience with the tool (in months) and participants self-perceived GenAI competence. Finally, the three categorized approaches to
ChatGPT use, which were complementary, substitute, and hybrid, were addressed. The descriptive insights aimed to gain a founda-
tional understanding of participants’ characteristics and their engagement with ChatGPT and other GenAI tools in academic contexts.
To investigate the relationships among the key variables, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. The analysis assessed the
bivariate  associations  between  EMI  course  academic  performance  (GPA),  language  proficiency  (GEP),  GenAI  usage  time,  GenAI
experience, self-perceived GenAI competence, and the perceived uses of ChatGPT as complementary, substitute, and hybrid uses. The
correlational findings provided initial insights into the direction and strength of associations among these variables. Subsequently, a
multiple linear regression analysis was performed to make the causal chain explicit. This model tested the extent to which a set of
predictor variables predicted the primary outcome variable, EMI course academic performance (GPA). The specific predictor variables
(independent variables) entered into the model were language proficiency (GEP), GenAI usage time, GenAI experience, self-perceived
GenAI competence, and the three perceived uses of ChatGPT (complementary, substitute, and hybrid). This regression analysis aimed
to identify significant predictors among the variables, and to quantify their collective explanatory power for variance observed in EMI
courses academic performance (GPA).

Finally,  to  address  the  third  research  question  regarding  the  functions  of  GenAI  tools,  the  open-ended  survey  responses  were
subjected to Quantitative Content Analysis (Neuendorf, 2016). This section investigates students' perceptions of ChatGPT's functions
and their broader use of GenAI and GenAI-assisted tools in academic contexts. To this end, we classified tools in two groups: GenAI
tools and GenAI-assisted tools to better reflect their functions and relate them with our overall classification of complementary or
substitute use of GenAI and GenAI-assisted tools.

3.5. Questionnaire validation

To enhance the reliability and construct validity of the researcher-developed ChatGPT Use Questionnaire, an Exploratory Factor
Analysis  (EFA)  was  conducted.  First,  all  24  instrument  items  underwent  an  EFA  using  oblique  (oblimin)  rotation,  based  on  the
assumption that the three underlying factors (complementary, substitute, and hybrid) may be correlated. Following the initial analysis,
two items (1 and 8) were removed from the Complementary Use subscale, and two items (4 and 6) were removed from the Hybrid Use
subscale due to low factor loadings or cross-loadings. All items within the Substitute Use subscale demonstrated strong loadings and
were retained without modification. The adequacy of the sample for factor analysis was confirmed by a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO)
measure of 0.851, which indicated sampling adequacy. Bartlett's Test of Sphericity was significant, χ2 (28) = 1104.77, p < .001, which

Using maximum likelihood extraction factor analysis, a factor loading threshold of 0.40, and Kaiser's criterion (eigenvalues >1),

and the Hybrid Use factor had an eigenvalue of 0.82, which contributed to 10.34 % of the variance. The results provided strong
empirical support for the three-factor structure of the questionnaire.

3.6. Quantitative content analysis

To address the third research question regarding the functions of GenAI tools, the open-ended survey responses were subjected to
Quantitative  Content  Analysis  (Neuendorf,  2016).  Following  a  systematic  quantitising  procedure,  raw  text  responses  were  first
reviewed to identify distinct semantic units, such as references to “translation” or “grammar check.” These units were subsequently
grouped into mutually exclusive categories and converted into numerical frequency counts and percentages. By transforming the
textual data into categorical variables and ranking them by prevalence, this approach ensured that the analysis remained aligned with
the study's overarching quantitative framework, offering empirical evidence of usage trends rather than interpretive phenomeno-
logical insights.

4. Findings

4.1. Descriptive statistics of the study sample

This section presents the descriptive statistics for the 387 EMI students participating in the study. The analysis includes EMI course
academic performance (GPA), language proficiency scores, GenAI usage time, GenAI experience, and self-perceived GenAI compe-
tence. The participants’ EMI course GPA ranged from 53 to 94, with a mean of 76.46 (SD = 8.60). Language Proficiency scores showed
a wider distribution, ranging from 30 to 90, with a mean of 69.07 (SD = 11.62). Regarding engagement with the technology, students
reported a weekly GenAI usage time ranging from 1 to 17 h (M = 7.82, SD = 3.80) and an experience level ranging from 2 to 19 months
(M = 8.50, SD = 3.38). Self-perceived GenAI competence was generally moderate to low, with scores ranging from 1 to 10 and a mean
of 3.78 (SD = 1.34).

In addition, the study categorized students' aims for using ChatGPT into three distinct modes: Complementary, Substitute, and
Hybrid. The descriptive analysis revealed that students primarily engaged in Complementary use (M = 2.13, SD = 0.58), followed by
Hybrid use (M = 1.56, SD = 0.62). Substitute use was the least reported mode of interaction (M = 1.29, SD = 0.74).

4.3. Predictors of EMI academic performance (RQ2)

To answer Research Question 2 regarding how language proficiency and GenAI-related variables influence academic performance,
a linear regression analysis was conducted. The model evaluated language proficiency (GEP), GenAI usage time, experience, self-
perceived competence, and the three modes of ChatGPT use (complementary, substitute, and hybrid) as predictors of EMI course
GPA. The regression model revealed that these selected predictors accounted for 5.5 % of the variance in EMI course GPA (R2 = 0.055,
adjusted R2 = 0.038). As presented in Table 4, Language Proficiency (β = 0.139, p = .007) and the complementary use of ChatGPT (β =
0.178, p < .001) emerged as significant positive predictors. These findings imply that higher language proficiency and the constructive
integration of GenAI as a support tool are associated with better academic outcomes.

In contrast, GenAI usage time (p = .719), experience (p = .277), self-perceived competence (p = .666), substitute use (p = .662),
and hybrid use (p = .973) were not significant predictors. These results suggest that it is not the amount of time spent or experience
with the tool that impacts grades, but rather the manner in which the tool is applied (specifically, complementary usage).

4.4. Functions of ChatGPT and GenAI tool integration (RQ3)

Research Question 3 investigated the perceived functions of ChatGPT in students’  academic activities and the incorporation of

other GenAI tools into study routines. The findings presented below are derived from self-reported usage patterns.

4.4.1. Perceived functions of ChatGPT

Students reported utilizing ChatGPT for a diverse array of academic tasks, demonstrating its versatility as a study aid (see Table 5).
The most frequently cited function was translating content (n = 211), highlighting the tool's role in overcoming linguistic barriers in
multicultural academic environments. This was followed closely by proofreading (n = 196) and generating ideas (n = 175). The high
frequency of these mentions suggests that students utilize ChatGPT both to refine their written output and to initiate the brainstorming
process.

Other significant uses included simplifying complex topics (n = 154) to enhance comprehension, preparing for exams (n = 132),
and  summarizing  content  (n  = 129).  A  subset  of  students  utilized  the  tool  to  write  sections  of  assignments  (n  = 117),  improve
communication skills (n = 113), or polish ideas (n = 98). While less frequent, instances of using ChatGPT to learn about specific study
topics (n = 138) or non-study topics (n = 86) were also recorded. Notably, while paraphrasing (n = 87) and writing whole assignments
(n = 79) were mentioned, they were less common than complementary functions. A small minority (n = 23) reported not using the
tool.  Overall,  the  data  illustrates  that  students  perceive  ChatGPT  primarily  as  a  multi-functional  support  system  for  translation,
refinement, and comprehension, rather than solely as a content generator.

5. Discussion

This study investigated the interplay between social sciences students' English language proficiency, GenAI-related characteristics,
and their patterns of GenAI use within an EMI context at a Turkish university. Drawing on data from 387 participants, the findings offer
critical  conceptual  insights  into  the  mechanism  of  GenAI  integration  in  higher  education.  Consistent  with  prior  research  in  EMI
contexts (Curle et al., 2020; Rose, Curle, et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2022), English language proficiency emerged as a significant
positive predictor of students’ EMI course academic performance (GPA). The finding reinforces the importance of linguistic compe-
tence for academic success in settings where students are required to engage with complex disciplinary content in a non-native lan-
guage. It is consistent with the existing literature that highlights the cognitive demands imposed by EMI environments (Yuksel et al.,
2023b; Belhiah & Elhami, 2015) and underscores the continued need for robust language support mechanisms. However, the primary
contribution of this study lies in its empirical validation of the Complementary vs. Substitute distinction as a determinant of academic
success. While previous discourse has often treated GenAI usage as a monolithic variable (focusing on frequency or duration), our
regression analysis reveals that time spent and experience are statistically irrelevant compared to the mode of engagement.

Notably, the complementary use of ChatGPT, where GenAI is employed to support rather than replace student effort was positively
associated with higher EMI course academic performance. This offers a significant conceptual shift for EMI pedagogy: it suggests that
GenAI competence should not be viewed merely as ‘cheating’  or ‘outsourcing,’  but as a form of mediated agency where the tool
functions  as  a  legitimate  cognitive  scaffold.  The  finding  is  in  line  with  broader  perspectives  in  educational  GenAI  research  that
emphasise  the  pedagogical  value  of  GenAI  as  a  scaffold  for  learning  (Kasneci  et  al.,  2023;  Kikuchi,  2024),  but  extends  them  by
quantifying the academic dividend of this scaffolding effect.

Conversely, the substitute use of ChatGPT, where students rely on GenAI to perform tasks on their behalf, such as drafting sub-
stantial portions of assignments was negatively correlated with language proficiency. This finding exposes a critical “proficiency trap”
within the EMI context. The pattern may suggest that students with lower linguistic confidence are more inclined to use GenAI as a
compensatory tool and therefore bypass opportunities for authentic language practice and skill development. Conceptually, this in-
dicates that without targeted intervention, GenAI risks widening the gap between proficient and non-proficient students: the former
use it to enhance high-level cognition (complementary), while the latter use it to mask linguistic deficits (substitute). The findings
resonate with concerns regarding over-reliance (Khosravi et al., 2022; Sullivan et al., 2023). Interestingly, the absence of a direct
negative association with academic performance could imply that while short-term academic outputs are maintained, deeper learning
processes may be compromised. This highlights the need for further longitudinal research to examine the long-term implications of
substitute GenAI use on student learning and development.

AI-related tools usage and their functions further clarify the functional role of GenAI in this specific educational ecology. Most
notably, translation, proofreading, and idea generation emerged as the predominant uses. This suggests that for EMI students, ChatGPT
acts less as a “content generator” and more as a “translingual mediator”, helping them navigate the cognitive load of studying complex
social sciences material in a foreign language. This reflects a technological evolution of traditional translanguaging strategies (Webb
et al., 2025; Atas¸, 2023), where the interlocutor is now an algorithm rather than a peer. In addition, the widespread use of other GenAI
or GenAI-assisted tools, such as Grammarly, Duolingo, and DeepL emphasises how students construct technological support that is
tailored to their academic needs. These patterns resonate with Johnston et al. (2024), who reported a high uptake of Grammarly and
GenAI-based translation tools among students seeking linguistic assistance. The diversity of tools reported suggests that students are
not merely dependent on a single platform but are actively curating a suite of GenAI resources to support various aspects of their
academic work.

A further, critical dimension influencing these usage patterns, which this study did not measure, is the material context of equity,
access, and affordability. The diverse suite of GenAI or GenAI-assisted tools students reported—such as Grammarly, DeepL, and the
more powerful subscription tiers of models like ChatGPT or Notion AI—are not universally accessible. The prevalence of freemium
models means that students with greater financial resources can access more advanced, reliable, and nuanced GenAI support. This
access could directly impact the findings; for instance, the beneficial “complementary use”  we identified may be more effectively
facilitated  by  premium  tools  that  offer  deeper  analysis  and  better  suggestions.  Conversely,  students  from  lower  socioeconomic
backgrounds, or those limited by device constraints, may be restricted to free, less capable versions. This emerging “GenAI divide” risks
exacerbating existing educational inequalities within the EMI context, potentially limiting the academic benefits of GenAI to those who
can afford them and denying the most effective technological scaffolds to the students who may need the most linguistic support.

Despite the valuable insights produced by this study, the regression model accounted for only a modest proportion of the variance
in  students  EMI  course  academic  performance.  This  suggests  that  while  language  proficiency  and  complementary  GenAI  use  are
significant contributors, a substantial portion of academic success in EMI contexts is likely to depend on additional, unexamined
factors. These may include cognitive, affective, and socio-cultural dimensions such as learner motivation, academic self-regulation,
and classroom pedagogical practices (Yuksel et al., 2023b). Moreover, self-reported measures of GenAI competence and experience
did  not  emerge  as  significant  predictors  of  academic  performance.  This  raises  important  questions  about  the  nature  of  students’
engagement  with  GenAI  specifically  with  the  depth  versus  the  breadth  of  students'  GenAI  engagement  and  their  metacognitive
awareness of its educational value.

6. Conclusion

Our findings contribute to a limited but expanding body of literature on GenAI use in EMI higher education by moving beyond
descriptive usage statistics to establish a link between interactional modes and academic outcomes. The study provides empirical
evidence that the binary view of GenAI as either a “threat to integrity” or a “productivity tool” is insufficient. Instead, it proposes a
continuum where “Complementary Use” acts as a valid predictor of success, while “Substitute Use” serves as a marker for linguistic
vulnerability. These insights generate significant implications for policy and practice.

First, institutional bans on GenAI are likely counterproductive, as they fail to leverage the proven benefits of complementary use.
Second, EMI pedagogy must pivot from detecting AI text to teaching AI literacy—specifically, training students how to transition from
substitute usage (outsourcing) to complementary usage (scaffolding). Finally, as GenAI continues to transform the landscape of higher
education, this study highlights both the opportunities and challenges it presents within EMI contexts. Supporting students to use
GenAI tools judiciously, leveraging their benefits for language development and disciplinary learning while mitigating risks of over-
reliance, will be essential to maximise educational outcomes in an increasingly GenAI-mediated academic world.

7. Limitations and recommendations

Despite its contributions, this study is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First,

9

the research employed a cross-sectional design, which captured students' GenAI usage patterns, language proficiency, and academic
performance at a single point in time. To address this limitation, future research would benefit from longitudinal designs that track
students’ engagement with GenAI, language development, and academic outcomes across multiple semesters.

The study is also limited by its reliance on self-reported measures to assess variables such as GenAI usage time, self-perceived GenAI
competence,  and  perceived  patterns  of  complementary,  substitute,  and  hybrid  GenAI  use,  which  is  inherently  subject  to  biases.
Moreover,  we  did  not  differentiate  between  student  use  of  free  versus  paid  versions  of  GenAI  tools.  However,  the  significantly
enhanced capabilities of premium subscriptions (e.g., ChatGPT-4 versus the free 3.5 model) may correlate with different outcomes, and
access to these paid tools is often linked to socio-economic factors that could independently influence academic performance.

Furthermore, the study was conducted within a specific institutional and disciplinary context, which was with social sciences
students enrolled in EMI programs at a Turkish university. This contextual specificity may limit the generalisability of the findings to
other academic disciplines or educational systems with different linguistic demands, technological infrastructures and cultural atti-
tudes toward GenAI.

A final key limitation concerns the explanatory power of our statistical model. The multiple regression analysis yielded a modest R2
value of 0.055, which indicates that the selected variables (language proficiency, GenAI usage patterns, and perceptions) collectively
explain only 5.5 % of the variance in EMI academic performance. We interpret this not as a deficiency, but as a significant finding,
which underscores that the vast majority of variance is driven by factors not captured in this study.

Future research should consider longitudinal designs to trace the evolving impact of GenAI integration on both language devel-
opment and disciplinary learning outcomes over time. The incorporation of direct measures ensures triangulation with self-reported
data. We also explicitly call for research that extends this work into contexts such as full EMI models, specific science, technology,
mathematics, and engineering (STEM) disciplines, and a wider range of other countries and linguistic backgrounds given the growing
global prominence of GenAI use (Domínguez & Sandberg, 2024; Hrastinski, 2023).
