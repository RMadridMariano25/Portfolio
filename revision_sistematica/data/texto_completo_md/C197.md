Generative AI tools are rapidly becoming embedded in students' everyday learning, yet students' AI use varies
from  constructive  support  to  compulsive  dependence.  Drawing  on  Self-Determination  Theory  and  the  Hook
model,  this  study  proposes  a  dual-pathway  framework  examining  how  retrospective  reports  of  high-school
learning climate relate to university students’  GenAI-use patterns and perceived AI-supported learning quality
through motivational and behavioral relations. Using cross-sectional survey data from university students with
different high-school backgrounds and current university contexts (overall N = 508; China-HS group n = 305 and
non-China-HS  group  n  = 203;  China-Uni  n  = 193  and  Thailand-Uni  n  = 315),  we  tested  the  model  using
structural  equation  modeling  and  multi-group  analyses.  Results  were  consistent  with  a  pressure-compulsive
pathway: high-school pressure was associated with higher controlled motivation, which was in turn associated
with higher compulsive AI use, lower well-being, and lower perceived AI-supported learning quality. In parallel,
results  supported  an  autonomy-adaptive  pathway:  high-school  autonomy  support  and  current  university  au-
tonomy support were associated with higher autonomous motivation and planned trigger readiness, which were
associated  with  more  adaptive  AI  use,  higher  perceived  academic  performance  and  well-being,  and  higher
perceived AI-supported learning quality. The model explained substantial variance in key endogenous outcomes.
Scalar-level measurement invariance supported meaningful cross-group comparisons, and multi-group analyses
suggested that the overall pattern of associations was broadly similar across the sampled high-school and uni-
versity groupings, with differences concentrated in specific links. Overall, the findings suggest that motivational
climate may be an important correlate of whether GenAI is incorporated into learning in more adaptive versus
more reliance-oriented ways within the China, Thailand-linked contexts examined in this study.

1. Introduction

1.1. Broad background

Students today are undergoing an unprecedented transformation in
the way they learn, as generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) rapidly
reshapes  educational  experiences.  Tools  like  ChatGPT  reached  100
million users within two months of launch, signaling both massive in-
terest and widespread adoption (Dwivedi et al., 2023). For many stu-
dents,  such  tools  provide  empowering  support,  24/7  access  to
explanations, feedback, and learning personalization. Yet for others, AI
can become a source of anxiety or dependency, especially when used to
cope  with  academic  pressure  or  cognitive  overload  (Zawacki-Richter
et  al.,  2019).  At  the  same  time,  research  on  technology-supported

1.2. Theoretical grounding

To understand how prior learning environments are associated with
students’  present-day  AI  behaviors,  we  draw  on  two  complementary
theoretical  frameworks:  Self-Determination  Theory  (SDT)  and  habit-
formation models from persuasive technology research.

SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2020) posits that human motivation varies along
a continuum from autonomous motivation, where actions are driven by
intrinsic  interest  or  personal  values,  to  controlled  motivation,  where
behavior  is  regulated  by  external  pressures  such  as  rewards,  punish-
ments,  or  guilt.  The theory  emphasizes  the  importance  of  three  basic
psychological  needs:  autonomy,  competence,  and  relatedness.  When
these  needs  are  satisfied  through  autonomy-supportive  environments
such as choice-rich classrooms or supportive teacher feedback, students
are  more  likely  to  engage  deeply  and  persist  in  learning.  Conversely,
controlling  learning  environments, such  as  those marked  by  rigid  de-
mands,  surveillance,  or  fear  of  failure,  are  often  associated  with
compliance without engagement, or worse, anxiety and disengagement.
Empirical  studies  have  confirmed  these  patterns.  For  example,
Johansen et al. (2023) found that perceived teacher autonomy support
positively  predicted  university  students’  autonomous  motivation  and
academic engagement, while autonomy thwarting (i.e., pressure) was
associated with higher controlled motivation and lower vitality. Simi-

motivational patterns. One widely cited model is the Hook model, which
outlines  a  four-stage  cycle:  trigger  →  action  →  reward  →  investment
(Eyal,  2014;  Lukyanchikova  et  al.,  2023).  This  model  was  initially
developed  to  explain  how  digital  platforms  build  habits,  and  has
recently  been  applied  to  education  and  persuasive  learning  environ-

interact with AI tools in planned, reflective ways (e.g., asking for clari-
fication or iterating writing), while students with controlled motivation
may respond impulsively to stress-triggered cues, using AI to avoid effort
or mask anxiety. In this sense, motivation functions as a psychological
filter, shaping not only whether students use AI, but how they use it, and
whether the outcomes are adaptive or compulsive.

Finally,  there  remains  a  limited  cross-cultural  evidence  base  for
evaluating  whether  these  motivational  pathways  operate  similarly
across  educational  systems.  Although  some  studies  from  East  and
Southeast Asian contexts suggest that pressure-intensive and autonomy-
supportive learning climates may matter for students’ AI engagement, it
remains  unclear  whether  comparable  pathway  structures  would  be
observed  in  Western  higher-education  settings  or  other  institutional
environments. Accordingly, the present study should be understood as
testing  a  theory-informed  model  in  China,  Thailand-linked  contexts
rather  than  as  validating  a  universally  generalizable  psychological
mechanism.

Recent  empirical  research  from  Western  and  English-speaking
higher-education settings also suggests that the opportunities and risks
of student GenAI use are not unique to China, Thailand-linked contexts.
In the UK, survey evidence indicates that many students have already
used  or  considered  using  generative  AI  for  academic  purposes,  while
levels of acceptance differ across tasks, with more support for assistive
uses than for using ChatGPT to produce a whole essay (Johnston et al.,
2024).  Another  UK  study  likewise  identified  grammar  correction  and
idea  generation  as  common  uses,  alongside  recurring  concerns  about
plagiarism,  privacy,  and  unclear  institutional  guidance  (Arowosegbe
et  al., 2024). In  Australia, Gruenhagen et  al.  (2024) found  that more
than a third of surveyed university students had used a chatbot to assist
with  assessments  and  did  not  necessarily  regard  such  use  as  an
academic-integrity  breach.  Qualitative  evidence  from  the  UK  further
suggests that students may value ChatGPT for immediacy, reassurance,
efficiency, and creativity, while also expressing concerns about equity,
peer misuse, and weak policy transparency (Holland & Ciachir, 2025).
Taken together, these studies indicate that the benefit-risk tension sur-
rounding  GenAI-supported  learning  is  visible  beyond  the  specific
regional  setting  examined  in  this  study.  However,  they  do  not  yet
establish  that  the  same  constructs,  measurements,  or  mechanisms
operate equivalently across contexts.

display  strong  and  sometimes  unedited  traces  of  generative  AI  use.
Typical markers include template-like phrasing, stylistic discontinuities,
residual formatting such as double asterisks, excessive dash sequences,
or other artifacts that suggest direct pasting of AI outputs with minimal
revision. While these observations do not imply that AI use is inherently
problematic, they do raise a concrete pedagogical concern: under what
motivational and contextual conditions does generative AI function as a
constructive  scaffold  for  learning,  and  when  does  it  drift  toward  an
unreflective  shortcut  that  may  hinder  students’  engagement,  confi-

address the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1. To what extent are High-School Pressure (HSP), High-School
Autonomy Support (HSAS), and Current University Autonomy Sup-

Controlled Motivation (CM), and Planned Trigger Readiness (PTR)
for AI-supported learning?
RQ2.  Are  AM  and  PTR  associated  with  greater  adaptive  AI  use
(ADP), and is CM associated with greater compulsive AI use (COP)?
RQ3.  Do  ADP  and  COP  differentially  relate  to  behavioral  func-
tioning, such that ADP is associated with higher perceived academic
performance (APerf) and subjective well-being (WB), whereas COP is
associated with lower WB, and are APerf and WB also associated with
perceived AI-supported learning quality (AIQ)?
RQ4. Are these structural paths invariant across high-school back-
ground groups (China-HS group vs non-China-HS group), and where
do key path magnitudes differ?
RQ5 (secondary). After controlling for high-school background, do
the CM → COP path and the total indirect effect of CAS → AM → PTR

1.4. Contribution

Theoretically, this study extends Self-Determination Theory by using
it to interpret how students' formative motivational experiences may be
associated with their current technology-mediated learning behaviors.
While prior  SDT research has emphasized classroom engagement and
academic  outcomes,  the  present  model  examines  how  these  motiva-
tional  foundations  may  be  linked  to  students’  interaction  with  AI,
particularly whether their engagement is more consistent with adaptive,
goal-directed strategies or with compulsive, trigger-driven overuse. The
integration  of  SDT  with  the  Hook  behavior  model  also  offers a  novel
conceptual  lens  for  examining  the  intersection  of  psychological  moti-
vation and habit-formation processes in digital education contexts.

Methodologically, the study applies dual serial mediation modeling
using  SEM  to  examine  a  theoretically  specified  chain  from  distal
educational predictors (e.g., high-school climate) to AI-related behav-
ioral  and  perceived  learning  outcomes.  By  conducting  multi-group
comparisons  across  students  from  China  and  Thailand,  the  study  re-
sponds to calls for more culturally contextualized EdTech research and
evaluates whether the proposed pattern of associations is similar across
the sampled educational groupings.

Practically, the findings offer preliminary implications for educators,
curriculum designers, and AI platform developers. The results suggest
that autonomy-supportive educational environments may be associated
with more self-regulated and effective AI learning habits, indicating the
potential  value  of  emphasizing  choice,  competence,  and  rationale
throughout earlier stages of education, not only at the university level.
In  parallel,  platform  designers  may  consider  features  that  support
intentional,  low-friction,  and  goal-aligned  use  rather  than  more
compulsive  forms  of  engagement.  In  an  era  where  AI  is  becoming
increasingly embedded in students’  academic lives, understanding the
motivational conditions associated with healthier forms of AI engage-
ment is both timely and important. Within the China, Thailand-linked
contexts examined here, this study offers an integrative and culturally
contextualized  framework  for  understanding  adaptive  and  reliance-
oriented AI use in education.

2. Method

2.1. Participants and procedure

The sample comprised 508 undergraduate students who participated
between March and May 2025. Participants completed an anonymous

online questionnaire about their high school and current university ex-
periences in their own time (outside scheduled class sessions). Recruit-
ment  was  conducted  through  announcements  posted  in  university
classes  and  learning  management  systems,  and  participation  was
entirely voluntary.

Students were categorized into two sets of comparison groups: (a)
high school country, contrasting those who had attended high school in
China (China-HS group, n = 305) with those whose high school was in
another country (non-China-HS group, n = 203), and (b) current uni-
versity location, contrasting students enrolled at universities in China
(China-Uni  group,  n  = 193)  and  those  enrolled  at  a  university  in
Thailand (Thailand-Uni group, n = 315). Alongside the focal constructs,
participants  reported  limited  non-identifying  background  information
(e.g.,  major  area,  study  year/level,  living  on  campus,  weekly  study
hours, AI-use frequency and experience, and response language), as well
as the country of their high school and current university, which were
used to define the multi-group subsamples. To reduce identifiability risk
and  keep  the  questionnaire  concise,  we  did  not  collect  sensitive  de-
mographics such as age and gender.

All  procedures  complied  with  international  ethical  standards  for
human  subjects  research  and  the  2013  revision  of  the  Declaration  of
Helsinki  (World  Medical  Association,  2013).  The  study  received  an
ethics exemption determination from the institutional ethics committee
at Raffles International College Bangkok (ID: RICB-ETH-2025-019) and
was  classified  as  exempt,  minimal-risk  anonymous  survey  research
under  the  institution's  regulations  for  research  involving  human  par-
ticipants. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. At the start of the
survey, respondents read an online information statement and provided
informed  consent before  proceeding.  The  questionnaire  took approxi-
mately 15 to 20 min to complete.

2.2.2. Current University Autonomy support (CAS)

Perceived autonomy support in the current university context was
measured with four items adapted from the LCQ to reflect autonomy-
supportive  instruction  at  the  course  level  (Williams  &  Deci,  1996).
Items asked students to rate the extent to which their courses and in-
structors provided meaningful options and flexibility this semester (e.g.,
“My courses/instructors provide choices in tasks or learning paths this
semester”; “I am able to adjust pacing or deadline windows when needed
this semester”). The Current University Autonomy Support (CAS) scale
showed excellent reliability (α = .967).

2.2.3. Motivation (autonomous and controlled)

Drawing on self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan &
Deci, 2000), motivation for studying was operationalized as two com-
posite constructs. Autonomous Motivation (AM) combined intrinsic and
identified regulation items that reflected learning for interest and per-
sonal  value  (e.g.,  “I  study  because  I  am  genuinely  interested  in  the
material this semester”; “Studying feels meaningful and valuable to me
this semester”). Controlled Motivation (CM) combined introjected and
external regulation items that reflected pressure, guilt, or external de-
mands (e.g., “I would feel guilty or anxious if I didn't study hard this
semester”; “I often feel compelled by external demands or evaluations
this  semester”).  Items  were  adapted  from  validated  SDT-based  scales
(Ryan & Deci, 2000; Williams & Deci, 1996) and contextualized for the
current semester. Internal consistency was satisfactory (α = .953 for AM;
α = .942 for CM).

2.2.4. AI learning pathways (PTR, ADP, COP)

Three constructs captured students’ typical approaches to AI-assisted
learning, informed by research on habit formation and the Hook model
of  user  engagement  (Lukyanchikova  et  al.,  2023).  Planned  Trigger
Readiness  (PTR)  assessed  the  extent  to  which  students  deliberately
planned  when  and  how  to  use  AI  tools  for  study  (e.g.,  “At  set  time-
s/places, I generally start studying/using AI this semester”; “I usually
plan steps before I start this semester”). Adaptive AI Use Pathway (ADP)
measured  constructive,  reflective  engagement  with  AI  in  ways  that
deepen understanding and support self-regulation (e.g., “This semester, I
use AI to plan steps and decide what I should do next”; “This semester, I
check or compare AI results before using them in my work”). Compul-
sive AI Use Pathway (COP) assessed a tendency to rely on AI for quick,
minimally  checked answers  and  final  wording (e.g.,  “This semester,  I
tend to paste questions into AI to get a ready-to-use answer as quickly as
possible”; “This semester, when AI gives a plausible answer, I often move
on with minimal checking”; “This semester, I rely on AI to produce final
wording with only minor edits”). Each construct was measured with four
items.  Internal  consistency  was  high  for  all  three  scales  (all  α  values
above 0.90).

2.2.5. Academic performance (APerf)

Perceived academic performance was measured with four self-report
items developed for this study, informed by prior self-description work
on academic functioning in late adolescence (Marsh & O’Neill, 1984).
Items  asked  students  to  evaluate  how  effectively  they  managed  their
coursework this semester (e.g., “This semester, I usually complete my
coursework  on  time”;  “This  semester,  I  keep  up  with  most  academic
requirements in my classes”; “This semester, the quality of my academic
work  generally  meets  instructors'  expectations”;  “This  semester,  I
manage my studies effectively overall”). Responses were given on the
same seven-point scale, and reliability was high (α = .954).

2.2.6. Subjective well-being (WB)

Subjective  well-being  this  semester  was  assessed  with  four  items
developed  for  this  study,  focusing  on  students’  feelings  of  energy,
study–life  balance,  sleep/rest,  and  workable  stress  management  (e.g.,
“This semester, I generally feel positive and energetic”; “This semester, I
maintain a workable balance between study and life”; “This semester, I
can manage study-related stress in a workable way”). Items were rated
on the same seven-point scale, and scores were averaged, with higher
scores indicating higher subjective well-being. The scale showed excel-
lent internal consistency (α = .951).

2.2.7. Perceived AI-supported learning quality (AIQ)

Perceived AI-supported learning quality (AIQ) was operationalized
as a self-reported indicator of students' evaluations of the quality of AI-
supported learning in the current semester, rather than as an objective
measure of learning gains. In this study, AIQ refers to students’ subjec-
tive evaluations of the perceived quality and usefulness of AI-supported
learning in the current semester, rather than to objective learning gains
or externally verified academic performance.

The  four  items  were  developed  for  this  study  to  capture  whether
students perceived that GenAI use improved the depth, reliability, and
efficiency  of  their  learning  (e.g.,  “This  semester,  using  AI  helps  me
deepen my understanding”; “This semester, AI outputs are useable and
can  be  organized  into  reliable  study  materials”;  “This  semester,  after
checking, I am generally confident in AI outputs' accuracy/consistency”;
“This  semester,  AI  helps  me  work  more  efficiently  and  with  more
assurance”).  Given  the  cross-sectional,  anonymous,  multi-institution
survey design, a perceived outcome measure was used as a pragmatic
indicator of students’ learning-related evaluations of GenAI use, while
acknowledging that it does not substitute for direct performance-based
assessment.  Responses  were  made  on  the  same  seven-point  scale.  In-
ternal consistency for the AIQ scale was excellent (α = .959).

2.2.8. Sampling and recruitment

Data were collected via a cross-sectional online survey administered
to  undergraduate  students  in  China  and  Thailand.  We  used  a  conve-
nience  and  volunteer  sampling  strategy,  with  targeted  recruitment  in
both  countries  to  capture  variation  in  high-school  background  and
current  university  context.  Participation  was  voluntary  and  uncom-
pensated, and all responses were completed outside of scheduled class
time.

On  the  Chinese  side,  the  survey  link  was  distributed  through  two
main  channels:  (a)  a  recruitment  post  on  the  social-media  platform
Rednote and (b) internal communication channels at five universities in
Jiangsu  Province  that  preferred  not  to  be  named.  These  institutions
included both public and private universities (4 public, 1 private). In all
cases, instructors shared the  survey link with their students, but data
collection took place outside class sessions and was not tied to course
grades or attendance.

On the Thailand side, participants were recruited from four univer-
sities.  Two  private  institutions  consented  to  be  named  in  this  article,
Siam University and Raffles International College Bangkok, whereas two
public  universities  requested  anonymity.  As  in  China,  instructors
circulated the survey link to their students, who could decide whether to
participate in their own time. This procedure yielded a diverse univer-
sity sample spanning business and related majors in both Chinese and
Thai higher education.

To enhance data quality, the final block of the questionnaire (Block
9: Quality Control and Debrief) contained two attention-check items. In
the first item, participants were instructed to select the option “Agree”
on a standard 7-point Likert scale to confirm that they were reading the
questions carefully. In the second item, they were asked to select the
number  “5”  from  a  1-to-7  numeric  response  scale.  Participants  who
failed  at  least  one  of  these  attention  checks  were  excluded  from  the
analytical sample (33 respondents), resulting in a final sample of 508
students for the SEM analyses.

This study used a cross-sectional survey with retrospective reports of
high-school experiences; therefore, the SEM paths should be interpreted
as theoretically motivated associations rather than evidence of temporal
or causal effects.

2.2.9. Data analysis plan

All  analyses  were  conducted  in  SPSS  29  and  AMOS  29.  Data
screening confirmed that item-level skewness and kurtosis values were
within acceptable ranges (absolute skewness less than 2 and absolute
kurtosis less than 7). Missing data were below 5 percent on all items, and
Little's  MCAR  test  indicated  that  missingness  was  random  (p  greater
than 0.05). Given the sample size (N = 508) and model complexity, the
data  set  was  adequate  for  structural  equation  modeling  (Kline,  2016;
Little, 1988).

Following  the  two-step  approach  to  structural  equation  modeling
(Anderson & Gerbing, 1988), analyses proceeded in several stages. First,
descriptive statistics, Cronbach alpha values, and Pearson correlations
were inspected to evaluate data quality and initial relationships among
constructs.

Second,  a  series  of  confirmatory  factor  analyses  (CFA)  tested  the
hypothesized measurement model for all latent constructs. Items were
specified to load on their intended factors only, and residuals were not
allowed to correlate unless there was a strong theoretical justification.
Standardized factor loadings were expected to exceed 0.50. Convergent
validity was evaluated using composite reliability (CR, with a target of
0.70 or higher) and average variance extracted (AVE, with a target of
0.50 or higher). Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Fornell
and Larcker (1981) criterion that each construct's AVE should exceed
the squared correlations with other constructs.

Third, overall model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: the chi-
square  statistic  divided  by  degrees  of  freedom  (chi-square/df,  with
values below 3.0 indicating acceptable fit), the Comparative Fit Index
(CFI),  the  Tucker-Lewis  Index  (TLI),  the  Root  Mean  Square  Error  of
Approximation (RMSEA) with 90 percent confidence interval, and the
Standardized  Root  Mean  Square  Residual  (SRMR).  Model  fit  was
considered acceptable when CFI and TLI were at least 0.90 (good if 0.95
or higher), RMSEA was at or below 0.08 (good at or below 0.06), and
SRMR  was  at  or  below  0.08  (Hu  &  Bentler,  1999).  Although  the
chi-square  statistic  was  reported  for  completeness,  fit  evaluation
emphasized the incremental and residual indices, which are less sensi-
tive to sample size.

Fourth,  after  a  satisfactory  measurement  fit  was  established,  the
hypothesized structural model was estimated to test the proposed dual-
pathway  relations  among  high-school  and  university  motivational
climate, motivational quality, AI-use pathways, academic performance,
well-being,  and  perceived  AI-supported  learning  quality.  Direct,  indi-
rect, and total effects were examined, and mediation was tested using
bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. Indirect effects were
considered statistically significant when the 95 percent confidence in-
terval did not include zero (Preacher & Hayes, 2008). Fig. 1 presents the
hypothesized dual-pathway structural model that was evaluated in these
analyses.

Fifth, multi-group structural equation modeling examined whether
measurement and structural relations were equivalent across the high-
school  and  university  subgroups  (China-HS  group  versus  non-China-
HS group, and China-Uni group versus Thailand-Uni group). Measure-
ment invariance was assessed in a hierarchical sequence of configural,
metric, and scalar models (Wu et al., 2007). Nested model comparisons
used Satorra-Bentler scaled chi-square difference tests and changes in
CFI and RMSEA (Satorra & Bentler, 2001). Following recommendations
by  Cheung  and  Rensvold  (2002) and  Chen  (2007),  invariance  was
judged to hold when the change in CFI was 0.010 or less, and the change
in RMSEA was 0.015 or less. When full scalar invariance could not be
achieved,  partial  invariance  models  were  specified  by  freeing  non-
invariant parameters. After  establishing  an acceptable level  of  invari-
ance, latent means and key structural path coefficients were compared

The structural model showed good fit in both university subsamples
(Table 10; Figs. 5–6), indicating that the proposed dual-pathway struc-
ture fit both sampled university groupings adequately.

Across the two university groups, the overall pattern of associations
closely  resembled  the  full-sample  model  (Table  11).  In  both  groups,

autonomy-supportive climates were associated with a more autonomous
and  planful  adaptive-AI  route,  whereas  pressure-related  experiences
were associated with a more controlled and compulsive route linked to
lower  well-being  and  indirectly  to  lower  perceived  AI-supported
learning quality.

Between-group  parameter  difference  tests  indicated  that  only  one
structural  path  differed  significantly  across  university  subsamples
(Table 11): the CM →  COP association was stronger in the China-Uni
group than in the Thailand-Uni group. All other path differences were
not statistically significant, and explained variance for the key endoge-
nous variables was substantial in both groups (Figs. 5–6).

3.5. Additional multi-group mediation for university groups

To further address RQ5 and H5c, we estimated bias-corrected boot-
strapped indirect effects separately for the China-Uni and Thailand-Uni
groups (Table 12). Across both university contexts, the indirect-effect
patterns were consistent with the proposed dual-pathway framework.
Specifically,  higher  perceived  high-school  pressure  showed  negative
indirect  associations  with  perceived  AI-supported  learning  quality
through  the  pressured–compulsive  chain  (via  controlled  motivation,
compulsive AI use, and lower well-being), and also through the read-
iness–adaptation  route  (via  reduced  planned  trigger  readiness,  lower
adaptive AI use, and downstream outcomes).

In parallel, high-school autonomy support exhibited positive indirect
associations with perceived AI-supported learning quality through the
autonomy-adaptive pathway. Indirect effects involving planned trigger
readiness  and  adaptive  AI  use  were  consistently  supported,  including
routes  operating  through  perceived  academic  performance  and  well-
being.  Taken  together,  these  bootstrapped  mediation  results  provide
convergent evidence that the theorized pressure-linked and autonomy-
linked mechanisms generalize across students in Chinese and Thai uni-
versity settings (Table 12).

4. Discussion

The  present  study  aimed  to  clarify  the  psychological  processes
potentially  associated  with  university  students'  interactions  with
generative  artificial  intelligence  (GenAI)  for  learning.  By  integrating
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) with the Hook model of habit forma-
tion (Eyal, 2014), the study tested a dual-pathway model linking stu-
dents’ retrospective reports of high-school learning climate (pressure vs.
autonomy  support)  to  current  motivational  orientations,  AI-use  pat-
terns, and perceived outcomes. Because the study used a cross-sectional
survey design with retrospective reports of high-school experiences, the
directional  paths  specified  in  the  SEM  should  be  interpreted  as  theo-
retically motivated associations rather than as evidence of temporal or
causal effects (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Hu et al., 2025; Lukyanchikova et al.,
2023; Ryan & Deci, 2000, 2020).

Overall, the results were consistent with two distinguishable patterns

of association. First, higher perceived high-school pressure was associ-
ated with higher controlled motivation, which in turn was associated
with more compulsive AI use and lower well-being; these associations
were further linked to lower perceived AI-supported learning quality.
Second,  higher  perceived  high-school  autonomy  support  and  current
university autonomy support were associated with higher autonomous
motivation and greater planned trigger readiness, which were linked to
more adaptive AI use and, in turn, to higher perceived academic per-
formance,  higher  well-being,  and  higher  perceived  AI-supported
learning  quality.  Consistent  with  this  interpretation,  the  reported
bootstrapped indirect effects provide additional support for the indirect
paths specified in the model (see Results Section 3.2.1 and Table 4 for
the full sample, and Tables 9 and 12 for subgroup analyses).

Before interpreting these modeled patterns further, it is important to
note  that  the  high-school  climate  variables  were  measured  through
retrospective  self-reports  and  may  therefore  be  shaped  partly  by

4.3. Contextual variation in the modeled associations

The multi-group difference tests indicated that the CM →  COP as-
sociation  was  significantly  stronger  in  the  high-pressure  high-school
background  group,  operationalized  in  this  sample  as  students  who
attended  high  school  in  China,  than  in  the  comparison  group  (see
Table 8). Within the present dataset, this pattern is consistent with the
possibility  that  in  more  high-stakes  and  controlling  educational  cli-
mates,  controlled  motivation  may  be  more  closely  associated  with
reliance-oriented  GenAI  behavior.  One  possible  interpretation  is  that
when performance-avoidance goals are more salient, GenAI may be used
less  as  a  learning  partner  and  more  as  a  coping-oriented  tool.  This
interpretation  is  broadly  consistent  with  recent  cross-cultural  work
suggesting that students’ perceptions and enactments of GenAI can vary
across learning contexts and that culture and norms are linked to how AI
is understood and used in education (Harrington et al., 2025). At the
same time, the country labels in this study serve only as an operation-
alization  within  the  present  sample;  the  theoretical  emphasis  is  on
pressure-intensive versus autonomy-supportive climates more broadly,
rather than on national categories themselves. Whether a similar pattern
would be observed in Western higher-education settings or other insti-
tutional environments remains an open empirical question.

To connect this finding to adjacent evidence without conflating do-
mains, it is also useful to note that problematic technology use has been
linked  to  learning-related  outcomes  in  broader  digital  behavior
research.  For  example,  Meng  et  al.  (2025) found  that  mobile  phone
addiction  among  Chinese  college  students  was  associated  with  lower
learning engagement, with academic self-efficacy and physical activity
operating as mediating mechanisms. Although mobile phone addiction
is not equivalent to GenAI use, this literature supports the more general
point that unregulated reliance on digital tools can coincide with weaker
engagement-related learning processes, which conceptually aligns with
the  risk  pattern  reflected  in  the  pressure  →  controlled  motivation  →
compulsive-use association pattern modeled here.

4.4. Interpreting perceived AI-supported learning quality (AIQ) and the
strength of claims

In this study, perceived AI-supported learning quality (AIQ) refers to
students'  self-reported  evaluations  of  the  quality  of  AI-supported
learning  in  the  current  semester,  rather  than  to  objective  learning
gains or externally verified academic performance. The AIQ scale cap-
tures learners' evaluations of whether GenAI use supports understand-
ing,  produces  useable  study  materials,  and  enables  effective  learning
with  greater  confidence  after  checking  or  verifying  information.
Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as evidence about
students’ perceived learning-related value of GenAI use, not as evidence
of  causal  or  objectively  verified  improvements  in  achievement.  This
distinction  is  important  because  adjacent  research  on  AI-mediated
assessment  has  also  shown  that  AI-generated  educational  judgments
should not be treated as automatically interchangeable with validated
human  or  performance-based  measures.  For  example,  Zacharis  and
Papadakis  (2025) found  that  AI-human  agreement  in  coursework
assessment was substantially weaker than human inter-rater agreement,
underscoring the need to distinguish perceived usefulness from objec-
tive evidence of learning quality. Within that scope, the model results
suggest that perceived AI-supported learning quality is associated not
merely  with  whether  students  use  GenAI,  but  with  how  GenAI  is
incorporated  into  learning  through  the  motivational  and  behavioral
processes captured in the model. Adaptive AI use was positively asso-
ciated with perceived academic performance and well-being, and both
were positively associated with AIQ. Conversely, compulsive AI use was
associated with lower well-being, which in turn was linked to lower AIQ.
These patterns are consistent with the interpretation that GenAI may be
experienced  as  more  learning-supportive  for  some  students  and  more
reliance-oriented  for  others,  while  remaining  within  the  limits  of
cross-sectional and self-reported data.

rather than only final answers. For example, instructors can ask students
to pause before opening an AI tool and first write down three things: the

such as outlining one's reasoning or prompt intention before consulting

frequency  counts  toward  patterns  that  reflect  planful,  iterative  use

healthier engagement patterns. More broadly, the framing supports an
educational innovation perspective that shifts GenAI guidance from tool
training  alone  to  motivation-  and  routine-aware  pedagogy  (e.g.,
autonomy-supportive  course  design  plus  explicit  trigger-verification
routines).

4.6. Limitations and future suggestions

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these
findings. First, the cross-sectional survey design means that the direc-
tional  paths  specified  in  our  model  remain  theoretically  motivated
rather than causally demonstrated; reciprocal or bidirectional relations
cannot be ruled out. Second, all variables were assessed via self-report,
including  retrospective  evaluations  of  high-school  experiences  and
current  AI-use  patterns,  which  are  susceptible  to  recall  bias,  social
desirability, and shared method variance. Third, the key constructs of
planned trigger readiness, adaptive AI use, and compulsive AI use were
newly developed for the GenAI context; although their reliability and
convergent validity were satisfactory, further work is needed to estab-
lish their discriminant validity vis-a-vis established measures  of habit
strength, problematic technology use, and digital well-being. Fourth, the
sample  was  restricted  to  Chinese  universities  and  Thai  international
largely
colleges,  with  non-China-HS  group  school  backgrounds
concentrated  in  Thailand  or  international  curricula.  Accordingly,  the
present findings are best interpreted as evidence from China, Thailand-
linked educational settings rather than as confirmation of a universally
generalizable  GenAI  pathway  structure.  To  reduce  identifiability  risk
and  keep  the  questionnaire  concise,  we  did  not  collect  sensitive  de-
mographics  (e.g.,  gender,  age),  and  thus  cannot  fully  rule  out  de-
mographic confounding; future studies should include these variables to
test  robustness  and  the  modeled  relations  within  the  dual-pathway
framework.

At the same time, these Western and English-speaking studies should
not be treated as direct replications of the present model. First, the same
construct  assumption  is  not fully met.  Much  of the  existing  literature
focuses  on  student  perceptions,  acceptable  uses,  academic-integrity
concerns, or lived experience, whereas the present study models retro-
spective  high-school  motivational  climate,  autonomous  versus

controlled  motivation,  planned  trigger readiness,  and  adaptive  versus
compulsive AI-use patterns. Second, the same measurement assumption
is also not met. Prior studies have often used descriptive surveys, qual-
itative  interviews,  or  adoption-oriented  frameworks  rather  than  the
SDT-based  latent-variable  structure  developed  here.  Third,  the  same
mechanism assumption therefore remains open. Cross-regional evidence
from the Nordic countries and the USA suggests that some broad drivers
of  student  ChatGPT  adoption  recur  across  settings,  but  the  relative
importance of specific predictors still differs by region (Faraon et al.,
2025).  The  present  findings  should  therefore  be  interpreted  not  as
confirmation of a universally invariant psychological mechanism, but as
a  theory-informed  mechanism  hypothesis  supported  within  China,
Thailand-linked contexts.

These  limitations  also  suggest  several  productive  directions  for
future research. Longitudinal designs that follow students from late high
school into university would allow stronger tests of temporal ordering
and could clarify whether these adaptive and compulsive patterns reflect
relatively stable tendencies or more fluid states over time. Experimental
and  quasi-experimental  work  could  manipulate  autonomy-supportive
teaching  practices,  AI-use  guidelines,  or  interface  “friction”  to  test
whether  strengthening  Current  University  Autonomy  Support  or
scripting “healthy” AI triggers can attenuate reliance-oriented patterns
and reinforce adaptive engagement. Future studies could also combine
surveys with behavioral and learning analytics data (e.g., AI log traces,
assignment  histories,  or  experience  sampling)  to  capture  the  micro-
dynamics of trigger-action cycles more directly and extend the present
comparisons to additional regions, disciplines, and institutional types.
Finally,  qualitative  or  mixed-methods  work  with  students  and  in-
structors could deepen understanding of how GenAI is experienced as
either  an  “escape  valve”  or  a  “learning  partner,”  providing  richer
grounding for policy, training, and design efforts aimed at supporting
both learning outcomes and digital well-being.

4.7. Conclusion

This study proposed and tested a dual-pathway model of GenAI use
that  integrates  Self-Determination  Theory  with  habit-formation  per-
spectives to examine how distal educational climates and current uni-
versity  contexts  are  associated  with  students’  digital  behaviors  and
perceived outcomes. Addressing RQ1 to RQ3, the results were consistent
with the idea that high-school pressure and autonomy support, together
with Current University Autonomy Support, are associated with distinct
motivational  profiles  and  patterns  consistent  with  either  an  adaptive
pattern,  characterized  by  autonomous  motivation,  planned  trigger
readiness,  adaptive  AI  use,  and  higher  perceived  performance,  well-
being,  and  perceived  AI-supported  learning  quality,  or  a  compulsive
pattern, in which pressure-aligned controlled motivation is associated

in the full sample and subgroup analyses (Table 4; Tables 9 and 12).

pattern  of  associations  appeared  broadly  similar  across  the  sampled

controlled motivation and compulsive AI use was stronger in the high-
pressure high-school background group as operationalized in this sam-
ple  (Table  8).  Taken  together,  the  findings  suggest  that  the  central
challenge for GenAI in education is not simply whether students use AI,

oriented  shortcut.  By  foregrounding  autonomy  support,  planned
trigger  readiness,  and  context-sensitive  pressure,  the  model  offers  a
useful framework for future research and for the design of autonomy-
supportive  pedagogies  and  AI  systems  that  encourage  more  adaptive,
rather than more compulsive, engagement with GenAI in higher edu-
cation. However, these interpretations should be understood within the
