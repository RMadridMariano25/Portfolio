This  study  examined,  using  eye-tracking  technology,  the  gaze  patterns  of  guided  exposure  to
deepfake and authentic portrait images in comparison to unguided exposure to assess their in-
fluence on the acquisition and application of critical thinking literacy among first-year Bachelor
students.  Employing  a  between-groups  experimental  design,  we  analyzed  visual  gaze  patterns

group (n = 44). The results revealed significant differences in visual attention patterns, with the
experimental group exhibiting a longer duration and broader distribution of fixations compared
to  the  control  group.  The  experimental  group  also  achieved  higher  accuracy  in  identifying

integrating  cognitive  training  with  technological  solutions  to  combat  GenAI-generated  misin-
formation. This research contributes to the fields of GenAI literacy and critical thinking education
by providing objective evidence, as measured through eye-tracking, that targeted interventions
can  significantly  alter  cognitive  processing  strategies.  Educational  practice  must  balance  the
development of critical evaluation skills with fostering a realistic awareness of human limitations,
thereby  preparing  students  to  become  discerning  consumers  and  responsible  creators  in  an
increasingly complex digital information landscape.

1. Introduction and aim of the study

Humanity is entering an age of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), which is rapidly permeating people’s daily lives and
transforming how they consume and evaluate information. This technological revolution is particularly evident in the way GenAI
influences humans’ perceptions of the world through visual representations, specifically images (Rapp et al., 2025; van Hees et al.,
2025). Images can convey complex information quickly and succinctly, allowing people to grasp ideas faster than when reading a text
(Mayer, 2024; Mayer & Sims, 1994). In addition, humans are more likely to remember information presented in images because of
their brain’s ability to process and store visual data more effectively (Lahner et al., 2024). However, exposing humans, especially
learners, to images generated by GenAI (e.g., deepfake images) raises questions regarding the perception and understanding of their

2. Literature review

2.1. GenAI in higher education

Higher education institutions and their research endeavors function as dynamic ecosystems that facilitate the transfer and co-
creation of knowledge through diverse pedagogical practices. The educational journey is marked by the interaction between teach-
ing and learning, wherein faculty members serve as knowledge transmitters and facilitators of a learning environment that promotes
critical thinking, problem-solving, and the development of competencies pertinent to current and future professional fields. These
pedagogical practices underscore the significance of active student engagement and self-directed learning, culminating in profound
and meaningful learning outcomes (Chernenko, 2020). For students, learning constitutes acquiring knowledge that augments pro-
fessional capabilities in both present and future contexts (Lizzio & Wilson, 2004; Veiga Sim˜ao & Flores, 2010).

Recent research has underscored the transformative potential of GenAI for students in higher education. Tools such as ChatGPT can
substantially enhance students’ knowledge acquisition and learning processes across diverse subjects. They also can empower learners
by  granting  them  autonomy,  which  enhances  their  sense  of  control  over  their  learning  processes  and  bolsters  their  self-efficacy.
Furthermore,  these  tools  provide  continuous  real-time  feedback  on  performance,  potentially  enriching  students’  learning  experi-
ences (Cotton et al., 2023; Crawford et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023). Consequently, student engagement in the learning process is likely to
increase, as is their capacity to critically evaluate information and formulate questions, thereby strengthening their critical learning
skills (Cotton et al., 2023; Tajik & Tajik, 2023).

While GenAI presents considerable opportunities for higher education, it is also associated with certain concerns. Integrating GenAI
into educational settings raises ethical challenges that require a thorough investigation. For example, GenAI applications facilitate
content creation that is nearly indistinguishable from human-produced work. This capability may undermine the value of academic
degrees and impede higher education institutions’ ability to produce adequately prepared graduates in the labor market (Cotton et al.,
2023; Crawford et al., 2023). Furthermore, it is found that most references to GenAI ethics in academia pertained to academic integrity
issues or the misuse of information among students (Sok & Heng, 2023). These behaviors are indicative of the negative consequences
associated with the use of GenAI applications in academia. One concern is the potential misuse of these applications without proper
attribution, which may compromise essential academic skills, such as critical thinking, problem-solving, creativity, research abilities,
and interpersonal communication (Gupta et al., 2023; Tlili et al., 2023).

Another critical issue relates to students’ limited ability to recognize GenAI-generated media that is indistinguishable from human
work. This difficulty exposes them to particular risks that go beyond academic integrity. This GenAI-generated media can enable
advanced forms of cyberbullying, including the creation of non-consensual or defamatory content, with potentially severe conse-
quences for students’ wellbeing and future careers. Additionally, fabricated statements or visuals attributed to institutions and their
figures may erode trust in universities and learning resources. The spread of false facts, manipulated visuals, or synthetic datasets also
poses risks to the reliability of research and teaching materials (Roe et al., 2024). Without targeted efforts to foster digital literacy and
critical thinking skills, students remain particularly vulnerable to the harms of undetected AI-generated media (Fulsher et al., 2025, pp.
1–13).

The ability of humans to distinguish between deepfakes and authentic images has been studied for over a decade and has intensified
as GenAI has become increasingly prevalent. These studies focused on various kinds of images, starting with video images (Wagner &
Blewer, 2019), food images (as part of understanding consumer preferences; Califano & Spence, 2024), and human face images as a
hatch to understanding human social behaviors (Miller, Steward, et al., 2023). However, most research on deepfake detection by
humans has focused on facial images (Diel et al., 2024). Faces are uniquely demanding and combine perceptual and cognitive com-
plexities. Deepfake faces are influenced by biases in human judgment (Makowski et al., 2025), and are processed differently in the
brain compared to other objects (Kanwisher et al., 1997). This makes them especially compelling for studying detection. In addition,
leveraging well-documented gaze patterns in face perception (as will be presented further in the article), allows researchers to predict
control group behavior more accurately, further justifying the focus on facial images (Fearington et al., 2025).The results of studies
conducted by Farid and colleagues between 2007 and 2017 (Farid & Bravo, 2007, 2012; Holmes et al., 2016; Mader et al., 2017),
together with the systematic review by Miller and colleagues up to 2022 (Miller, Steward, et al., 2023), revealed inconsistent findings
concerning the ability to distinguish between deepfake and authentic images. Nevertheless, a recurring trend is that earlier studies
tended to report a higher probability of distinguishing between the two types of images. This pattern can be explained by the presence
of abnormal and easily detectable features in the AI-generated faces of that period, in contrast to the increasingly realistic and refined
outputs produced by more recent GenAI systems as algorithms advanced (Lago et al., 2021).

An illustration of this shift can be found in the study by Nightingale and Farid (2022), which demonstrated that participants’
performance in differentiating authentic from AI-generated faces was essentially at chance level, with accuracy hovering around 50 %.
Similar findings regarding the increasing difficulty in distinguishing between authentic and AI-generated facial images are evident in a
recent study by Huang and colleges (Huang et al., 2024), reporting difficulty in distinguishing real from fake facial images: while
per-participant recognition accuracy averaged around 76–80 %, the confusion matrix analyses showed that only 40 % of real images
and 37 % of fake images were correctly identified across the dataset, suggesting that participants often relied on guessing strategies.

To address this, Bray and colleagues (Bray et al., 2023) conducted an intervention study to teach students how to identify deepfake
face images. In their study, before the decision-making of face images (i.e., GenAI or real), the study group learned 10 principles for

2.3. Critical thinking literacy in higher education in the context of GenAI

Critical thinking is defined as the intellectually disciplined process of actively conceptualizing, analyzing, and evaluating infor-
mation gathered from observations or experiences (Paul & Elder, 2020). This definition emphasizes the active and systematic nature of
critical thinking, involving skills such as conceptualization, analysis, and evaluation.

In  higher  education,  critical  thinking  literacy  is  perceived  as  a  crucial  skill  that  has  gained  significant  attention  due  to  its
importance in academic and professional success (Indraˇsien ˙e et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2014). The integration of GenAI in education has
further emphasized the need for critical thinking literacy. One of the well-known AI competency frameworks established by UNESCO.
The framework emphasizes the importance of developing a human-centered approach to AI, fostering critical thinking, and under-
standing the ethical implications of AI technologies. This holistic approach to AI education goes beyond mere technical skills, aiming to
cultivate students’ abilities to evaluate AI systems critically, understand their societal impacts, and contribute to the development of
ethical and sustainable AI (Holmes & Miao, 2023; Krause et al., 2025).

Although the rapid progression of GenAI might prompt some to assert that the human capacity to discern authentic from synthetic
media visually is a ’lost cause,’ this viewpoint overlooks a fundamental cognitive step that precedes and initiates deeper verification
processes. Before a learner can choose to cross-reference an image, they must first adopt a mindset of active, critical scrutiny rather
than passive acceptance. The ability to question an image’s authenticity acts as a vital cognitive trigger. It is this disruption of passive
consumption that transforms a student from a mere consumer into a critical evaluator of information, encouraging them to engage in
the triangulation that the digital age demands (Pennycook et al., 2021; Vincent-Lancrin, 2023).

Several studies have highlighted the relevance and impact of critical thinking literacy on various aspects of student learning and
development. For example, critical thinking dispositions and new media literacies have been found to predict students’ ability to detect
fake  news  on  social  media.  A  study  involving  university  students  revealed  a  positive  and  moderate  relationship  between  critical
thinking  dispositions,  new  media  literacies,  and  fake  news  detection abilities  (Orhan,  2023). This  underscores  the  importance  of
developing critical thinking skills in the context of digital literacy. Another study on UK postgraduate business school students found
that while AI text generators like ChatGPT improved skills at lower levels of Bloom’s taxonomy, concerns were raised about reliability,
accuracy, and ethical implications (Essien et al., 2024). This highlights the complex relationship between GenAI technologies and
critical  thinking  skills  in  higher  education.  As  GenAI  technologies  evolve,  cultivating  these  skills  will  be  crucial  for  ensuring
responsible and informed use of GenAI in education, research, and professional practice.

Higher education institutions increasingly recognize the need to incorporate critical thinking development into their curricula,
with some studies suggesting using technology-supported classrooms and explicit teaching methods to foster these skills (Dori & Lavi,
2023; Falloon, 2023). The importance of critical thinking literacy extends beyond academic success, preparing students for future
competencies and informed decision-making in an increasingly complex world.

Additionally, recent research shows that although explanatory interventions can improve human performance when examining
critical thinking regarding AI, it may also introduce systematic biases in participants’ self-perception and task confidence, particularly
among those who overestimate their abilities (Hey et al., 2023). Similarly, Bray et al. (2023) found that individuals often perform at
near-chance levels when distinguishing real from synthetic faces, yet still report inflated confidence in their judgments.

Taken together all the studies described, there is still a key gap in understanding the cognitive mechanisms underlying deepfake
detection. Therefore, methodologies capable of capturing real-time cognitive processing, such as visual gaze patterns, are needed to
inform educational interventions that prepare students to confront the perceptual and analytical challenges posed by deepfake media.

2.4. Eye-tracking method and image distinction

Eye-tracking studies involve the collection and analysis of eye movements and gaze location of participants during various visual
research activities (Carter &  Luke, 2020; Lai et al., 2013; Liu &  Cui, 2025; Schiessl et al., 2017). It is measured by non-invasive
technologies that include both stationary and mobile eye sensors, accompanied by analytical software. This technology enables the
observation and measurement of individuals’ visual focus, visual pattern behaviours and visual attention, providing valuable insights
into human behaviour (Ball & Richardson, 2022).

The eye-tracking methodology is based on the eye-mind hypothesis, which proposes a direct link between eye movements and
cognitive processes. Within this notion, eye-movements provide a window into ongoing cognitive operations, allowing researchers to
observe and analyse attention allocation and information processing in real time. The assumption implies that the duration and fre-
quency of visual focus correspond to cognitive engagement before and during the execution of a visual task (Hessels et al., 2024; Khalid
et al., 2016; Schütz et al., 2011).

Within eye-tracking research frame, two key measurements are commonly used.

(1)  Fixations-brief eye pauses at a specific point in the visual field. During fixations, visual information is gathered and processed.
Fixations  are  mainly  measured  temporally  (measured  by  time),  quantitatively  (measured  by  frequency),  and  spatially
(measured by location). The duration and frequency of fixations vary depending on the complexity of the task. For example, by
examining average fixation count and duration across Areas of Interest (AOIs), researchers can identify regions that received
more visual attention than others. Higher frequency and longer time of fixations mostly indicate greater cognitive engagement,
reflecting either difficulty or ambiguity in task performance (Cheetham et al., 2013; Fei et al., 2023; Mahanama et al., 2022; Ye
et al., 2024).

(2)  Saccades-rapid eye movements as the gaze shifts between points of visual interest. Saccades are mainly measured spatially (the
amplitude and distance between two fixations), quantitatively (frequency) and temporally (time). Saccade values are dependent
on the nature of the visual task. Their amplitudes tend to decrease with the increasing of task difficulty and increasing cognitive
load (Mahanama et al., 2022).

Fixations and saccades function as a continuous “cycle” of visual search process. However, in eye-tracking research, the selection of
specific measures for analysis typically depends on the study’s design and objectives. Most studies utilize these measures as raw eye-
tracking data for statistical analysis, often complemented by visual representations such as ’heat maps’ and ’gaze plots’ to enhance the
interpretation of results (Lai et al., 2013; Lima & Ventura, 2023).

Eye-tracking is widely applied across disciplines for various purposes, including understanding cognitive processes in learning,
Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), and modelling variable competition in decision-making. By analysing eye-movement patterns,
researchers can, for example, identify the visual sequence, the elements that captured most attention, and how this information in-
fluences the study domain (Lai et al., 2013; Stuart, 2022).

Further to this, eye-tracking technology has emerged as a valuable tool for analysing cognitive processing. Studies have shown that
eye-movements can distinguish between strategic and non-strategic learners (Catrysse et al., 2018), problem-solving (Soares et al.,
2021), indicate local versus global visual attention and reaction to intervention (Butcher et al., 2025; Schreiter & Vogel, 2023). These
findings  highlight  the  growing  role  of  eye-tracking  within  educational  research,  particularly  in  understanding  student  learning
cognitive processing.

2.5. The present study

The objective of this study is to compare visual gaze patterns of guided exposure to deepfake and authentic portrait images in
comparison to unguided exposure, in order to assess their influence on higher education students’  acquisition and application of
critical  thinking  literacy.  From  a  theoretical  perspective,  this  research  advances  understanding  of  human  cognitive  processing  in
deepfake facial image detection. Prior work has shown that AI systems often reproduce and exaggerate facial features, producing
hyperrealistic images that are increasingly difficult to distinguish from authentic ones (Miller, Foo, et al., 2023). Given the novelty of
using  eye-tracking  to  assess  cognitive shifts  in  response  to  deepfake  detection training,  this  research  is  exploratory  in  nature.  Its
primary aim is to investigate initial patterns and generate preliminary insights that can inform hypotheses for future, larger-scale
studies. By analyzing gaze patterns, the study examines whether guided interventions can effectively alter the cognitive processes
involved in detecting deepfake facial images. Moreover, since faces require higher-order judgment that extends beyond perception into
evaluation and interpretation, they are directly relevant to fostering and assessing students’ critical thinking skills (Paul & Elder, 2020;
Vincent-Lancrin, 2023).

From a practical standpoint, the study contributes to education and research by evaluating a specific pedagogical intervention
designed to enhance critical thinking literacy in the GenAI era. By comparing gaze patterns under guided and unguided conditions, this
research seeks to identify teaching strategies that can support learners in developing more deliberate and reflective approaches when
engaging with potentially manipulated visual materials.

Following this, five research questions were addressed.

1.  Are there differences (and to what extent) between the experiment and control groups’  patterns of visual gaze attention when

viewing authentic versus deepfake portrait images?

2.  Are  there  differences (and  to  what extent)  between  the  experiment  and  control  groups in visual  gaze duration  when  viewing

authentic versus deepfake portrait images?

3.  Are there differences (and to what extent) between the experiment and control groups’ actual success in identifying portrait images

accurately?

4.  Are there similarities between the experiment and control groups in their AI hyperrealism tendency?
5.  Are there differences (and to what extent) between the experiment and the control group in perceived success in identifying portrait

images accurately?

Drawing on the reviewed literature and research questions, the following hypotheses were proposed to guide the present study:
Eye-tracking provides a reliable method for examining cognitive processes during image-based decision-making (Fearington et al.,
2025; Gupta et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024; Sano et al., 2024; W¨ohler et al., 2021). Prior research has also shown that interventions
can reshape  gaze behavior  by  directing  attention toward  relevant features and  modifying  fixation patterns  (Butcher et  al., 2025;
Schreiter & Vogel, 2023). Based on these evidences, the following hypotheses are proposed.

H1. Participants in the experimental group will demonstrate significantly different patterns of visual gaze attention in AOIs compared to the
control group.

H2. Participants in the experimental group will demonstrate significant differences in the duration of eye gaze compared to the control group.

Prior studies highlight the importance of fostering critical thinking and digital literacy to help students detect manipulated media,
with evidence that these skills predict the ability to identify fake news and synthetic content (Holmes & Miao, 2023; Krause et al.,
2025; Orhan, 2023). Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H3. Participants in the experimental group will be significantly more accurate in identifying deepfake and authentic portrait images compared
to the control group.

The rapid advancement of GenAI has led to increasingly realistic human portrait images, producing the phenomenon of AI hy-
perrealism, where participants often misclassify synthetic faces as authentic (Miller, Foo, et al., 2023); Nightingale & Farid, 2022;
Makowski et al., 2025). In light of these findings, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H4. No statistically significant differences in AI hyperrealism tendency will be observed between the experimental and control groups.

3. Method

The study, approved by the Ethical Committee at the Holon Institute of Technology, employs a between-group design, incorpo-
rating  both  an  experimental  group  and  a  control  group.  A  between-group  design  (also  called  a  between-subjects  design)  is  an
experimental research design in which participants are assigned randomly to separate groups. One group received an intervention
(experimental) and the other did not (control). The outcomes of these independent groups are then compared to determine the effect of
the manipulated variable (Gravetter & Forzano, 2018).

3.1. Participants

The study included a total of 76 participants (65 female and 18 male). Most (90 %) of the participants were first-year Bachelor’s
degree  students  between  the  ages  of  18–32  (M  = 24,  sd  = 2.8). All  participants  were  recruited  from the  same  higher  education
institution. 60 % reported they did not use at all GenAI, 32 % used it rarely and only 7 % used GenAI several times a week.

Participants were assigned to three groups: a pretesting group (n = 8, feasibility study; see Appendix A), an experimental group (n
= 24, received intervention), and a control group (n = 44, no intervention). The experimental and control groups were drawn from two
intact first-year “Visual Communication” classes, resulting in unequal group sizes due to natural enrolment differences. All participants
met standard eye-tracking criteria (normal or corrected-to-normal vision; no colour blindness).

3.2. Measures

3.2.1. Deepfake detection task

The purpose of the task was to evaluate participants’ visual behaviour and decision making regarding the nature of 12 human
portrait images  (i.e., “12  stimuli”)  presented on  a  computer  screen and  recorded using  an eye-tracking  device. Participants were
requested to decide whether the image presented was conducted by GenAI (i.e., deepfake) or an authentic human image. The task was
conducted linearly. Images were presented sequentially following a written explanation and a one-trail practice (to ensure instruction
comprehension). The stimuli set was equally distributed for image source (6 AI-generated, 6 authentic) and gender representation (3-
woman profiles per source category). For each image, participants were asked to decide whether the portrait (see example in Fig. 1a
and b) is authentic or generated by AI and accordingly click the appropriate response button. No time constraints were imposed. The
AI-generated portrait images were created using Gen AI this person does not exist reservoir, and the authentic images were from
freepik. To maintain consistency, all 12 portraits—authentic and AI-generated, were presented with identical framing, head size, and
positioning. Within this controlled framing, the images vary in the synthetic set, in typical GAN artifacts (asymmetries, background
distortions, blurred accessories, irregular details).

3.3. Procedure

The study was conducted at the “Eye-Tracking Lab” of a higher education institution. For the purposes of maintaining anonymity

participants were coded by subject numbers.

During the task, eye movements were recorded using the eye-tracker “Tobii Fusion” (120 Hz sampling rate), connected to a 32-inch
LCD monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution). Participants were seated 60–70 cm from the display, with stimuli presented in full-screen
mode.

The procedure followed a standardized protocol (Fig. 2): First, a participant registration and anonymous coding, followed by an
eye-tracking calibration (of nine-point). Next, participants read the instructions presented on screen, completed the trail and con-
ducted the “Deep Fake Detection Task” (which was recorded by the eye-tracker). At the end, participants answered the “post-huck
survey” on a google form.

4. Data coding and analysis

Using “Tobii Pro-Lab” software, each stimulus (human portrait images) was defined with a number from 1 to 12 in according to the
order of presentation in the task. Within each stimulus, see also Fig. 3, two main Areas Of Interest (AOI’s) were defined: (1) Facial
region  AOI:  eyes,  nose,  mouth  (2)  Background  AOI:  areas  without  facial  region.  These  AOIs  are  well  established  in  eye-tracking
research on face processing (Fearington et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024; Sano et al., 2024; W¨ohler et al., 2021).
AOI detection is a common approach for analysing eye-tracking data, particularly fixation patterns (Vehlen et al., 2022). In this study,
we focused on two measures: fixation count, which indicates attentional allocation to areas of interest (Catrysse et al., 2018; Cheetham
et al., 2013), and fixation duration, which reflects depth of processing and cognitive effort (Sano et al., 2024). These measures are
widely used to evaluate how interventions shape gaze behaviour in learning and decision-making tasks (Schreiter &  Vogel, 2023;
Soares et al., 2021)

Following the AOI coding, eye-tracking data was extracted, and a cleaning procedure was conducted using Python (Appendix B).
Visualization through heat maps provided an initial overview of gaze patterns (H1), which was then followed by statistical analyses
(H2–H5) conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0.

In order to understand the relationship between fixation count and fixation duration, a preliminary analysis was conducted using a
Pearson correlation test. Results revealed a strong positive correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.972), indicating that participants who exhibited
a higher number of fixations on a stimulus also tended to have longer total fixation durations. Based on this strong association, total
fixation duration was selected as the primary measure for subsequent analyses, as presented in the results.

6. Discussion

This exploratory study examined, using eye-tracking technology, the gaze patterns of guided exposure to deepfake and authentic
portrait images in comparison to unguided exposure to assess their influence on the acquisition and application of critical thinking
literacy among first-year Bachler students.

Eye-tracking represents a central methodological contribution of this study, as it provides objective indicators of participants’
cognitive  processing  during  the  intervention.  Prior  research  has  shown  that  eye-movements  can  distinguish  learning  strategies,
support analysis of problem-solving, and capture local versus global visual attention (Catrysse et al., 2018; Schreiter & Vogel, 2023;
Soares et al., 2021). By applying this method to the context of deepfake detection, the study not only advances understanding of how
guided versus unguided exposure shapes gaze behaviour but also demonstrates the broader potential of eye-tracking for investigating
how educational interventions influence students’ critical thinking processes.

The results, while preliminary, show a significant difference in visual attention patterns between the experimental and control
groups.  These  initial  findings  suggest  that  targeted  training  can  alter  the  cognitive  processing  strategies  students  employ  when
evaluating GenAI-generated content. Specifically, the experimental group, which received critical thinking intervention, exhibited a
wider distribution of fixations compared to the control group (H1 accepted, H2 partially accepted).

As in previous studies (Cheetham et al., 2013; Gupta et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024; Sano et al., 2024; W¨ohler et al., 2021), both
groups spend similar total fixation durations across the main facial features of eye, nose and mouth. Focusing on these features while
examining a face is mainly explained by psychological and social aspects, since these features are critical for conveying emotional
expressions, social signals, and identity recognition (Porras & Louwerse, 2025).

However, the finding that the experiment group significantly expanded their visual attention to peripheral areas (higher total
fixation duration of background AOI’s), may indicate a shift from intuitive visual examination strategy to a more comprehensive
evaluation approach, emphasizing the interventions impact. This finding is particularly significant given the challenges identified in
previous deepfake detection research (Gupta et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024). Huang et al. (2024) found that an image’s background
plays a key role in how viewers judge its source. Yet, most visual attention typically focuses on the central facial features - the eyes,
mouth, and nose (Fearington et al., 2025). Building on this, the current study suggests that the intervention enhanced students’ critical
thinking literacy and encouraged them to broaden their visual attention to image areas that support more accurate deepfake detection.
The significantly better overall detection accuracy demonstrated by the experiment group in comparison to the control group (H3
accepted) further supports the intervention’s positive impact on students’ practical skills in detecting deepfakes. Nevertheless, it is
crucial to note that both groups still exhibited relatively low accuracy for individual images. This outcome aligns with the literature,
which  consistently  reports  the  inherent  difficulty  humans  face  in  distinguishing  between  GenAI-generated  content  and  authentic
media, especially as GenAI technology rapidly advances (Huang et al., 2024; Nightingale &  Farid, 2022). While the intervention
improved overall accuracy, the persistent challenge at the individual image level underscores the sophistication of current deepfake
algorithms. This suggests that even with explicit guidance, the visual cues for detection can be subtle, inconsistent, or even absent in
highly convincing deepfakes. The results align with those of Bray et al. (2023), who found that while an intervention increases ac-
curacy,  the  overall  improvement  may  not  always  be  substantial  enough  to  overcome  the  fundamental  difficulty  posed  by  highly
realistic synthetic media. Diel et al. (2024) suggested combining several intervention strategies, such as feedback training and raising
awareness, to improve detection. The implication for education is that, while training is beneficial, it must be continuous, diverse and
adaptive to the evolving visual capabilities of GenAI.

The findings revealed that the control group tended to classify the images as authentic compared to the experimental group. The
control group’s tendency to classify more images as authentic, even when they were deepfakes, aligns with the documented AI hy-
perrealism bias., e.g., a higher tendency to choose GenAI images of faces as authentic humans (Nightingale & Farid, 2022). Participants

While training can improve visual sampling strategies and help mitigate biases such as hyperrealism, it may also introduce a new bias

elements are needed.

By providing specific principles and strategies for deepfake detection, the intervention equipped students with cognitive tools to
critically scrutinize images rather than defaulting to an assumption of authenticity. This is a critical outcome for critical thinking
literacy in a GenAI-saturated information environment, as it demonstrates the potential to mitigate inherent biases that can lead to
misinterpretations of digital content.

Contrary to the hypothesis, there was no significant difference between the experimental and control groups in their perceived
success of identification (H5 rejected). Both groups similarly overestimated their performance, with self-assessed estimates signifi-
cantly higher than their actual accuracy. This finding aligns with the existing literature (K¨obis et al., 2021), which suggests that in-
dividuals often struggle to accurately gauge their performance on complex tasks, especially when guessing is involved (Vuorre &
Metcalfe, 2022). This persistent overestimation, even in the experiment group, suggests that while the intervention improved actual
detection skills and altered visual gaze patterns, it did not necessarily enhance metacognitive awareness of those improvements. It is
possible that the cognitive load associated with the deepfake detection task made it challenging for participants to accurately monitor
their performance. Another explanation, also suggested by K¨obis et al. (2021), is the Dunning–Kruger effect (Kruger & Dunning, 1999).
According to this effect, individuals with limited competence are more likely to misjudge their performance. This may indicate that
participants in the control group were overconfident, whereas participants in the experimental group had a more realistic, though still
inflated, self-assessments. Consequently, both groups reported similar levels of perceived success, despite actual differences in ac-
curacy. This highlights a crucial pedagogical challenge: not only teaching students how to evaluate GenAI content critically but also
helping them develop a realistic understanding of their capabilities and limitations in doing so. This metacognitive gap could lead to
overconfidence, potentially making students more vulnerable to sophisticated deepfakes in real-world scenarios.

In sum, this study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on GenAI literacy and critical thinking pedagogy, paving the way
for educational practices that empower students to become discerning consumers and responsible creators in a GenAI-driven world.
Using eye-tracking technology in this examination contributes to educational technology research by demonstrating how such mea-
sures  can  reveal  the  mechanisms  through  which  educational  interventions  modify  cognitive  processing.  The  use  of  eye-tracking
technology proved to be valuable in revealing the underlying cognitive processes, offering a deeper understanding of how students
engage with visual information when attempting to detect deepfakes. The integration of eye-tracking into pedagogical evaluation
provides evidence-based foundations for educational interventions that move beyond declarative knowledge about GenAI into pro-
cedural and strategic knowledge essential for higher education students.

Framing the task of visual discernment as futile risks overlooking a fundamental cognitive step that precedes and initiates deeper
verification processes. While the rapid progression of GenAI might lead some to argue that the human capacity to distinguish authentic
from synthetic media is a “lost cause,” this perspective neglects the essential role of visual analysis within the broader framework of
critical evaluation. The primary goal of pedagogical interventions, such as the one examined in this study, is not to create infallible
human detectors but to foster this initial stage of cognitive engagement. By requiring individuals to actively assess the authenticity of
images, such interventions stimulate cognitive and metacognitive processes that are essential for the development of critical thinking
skills.

By  training  students  to  move  from  passive  consumption  to  active  scrutiny,  we  equip  them  with  an  essential  foundation  for
recognizing and questioning misinformation. This study uses eye-tracking to objectively measure whether such an intervention can
shift the underlying cognitive processing strategies that are essential for navigating the complex GenAI landscape.

As GenAI technologies continue to evolve, higher education institutions must prioritize the development of the essential 21st-cen-
tury literacies, integrating cognitive training with technological solutions to address GenAI-generated misinformation. Educational
practice must balance the development of critical evaluation skills with fostering a realistic awareness of human limitations, thereby
preparing students to become discerning consumers and responsible creators in an increasingly complex digital information landscape.

7. Limitations and future studies

Several limitations should be acknowledged, informing future research directions. It is essential to interpret the findings of this
study in light of its exploratory design and its associated constraints. The primary limitation is the small sample size (n = 24 in the
experimental  group),  which  affects  the  statistical  power  of  our  analyses  and  limits  the  generalizability  of  the  results.  While  the
between-groups design enhances internal validity, we acknowledge that unmeasured factors could have influenced the outcomes.
Therefore, the value of this research lies in its novel use of eye-tracking to reveal cognitive patterns, providing a foundation upon which
future, more robust confirmatory studies can be built.

The study focused on portrait images in a controlled academic setting, raising questions about the generalizability of its findings to
other deepfake formats and real-world application contexts. At the same time, unmeasured factors, such as prior exposure to GenAI,
baseline analytical thinking, or cognitive style, may have influenced the outcomes.

Furthermore, this study’s analysis focused on the overall differences between the experimental and control groups. As shown in the
results, the distribution of correct and incorrect answers between the two groups differs across images, and both groups demonstrate
low accuracy in all images. However, we acknowledge that several participants in the control group were able to identify several

Future research should investigate broader and longer-term interventions, accompanied by follow-up assessments, to evaluate the
retention  and  transfer  of  skills  to  real-world  contexts.  Investigating  personalized  or  adaptive  training  approaches,  collaborative
learning methods, and integrating GenAI-assisted detection tools with human judgment represents promising directions. Additionally,
research examining individual differences in deepfake detection capabilities could inform more targeted educational interventions.

The persistence of detection difficulties and metacognitive overconfidence suggests that comprehensive digital literacy education
requires  multi-faceted  approaches.  Future  interventions  could  incorporate  post-experiment  interviews  and  include:  (1)  explicit

source credibility and situational factors. In addition, the use of concurrent think-aloud protocols may provide a clearer understanding
of participants’ judgment processes.

Appendix C. Source of Fixation Differences

To assure that the significant difference in total fixation duration in background areas did not occurre due to an extension of in-
dividual total fixation durations or an increase in the number of fixations, we repeated the same analysis. However, this time, instead of
examining the total fixation duration in each area of interest, we analyzed the duration of individual fixations within each area of
interest. This analysis allows us to determine whether the difference in total fixation duration is due to an increase in fixation length or
an increase in the number of fixations.

Results (Figure C1), present the distribution of single fixation durations across all areas of interest is similar, with no significant

difference between the experiment and control groups.

For example, when analyzing all fixations (Full image), it was found that there was no significant difference in the woman face
image between the experiment and control groups, the average single fixation duration in the experiment group was 288 ± 193 ms,
while control group was 301 ± 171ms (t-test, p = .17). Similarly, for the male face image, the average single fixation duration in the
control group was 290 ± 148 ms, whereas in the experiment group was 289 ± 180ms, and in the control group was 290 ± 148ms (t-
test, p = .90).

Statistical significance tests conducted for the other AOI’s produced similar results, indicating that the distribution of single fixation

durations did not differ significantly between the control and experiment groups.

These findings suggest that the difference in total fixation duration stems from a difference in the number of fixations rather than
their individual durations. We separately examined whether this difference indeed originated from the number of fixations, and the
results support this claim (data not shown).
