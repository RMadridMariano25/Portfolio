A B S T R A C T

Aim: To explore how first-year nursing students engaged with ChatGPT during an assessment task, and to understand the experiences, challenges, and perceptions
they reported regarding its use in academic work.
Background: As artificial intelligence (AI) tools like ChatGPT reshape higher education, new opportunities and ethical tensions emerge in how students learn. Rather
than  simply adopting  AI  as  a  shortcut, there  is  an  urgent  need  to  guide  students  toward  learning  with  AI  critically,  ethically,  and  creatively  within  assessment
practices. Despite the growing use of AI, limited research has examined how structured integration into undergraduate education influences learning outcomes,
digital literacy, and academic integrity.
Design:  A  qualitative  descriptive  study  investigated  the  ethical  and  pedagogical  implications  of  integrating  ChatGPT  into  a  first-year  undergraduate  nursing
assessment.
Method: Focus groups were conducted with 48 first-year nursing students following their use of ChatGPT in an assessment task. Semi-structured questions explored
students' experiences, challenges, and perceptions of ChatGPT's role in their academic development. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic
analysis, supported by the AI tool CoPilot for coding rigour.
Results: The analysis identified six themes: initial confusion, developing skills, evaluating reliability, negotiating academic integrity, recognising future benefits, and
valuing support. Students initially experienced uncertainty, particularly around ethical use and institutional messaging. Many developed critical engagement skills,
using ChatGPT to improve writing clarity, language proficiency, and idea generation. However, tensions remained between the benefits of AI support and concerns
about dependency, misinformation, and digital inequities.
Conclusions: Structured, ethical engagement with ChatGPT can foster critical literacy, digital fluency, and deeper learning outcomes in undergraduate education. To
fully realise AI's potential, institutions must develop coherent policies, embed AI literacy into curriculum design, and provide targeted support for educators and
students. Learning with AI, rather than passively relying on it, is essential for preparing graduates to thrive in an AI-integrated future.

1. Introduction

The rise  of generative  artificial intelligence (GenAI) has  marked a
profound shift in higher education. Powered by large language models
capable of performing tasks once thought exclusive to human cognition,
such as constructing complex academic texts, GenAI tools are reshaping
the ways students learn and educators teach (Bai˙Doo-Anu and Owusu
Ansah,  2023;  Kasneci  et  al.,  2023;  Strzelecki,  2024).  Among  these,
ChatGPT has emerged as a prominent platform for generating written
content  across  domains,  including  healthcare  and  medical  education
(Gilson et al., 2022; Gleason, 2022). The rapid adoption is attributed to
its ability to support language development, generate study materials,
and  assist  with  various  assessment  tasks  (Pack  and  Maloney,  2023;
Alasadi and Baiz, 2023; Voss et al., 2023).

Following ChatGPT's public release in November 2022, higher edu-
cation institutions faced an urgent paradigm shift. Initial attempts to ban
AI use, as noted by Wilcox (2023), quickly gave way to recognition that
prohibiting  such  technologies  was  both  impractical  and  counterpro-
ductive. As a result, AI integration has accelerated across educational
contexts, demanding new approaches to curriculum design, assessment
integrity, and teaching practices (Singh et al., 2023; Strzelecki, 2024). In
Australia,  the  Tertiary  Education  Quality  and  Standards  Agency
(TEQSA)  has  established  a  ‘Higher  Education  Good  Practice  Hub’  to
guide  institutions  in  navigating  both  the  opportunities  and  ethical
complexities associated with GenAI (TEQSA, 2024).

AI's  growing  presence  compels  universities  to  reconsider  funda-
mental aspects of education. Institutions that fail to strategically engage
with AI risk leaving students underprepared for increasingly digitised

A  total  of  48  students  self-selected  to  participate.  Each  student

groups. Five focus groups were conducted face-to-face and three were
conducted  online,  providing  flexibility  for  on-campus  and  remote
learners. Each group ran for approximately 30–40 min.

At the beginning of each session, participants were welcomed by the
facilitator  and  reminded  that their  contributions  would  remain  confi-
dential  and  would  not  influence  academic  outcomes.  Semi-structured
questions  were  used  to  guide  discussion,  allowing  participants  to
describe their experiences in their own words. All sessions were audio-

2. Methodology

4. Assessment task context

The  objective  of  this  study  was  to  explore  how  students  reported
engaging with ChatGPT during an assessment task, and to understand
the experiences, challenges, and perceptions they described regarding
its  use  in  academic  work.  A  Qualitative  Descriptive  Design  was
employed because it is well-suited to capturing participants' everyday
experiences and perspectives without imposing interpretive or evalua-
tive assumptions.

This approach is well-suited for studies focused on describing par-
ticipants'  everyday experiences and  perspectives.  It provided  a strong
foundation for exploring how students in a Bachelor of Nursing program
engaged with AI tools, and how such tools might support learning and
knowledge development when used responsibly. Given the dual focus on
understanding students' reported experiences with ChatGPT and exam-
ining how they navigated its use within an assessment task, a qualitative
descriptive design was deemed appropriate (Creswell and Poth, 2024;
Villamin et al., 2024). Ethical approval for this study was obtained from
the  University  Human  Research  Ethics  Committee  (ETH2024–0113).
Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained, and stu-
dents'  assessment  outcomes  were  not  influenced  by  their  decision  to
participate.  Ethical  clearance  also  permitted  the  incorporation  of
ChatGPT into the assessment task for first-year students enrolled in the
communication  subject  within  a  Bachelor  of  Nursing  course  at  an
Australian university.

The focus groups were facilitated by the course coordinator, who was
known to students in an instructional capacity but was not involved in
the marking of their assessment tasks. This separation was made explicit
to participants to minimise perceived power imbalance and to ensure
that participation would not influence academic outcomes. A reflexive
stance was adopted throughout the study to remain aware of the facil-
itator's dual role and to avoid introducing leading questions or expec-
tations during data collection.

The  assessment  required  students  to  analyse  a  case  study  and
respond  to  specific  questions  in  a  1200-word  essay.  Students  were
instructed  to  use  ChatGPT  as  a  “personalised  tutor”  to  support  their
understanding of the task and to assist with aspects of academic writing
such as sentence structure, clarity, and idea development. The purpose
of incorporating ChatGPT was to provide students with an opportunity
to engage with generative AI in a guided and ethical manner. Students
were  explicitly  advised  not  to  submit  AI-generated  text  as  their  own
work and were reminded of the importance of fact-checking AI outputs
and  acknowledging  use  of  the  tool.  The  full  assessment  instructions
provided to students are shown in Table 1.

5. Data analysis

The principles of Braun and Clarke's (2020), thematic analysis pro-
vided  a  means  to  analyse  and  interpret  the  data  (Braun  and  Clarke,
2020;  Liamputtong,  2019).  The  initial  phase  of  thematic  analysis
required deep engagement and becoming immersed in the data through
reading and rereading each transcript multiple times (Braun and Clarke,
2020;  Stratton-Maher  et  al.,  2023).  Deep  engagement  with  the  tran-
scripts assisted with the identification of commonalities that emerged
from the data, enabling major themes and sub-themes to be identified
(Liamputtong, 2019). Subsequently, coding, analysis, and interpretation
were  enhanced  with  the  assistance  of  the  Generative  AI  tool  CoPilot,
which  employs  the  GPT-4  language  model  and  includes  commercial-
grade data protection. Further, each researcher reviewed the emerging
themes  and  subthemes  to  determine  parallels  and  likeness  as  well  as
discrepancies  to  ensure  rigour  (Liamputtong,  2019;  Stratton-Maher
et al., 2023).

7. Navigating confusion and adaptation

The  introduction  of  ChatGPT  into  assessment  tasks  highlighted  a
range  of  initial  barriers  students  faced  when  engaging  with  AI  tools.
During  the  focus  groups,  many  participants  described  feelings  of
confusion,  hesitation,  or  uncertainty,  reflecting  differing  levels  of  fa-
miliarity with digital technologies and varied interpretations of the tool
created a sense of overwhelm, particularly when trying to understand
both its capabilities and its limitations.

For instance, Zara shared, “we didn't have much of an idea until we
went into the task sheet and got an idea of how to use it”. Similarly, Ella
commented,  “it  confused  me  more,  not  having  used  an  AI  before,”
highlighting the steep learning curve participants experienced. Willow
added, “I used the sentence starters, but I don't think I fully understood
how  to  use  it  properly,”  demonstrating  the  uncertainty  students  felt
when attempting to integrate AI support into academic writing.

Misunderstandings about ChatGPT's capabilities, such as its inability
to search scholarly databases, further exacerbated early confusion. As
Isabella noted, “I didn't realise that GPT couldn't search scholarly da-
tabases.” Despite these challenges, some students recognised emerging
benefits, as Mia shared: “I thought it was really good because obviously
I've just left school, so my extended vocabulary isn't the greatest,” sug-
gesting  that,  for  some,  the  AI  offered  tangible  support  even  amid
uncertainty.

Of particular importance was the confusion surrounding the ethical
use of AI. Victoria reflected, “I think I got a bit confused when we were
learning about how to use it ethically.” Similarly, Yasmin described her
initial scepticism:

I  was  so  cautious  and  sceptical.  I  feel  like  we'd  been  told  a  lot  of
times, don't use AI. Like we've just gone through this whole academic
integrity thing with three modules, … don't ever use AI, and then the
first assessment we get was like, no, you can use AI.

These  contrasting  messages  contributed  to  uncertainty  for  some  stu-
dents. Others expressed more neutral reactions, such as Xena, who felt “a
little bit indifferent,” or anxiety, as Ruby shared: “I was panicked.”

8. Developing functional and academic skills through AI

Participants described using ChatGPT in a variety of practical ways
to support their academic writing. While some students used the tool
frequently, others engaged with it more selectively, reflecting different
levels  of  familiarity,  confidence,  and  perceived  usefulness.  Many  stu-
dents commented on ChatGPT's ability to help with sentence structure,
clarity, and organisation of ideas.

Willow explained, “it helped with language structure,”  while Bella
shared,  “I  used  it  for  sentence  structure  and,  like,  improving  the
confusion  in  my  paragraphs.”  Adeline  also  noted  how  ChatGPT  sup-
ported  her  essay  organisation:  “structure  my  paragraphs  to  make  it
better for my sentence structure.” These accounts illustrate that several
students  used  ChatGPT  to  refine  their  own  writing  rather  than  to
generate content.

Some  students  used  ChatGPT  to  review  the  clarity  of  their  work.

Willow described this process:

“I just used my whole …… paragraph, and then just asked whether it's
written nicely or not. So, in that sense, it helped to really check whether
everything  was  cohesively  done  and  for  grammatical  mistakes,”
demonstrating  how  students  sought  feedback  rather  than  replace-
ment text.

Participants  also  described  using  ChatGPT  to  help  interpret  task  re-
quirements or initiate early planning. Courtney noted, “it gave the basic
information about the task, how to structure it and it was really helpful.”

9. Evaluating effectiveness and navigating reliability challenges

Participants  described  mixed  experiences  when  evaluating  the  ac-
curacy,  usefulness,  and  reliability  of  ChatGPT.  While  some  students
found  the  tool  helpful  in  offering  starting  points  or  clarifying  ideas,
others  expressed  uncertainty  about  how  much  they  could  rely  on  its
suggestions. These varied responses reflected differing levels of confi-
dence in assessing AI-generated content and highlighted the challenges
of applying critical judgement in digital learning environments.

Isabella reflected on ChatGPT's role as an initial guide, stating, “it
actually  gave us a bit  of (an) idea  like where you can start, but then
again, it's on you, but like all the research and everything, I did it on my
own.” Ella similarly shared, “I found it actually very good. I know I didn't
use it quite the way it was intended, but for me, it was very eye-opening
to see, to learn about it.”

At the same time, students highlighted concerns about relevance and
reliability.  Tia  commented,  “the  information  provided  by  ChatGPT  is
not, like, relevant all the time. So, like, we have to do research,” while
Angie emphasised the necessity for critical evaluation: “sometimes it's,
like, …… it just makes up the answer. So, you have to double check it.”
Instances  of  misinformation  or  inaccuracies  were  frequently  re-
ported. Amanda noted, “some of the responses weren't right. Some of
them didn't make any sense,” and Kaylah remarked, “we couldn't find
any  sources  that  supported  the  answers  in  ChatGPT,”  reinforcing  the
need for students to independently verify AI-generated content. Olivia
described the need to independently assess AI-generated content:

"yeah, obviously all the information that it gave, you had to cross-
check  it  just  to  make  sure  because  I  think  the  technology  or  at
least that version is still a bit undercooked. So, yeah, you just need to
sort of fact-check it and make sure what it's saying is legit."

Hridya summarised this sentiment succinctly: “yeah, it makes stuff up.”
Across  the  focus  groups,  participants  consistently  emphasised  the
importance  of  verification,  fact-checking,  and  independent  research.
However, the degree of caution varied, with some students approaching
ChatGPT sceptically from the outset and others learning through expe-
rience  that  outputs  required  scrutiny.  These  diverse  accounts  suggest
that while students found ChatGPT useful for generating ideas or clari-
fying  structure,  they  also  recognised  its  limitations,  highlighting  the
need for critical literacy and careful judgement when engaging with AI
in educational settings.

10. Negotiating academic integrity and ethical use of AI

Participants described a range of concerns and reflections related to
ethical use and academic integrity when working with ChatGPT. While
some students felt cautious due to previous academic integrity training,
others focused on balancing the benefits of AI support with the need to
maintain  originality  and  responsible  academic  practice.  These  varied
perspectives  highlight  that  students  approached  ethical  engagement
with AI in different ways.

Apprehension  was  particularly  evident  among  students  already
familiar  with  university  integrity  requirements.  as  Susan  shared,
“initially I was quite worried at first because of the academic integrity
module  that  we  did.”  Similarly,  Ella  emphasised  the  need  to  use  AI
carefully “we had to use it responsibly. Yeah, that was a task, right? Like,
you're using an AI, but still you're maintaining the academic integrity at
the same time.”

A recurring idea among participants was that ChatGPT could assist
with writing or idea development, although should not replace original
thinking or independent research. Gloria reflected this view, stating, “
rather than just copy and paste the whole thing, you just take an idea and
then  do  your  research.”  At  the  same  time,  some  students  described
concerns about becoming overly reliant on AI support. Willow noted,
“for me, I feel like the more I use ChatGPT, the more dependent I get on
it.”

Participants also described different strategies they used to ensure
academic integrity. Matilda explained, “we just have to take the normal
overview  of it,  then  we just  have  to  do a  summarisation,”  and  Angie
highlighted  her  approach  to  maintaining  originality:  “I  just  read
everything,  and  I  just  used  my  own  words.  I  never,  like,  copied  and
pasted it.”

Some students referenced using additional tools when paraphrasing.
Courtney  commented,  “you  do  have  websites  that  paraphrase,  like
Quillbot.” Angie described her method of engaging with ChatGPT while
preserving originality: “I looked at the information from the chat, but I
wrote it in my own words.”

Collectively, these accounts show that students grappled with both
the opportunities and tensions involved in using AI for academic work.
Their reflections point to a developing awareness of how to engage with
generative  tools  ethically,  and  the  importance  they  placed  on  main-
taining  academic  integrity  within  a  digitally  supported  learning
environment.

11. Recognising the benefits and future potential of AI
collaboration

Participants described a range of perceived benefits associated with
using ChatGPT, noting that the tool supported different aspects of their
academic work depending on their individual needs, learning styles, and
levels  of  familiarity  with  AI.  While  some  students  highlighted  im-
provements in clarity or idea generation, others valued ChatGPT for its
ability to simplify complex concepts or provide reassurance during in-
dependent study. These varied accounts suggest that students engaged
with ChatGPT as more than a technical aid, drawing on it in ways that
complemented their own learning approaches.

Rebecca described the impact on her writing, stating, “it enhanced
my  clarity  of  writing.  It wasn't  that good  before,  but  now  with using
ChatGPT,  I'm  able  to  write  more  nicely  than  before……with  more
diverse  viewpoints.”  For  some  students,  particularly  those  studying
online, ChatGPT also offered a sense of support that reduced feelings of
isolation. Sunita shared, “I think for me studying online I have found so far
to  be  quite  isolating  and  so  I've  really  enjoyed  having  I  guess  that  study
buddy.”

the  potential  for  ChatGPT  to  streamline  aspects  of  academic  work,
although views varied on the extent to which this was beneficial. Adam
commented,

"It just would  be a lot easier for  me, because it  gives out  answers
straight away, and I don't have to waste a lot of time finding answers,
finding  everything.  If  it's  not  the  right  answer,  then  I  can  always
search it up and find the right answer."

Overall, students' reflections demonstrated a range of perceived bene-
fits, from writing support to concept clarification and reduced isolation,
while also highlighting ongoing questions about consistency, access, and
appropriate  use  across  different  subjects.  These  perspectives  indicate
that  students  viewed  ChatGPT  as  a  potentially  useful  collaborator  in
their learning, provided it is integrated in ways that support responsible
and informed engagement.

12. The critical role of feedback, instructional support, and
digital literacy

Participants described structured feedback, training materials, and
tutor  guidance  as  important  supports  that  shaped  how  they  engaged
with ChatGPT. While some students felt confident navigating AI tools
independently, others relied heavily on instructional resources to clarify
expectations, reduce uncertainty, and understand how to use ChatGPT
appropriately within academic contexts.

The ChatGPT  instructions,  videos and  supplementary  training ma-
terials, were commonly mentioned as useful resources. James reflected
this view, stating, “the training videos were helpful; the explanation was
pretty good, because once you get through it……. once you get the ac-
cess to it, you can search most things.” Students who valued these ma-
terials often noted that practical demonstrations and clear explanations
helped them understand how to begin using the tool.

Tutor  demonstration  was  also  identified  as  a  helpful  element  of
support. Mercy recalled: “the tutor, put it up on the screen and put things
into it and showed us more on how to use it,” illustrating how real-time
modelling contributed to students' understanding of how ChatGPT could
be  used  within  assessment  preparation.  Several  students  noted  that
guidance on effective searching was particularly useful, as this was an
area where they initially encountered difficulties.

Not  all  students  relied  on  the  instructional  materials  to  the  same
extent. Angie shared, “I feel like I already knew how to do it,” describing
prior  exposure  to  AI  during  high  school.  Others  suggested  that  addi-
tional resources, different types of demonstrations, or more advanced
guidance could further support their ability to work critically with AI
technologies.

Across participants, instructional support, whether through videos,
written  guidance,  or  tutor  modelling,  was  commonly  perceived  as
helpful in clarifying appropriate use of ChatGPT, reinforcing academic
integrity  expectations,  and  reducing  uncertainty.  These  accounts  re-
flected differing degrees of reliance on support but a shared recognition
of the value of clear direction when engaging with AI tools in assessment
contexts.

Taken  together,  these  perspectives  highlight  the  importance  of
accessible  guidelines,  purposeful  instructional  design,  and  targeted
digital literacy support when integrating AI into undergraduate learning
environments.

13. Discussion

The  objective  of  this  study  was  to  explore  how  first-year  nursing
students engaged with ChatGPT during an assessment task, and to un-
derstand  the  experiences,  challenges,  and  perceptions  they  reported
regarding its use in academic work. Rather than positioning ChatGPT as
a  replacement  for  students'  own  knowledge,  the  assessment  was
designed to encourage students to work with AI as a supportive tool. The
findings reflect how students described engaging with AI as a supportive
tool,  using  it  to  refine  writing,  generate  ideas,  and  support  under-
standing, while also recognising its limitations and the need for critical
judgement.

Structured  engagement  with  AI  technologies,  as  described  by  par-
ticipants, appeared to support aspects of academic development, digital
fluency,  and  emerging  critical  literacy.  However,  the  findings  also
revealed  ongoing  tensions,  particularly  around  ethical  use,  potential
dependency, and inconsistent institutional messaging. It is also impor-
tant  to  acknowledge  that some  participants  described  concerns  about
becoming overly dependent on AI tools, reflecting wider trends in higher
education where passive reliance on generative AI has been reported.
These issues point to the importance of clear guidance and pedagogi-
cally grounded approaches when integrating AI into higher education
contexts.

Moving beyond the dichotomy of banning or embracing AI tools, this
study  advocates  for  a  model  of  AI  integration  that  cultivates  critical
engagement,  ethical  discernment,  and  pedagogical  innovation.  The
following  sections  explore  these  findings  across  five  interconnected
areas: critical engagement with AI, skill development, ethical tensions,
instructional support, and future directions for AI integration.

13.1. Critical engagement with AI in assessment design

This  study  explored  how  first-year  nursing  students  reported
engaging with ChatGPT when it was embedded into a formal assessment
task, with a focus on the experiences, challenges, and perceptions they
described  when  using  AI  in  academic  work.  The  study  emphasised
supporting  students  to  learn with  AI,  treating  ChatGPT  as  a  tool  that
facilitates thinking, planning, and reflection, rather than learning from
AI in ways that replace personal knowledge construction. The qualita-
tive descriptive approach enabled an examination of students' reported
interactions with ChatGPT within the assessment context, documenting
how they understood, navigated, and interpreted its role in their aca-
demic work.

13.2. Enhancing writing, language skills, and idea generation

Participants  described  ChatGPT  as  useful  for  clarifying  ideas,
refining  language,  and  improving  the  organisation  of  their  writing.
Many drew on the tool to support sentence construction, generate initial
ideas,  or  explain  complex  terminology. These  accounts  illustrate how
students used ChatGPT as one source of support in their writing process,
aligning with the principle of learning with AI rather than relying on it to
produce  academic  work  for  them.  These  findings  are  consistent  with
research  suggesting  AI  supportive  learning  can  offer  accessible,  per-
sonalised feedback and reduce cognitive load (George and George, 2023;
Lodge et al., 2023; Kayalı et al., 2023). Similarly, studies by Li (2023),
Ljuji´c et al. (2023), and Thi et al., 2025 note that AI tools can comple-
ment  students'  efforts  by  providing  adaptable  forms  of  academic
assistance.

Participants who spoke English as an additional language described
ChatGPT  as  particularly  helpful  for  understanding  discipline-specific
terminology  and  improving  grammatical  clarity.  Improved  grammat-
ical  accuracy  and  comprehension  were  especially  noted  in  technical
subjects like biology. This resonates with Kim et al. (2021), who found
that AI chatbots improved speaking and language performance among
English as Foreign Language learners. Likewise, Ghimire et al. (2024)

13.3. Ethical tensions and institutional messaging

Despite  acknowledging  these  advantages,  participants  frequently
described  uncertainty  arising  from  inconsistent  institutional  guidance
regarding  AI  use.  While  ChatGPT  was  actively  incorporated  into  this
subject, students had previously completed academic integrity modules
that cautioned strongly against AI use, creating confusion about how to
engage ethically and appropriately.

These  concerns  parallel  broader  issues  identified  in  the  literature,
where  unclear  AI  policies  can  heighten  student  anxiety  and  impede
responsible engagement (Cotton et al., 2024; Firat, 2023; Sabzalieva and
Valentini, 2023). Kong et al. (2024) similarly found that students new to
AI-enabled tasks often report hesitation and uncertainty in the absence
of  explicit,  consistent  guidance.  These  concerns  align  with  Simms
(2025),  who  emphasises  that  nursing  students  require  structured
generative AI literacy to critically evaluate AI outputs, recognise limi-
tations such as bias or misinformation, and develop the ethical compe-
tence needed for responsible use in both academic and clinical settings.
Participants' accounts reinforce the need for policies that support ethical
engagement  and  clarify  how  students  can  learn  with  AI  while  still
demonstrating independent understanding.

13.4.

Instructional support and educator preparedness

Participants identified instructional support as an important factor
shaping their  engagement with ChatGPT. Training  videos, written in-
structions,  and  tutor  demonstrations  helped  clarify  expectations  and
model appropriate use. These supports enabled students to understand
how ChatGPT could complement, not replace, their own analytic and
writing efforts, again reinforcing the pedagogical emphasis on learning
with AI rather than from it. Differing levels of digital fluency meant that
some students relied heavily on these supports, while others felt more
confident  navigating  AI  independently.  This  variation  highlights  the
need for flexible, layered digital literacy supports (Favero, 2024).

The findings also highlight the role of educator preparedness. Aca-
demic  staff  require  not  only  familiarity  with  AI  tools,  but  also  peda-
gogical strategies to integrate AI ethically and constructively (Walter,
2024). Integrated approaches across programs, as advocated by CRADLE
Deakin (2023a, 2023b), can help reduce inconsistencies and ensure that
assessments  invite  students  to  engage  with  AI  in  transparent  and
educationally meaningful ways.

13.5. Future directions for AI integration

Clear guidelines, accessible training, and structured opportunities for
practice  are  essential  for  supporting  ethical  engagement  with  AI
(Matthews  and  Volpe,  2023).  Existing  research  highlights  the  impor-
tance  of  explicit  guidance  to  help  students  critically  appraise  AI-
generated  outputs  (Oldham,  2024;  Bai˙Doo-Anu  and  Owusu  Ansah,
2023).  Kohnke  et  al.  (2023) similarly  emphasise  that  intentional  and
transparent integration is necessary to prepare students for increasingly
AI-rich learning environments.

AI  enabled  learning  offers  possibilities  for  personalisation,  learner
autonomy, and varied forms of academic support (Kamalov et al., 2023;
Weng et al., 2024). To make effective use of these possibilities, assess-
ment  design should  continue  to foreground  learning with  AI, encour-
aging critical, reflective, and ethically informed interactions, rather than
creating conditions where students may feel encouraged to rely on AI-
generated  content  in  place  of  their  own  academic  judgement  (Chiu,
2024a; Kasneci et al., 2023).

15. Implications for policy

To support ethical engagement and reduce uncertainty, institutions
must establish clear and consistent guidelines regarding the acceptable
use of AI tools. Participants described confusion arising from misaligned
messages  across  subjects  and  academic  integrity  modules.  University-
level  policies  that  articulate  expectations  for  both  students  and  staff,
and that are applied consistently across programs, would help minimise
ambiguity and ensure that responsible AI engagement aligns with aca-
demic integrity requirements.

16. Implications for practice

In practice, AI tools should be integrated intentionally into curricu-
lum and assessment design in ways that support students to learn with AI
while maintaining responsibility for their own academic work. Educa-
tors  require  targeted  professional  development  to  help  them  model
ethical  AI  engagement,  support  students'  critical  appraisal  skills,  and
ensure  that  assessment  tasks  do  not  inadvertently  encourage  over-
reliance  on  generative  outputs.  As  Simms  (2025) argues,  developing
generative AI literacy is now an essential component of nursing educa-
tion, requiring explicit instruction, ethical framing, and guided practice
to help students engage with AI tools critically and responsibly.

Developing students' digital and AI fluency is essential and can be
strengthened  by  embedding  opportunities  to  engage  with  AI  within
authentic assessment tasks. This approach supports students to navigate
complex information environments ethically, critically, and confidently.

17. Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when

interpreting the findings.

The  small  sample  size  and  qualitative  design  limit  the  general-
isability of the results. The perspectives reported reflect the experiences
of  the  first-year  nursing  students  who  volunteered  to  participate  and
may not represent those of the broader student cohort. This is consistent
with  qualitative  research,  which  prioritises  depth  and  detail  over
population-level generalisations (Creswell and Poth, 2024).

A further limitation is the focus on first-year students within a single
discipline. Students in later years, or those from other health and non-
health programs, may engage with AI differently due to variations in

18. Broader reflections: the future of AI in higher education

Excluding  AI  technologies  from  classrooms  and  assessments  over-
looks a critical opportunity to educate students about the responsible
and strategic use of generative AI. Today's students are already engaging
with AI platforms, whether through formal introduction or independent
exploration.  Rather  than  discouraging  AI  use,  educators  must  proac-
tively  guide  students  in  developing  critical,  ethical  engagement  with
these  technologies.  Recognising  that  AI  tools  are  now  integral  to  the
student learning experience and future careers, particularly in fields like
healthcare, education systems must evolve to emphasise ethical guide-
lines, critical thinking, and academic integrity in all AI-related learning
experiences.

This  study  highlights  the  transformative  potential  of  AI  tools  to
enhance  learning  by  fostering  digital  literacy,  critical  thinking,  and
problem-solving  skills,  all  while  preparing  graduates  for  a  workforce
where  AI  will  be  pervasive.  By  reimagining  traditional  assessment
models, educators can integrate AI technologies in ways that empower
students  as  co-creators  of  knowledge,  rather  than  passive  consumers.
Strategic  integration  can  streamline  learning  processes,  allowing  in-
stitutions to embrace innovation without placing additional academic
burden on staff or students.

Supporting  students  in  the  responsible  use  of  AI  bridges  the  gap
between  emerging  technologies  and  traditional  educational  practices.
This  study  advocates  for  a  forward-thinking  approach,  where  AI  be-
comes  a  partner  in  fostering  creativity,  collaboration,  and  discovery
within the academic experience. Higher education stands at a critical
juncture, where the convergence of human intelligence and AI offers the
potential to revolutionise the way students learn, think, and engage with
complex content.

Finally, this study underscores the global challenge of integrating AI
into education responsibly. It calls for a collective effort among educa-
tors, policymakers, and institutions to equip students with the ethical
understanding and digital competencies needed to thrive in an AI-driven
future. Through thoughtful integration, AI tools can not only enhance
educational  experiences  but  also  prepare  graduates  to  navigate  the
complexities of a rapidly evolving technological landscape with confi-
dence and integrity.

19. Conclusion

This  study  examined  how  first-year  nursing  students  reported
engaging with ChatGPT when it was included in a formal assessment
task. The findings suggest that students used the tool in varied ways to
support their writing, idea development, and understanding of complex
concepts,  while  also  recognising  the  need  to  verify  information  and
maintain academic integrity. These perspectives underscore the impor-
tance of supporting students to learn with AI, using it as a tool to aid
thinking and planning, rather than learning from AI in ways that replace
their own academic work.

For institutions, the findings highlight the need for clear guidelines,
consistent messaging, and purposeful instructional support to help stu-
dents navigate AI ethically and confidently. Coherent, ethically groun-
ded frameworks for AI integration will be essential as higher education
responds  to  the  growing  presence  of  generative  technologies.  By
embedding  opportunities  for  critical,  informed  AI  engagement  within
curriculum and assessment design, universities can help prepare grad-
uates to participate effectively in an AI-enhanced professional landscape
while upholding academic integrity and disciplinary standards.
