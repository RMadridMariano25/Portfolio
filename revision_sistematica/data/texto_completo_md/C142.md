State-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) can now answer conceptual textbook questions with near-perfect
accuracy and perform complex equation derivations, raising significant concerns for higher education. This study
evaluates  the  performance  of  LLMs  on  graduate-level  bioprocess  engineering  exams,  which  include  multiple-
choice, short-answer, and long-form questions requiring calculations. First, allowing students to use LLMs led
to a 36 % average score increase compared to exams taken with only textbooks and notes. Second, as students
gained  more  experience  using  LLMs,  their  performance  improved  further,  particularly  among  students  with
disabilities. Third, under optimized conditions on two exams, OpenAI’s GPT-4o scored approximately 70 out of
100,  while  more  advanced  models,  such  as  OpenAI  o1,  o3,  GPT-4.5,  Qwen3–235B-A22B,  and  DeepSeek  R1,
scored  above  84,  outperforming  96  %  of  human  test-takers.  This  indicates  that  students  with  access  to  more
capable AI tools may gain an unfair advantage. Fourth, we propose guidelines for developing exam questions that
are  less  susceptible  to  LLM-generated  solutions.  These  include  tasks  such  as  interpreting  graphical  biological
pathways, answering negatively worded conceptual questions, performing complex numerical calculations and
optimizations,  and  solving  open-ended  research  problems  that  demand  critical  thinking.  This  article  calls  for
urgent  reforms  to  bioprocess  engineering  education,  advocating  for  the  integration  of  LLM  literacy  through
hands-on activities that address both practical applications and ethical considerations.

1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are a subdomain of Artificial Intel-
ligence  (AI).  LLMs  like  OpenAI’s  GPT-4o  (OpenAI,  2023),  o1,  Qwen
(Yang  et  al.,  2025;  Yang  et  al.,  2024),  and  DeepSeek  R1  (Guo  et  al.,
2025),  have  significantly  infiltrated  higher  education.  Recent  studies
have highlighted the multifaceted impact of AI across various academic
settings.  For  example,  the  emergent  role  of  large  language  models
(LLMs) in higher education and research has been discussed (Alqahtani
et  al.,  2023).  Additionally,  recent research  explores  the  opportunities
and challenges that LLMs present for education, addressing both benefits
(e.g., enhancing student support) as well as ethical concerns (Kasneci
et al., 2023). Similarly, an examination of the implications of ChatGPT
and other LLMs in academia reveals their potential to enhance or disrupt
traditional  educational  practices  (Meyer  et  al.,  2023).  In  the  era  of
generative  AI,  instructors  must  reconsider  the  weighting  factors  for
homework and take-home exams/projects. Further studies investigated

the practical applications, ethical considerations, and future directions
of LLMs in education (Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2023; Baskara, 2023; Dai et al.
2023; Jeon and Lee, 2023; Perkins, 2023; Yan et al., 2024). In the field of
chemical engineering, integrating LLM into chemical engineering core
courses has been explored (Tsai et al., 2023). Recent study and survey
have tested GPT-3.5 as a useful tool for chemical engineering lab session,
highlighting  its  pitfalls,  challenges  and  opportunities  (Keith  et  al.,
2025). In medical education, LLMs can generate customized study plans
and explanations based on a student’s strengths and weaknesses, pro-
moting more efficient and effective learning (Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2023;
Scherr et al., 2023; Sriram et al., 2025). Other studies covered practical
teaching  issues  like  assessment  integrity  and  plagiarism  detection
(Bernabei et al., 2023; Nikolic et al., 2023).

Despite a wide range of studies on ethical and responsible incorpo-
ration of LLMs into higher education curricula, there remains gaps in the
literature. First, most existing studies focus on qualitative assessments,
or theoretical frameworks without providing data on how state-of-art

more  sophisticated,  traditional  question  formats  may  no  longer  be

need to better accommodate students’  needs  and future career devel-

backgrounds, LLMs can be used effectively to accommodate all students’

2. Methods

2.1. Preparing textbook homework question and answer key dataset

This study leveraged Bioprocess Engineering course (EECE 506) at
Washington  University  in  St.  Louis.  This  core  course  is  designed  for
graduate students from environmental engineering and chemical engi-
neering.  We  used  the  text  book  “Bioprocess  Engineering:  Basic  Con-
cepts”  (Third  Edition)  by  Shuler  and  Kargi  (2002) for  our  homework
questions and corresponding answer key. Tested questions were selected
from the end of each chapter and divided into smaller sub-questions for
focused  assessment.  Each  question  was  categorized  into  one  of  three
types:

1.  Conceptual Questions: These questions require explanations or brief
descriptions  about  terminologies  and  concepts  without  numerical
computations.

2.  Numerical Calculations: These questions involve performing step-by-
step mathematical calculations to arrive at a final numerical answer.
3.  Equation  Derivations:  These  questions  require  students  to  derive

equations using theories taught in class.

2.2. Using LLM for solving textbook questions and grading

The  textbook  questions  were  solved  using  OpenAI  API  with  two
models: gpt-4o-2024–08–06 and o1-preview-2024–09–12. A standard-
ized prompt: “Solve this problem as an exam problem. Be concise with
your answer.”, was used for all questions. For grading, two approaches
were employed. First, a human grader evaluated the responses accord-
ing to the answer key. Then second, both language models also graded
the  responses  according  to  the  answer  key,  and  their  scores  were
compared  with  the  human  graders  to  assess  the  consistency  between
human and AI evaluations. The grading scheme is either 0 or 1 point for
each question, where 0 is incorrect final answer and 1 is correct final
answer.  To  make  questions  more  challenging  for  LLMs,  we  added
redundant information, such as extra constants, equations, and data.

2.3. Real-world classroom exams with LLM

To assess the impact of LLMs on problem-solving, we designed in-
class  exams  with  two  parts  (both  parts  have  the  same  questions),
where 24 graduate students and 4 high school students participated in
the exam. In Part A (40 minutes), students answer questions using only
textbooks and class notes. In Part B (30 minutes), they can use GPT-4o
(via  the  university’s  generative  AI  platform)  to  revisit  questions  they
found challenging in Part A. Any response given in Part B overwrites the
corresponding answer from Part A, regardless of correctness. After the
exam,  students  completed  a  survey  collecting  data  on  their  academic
backgrounds. Besides human test takers, twelve LLMs were selected to
solve  the  exam  problems  by  authors.  Nine  of  them  are  cloud-based:
OpenAI  o1,  o3,  GPT-4o,  GPT-4.5,  o3  mini,  DeepSeek  R1,  DeepSeek
V3, Qwen2.5 Max, and Qwen3–235B-A22B. Three are locally deployed
on  a  PC  with  consumer-level  GPU:  DeepSeek  R1  14B  Qwen  Distilled,
Qwen  QwQ,  and  Qwen2.5  14B.  Then  a  human  grader  grades  all  re-
sponses according to an answer key.

2.4. Statistics test

The  statistical  analyses  were  conducted  using  Python.  A  series  of
propositions  were tested  to evaluate the  impact  of LLM  assistance on
exam performance. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was computed
to assess linear relationships between key variables. These included LLM
usage vs. overall performance, proportion of positive score gains vs. LLM
usage, and Part A exam baseline score vs LLM score gain. One-sample t-
tests  were  conducted  to  determine  whether  the  mean  score  gain  (or
question-specific  gain)  significantly  differed  from  zero.  To  further
compare performance across subgroups, an independent-samples t-test
was  used.  Exam  participants  were  divided  into  two  groups  based  on
whether  they  have  undergraduate  biology  background.  The  average
score gains of the two groups were compared. This analysis aimed to
reveal  whether  students  with  different  baseline  performance  levels
benefited  differently  from  the  LLM  assistance.  Additionally,  Levene’s
test was applied to assess the variances in total scores between groups.
Throughout the analyses, all tests were two-tailed. The underlying as-
sumptions for each test, such as normality for t-tests and linearity for
Pearson correlations, were considered during the analysis.

3. Results and discussion

3.1. LLM’s ability of solving textbook questions

Since the emergence of LLMs, one of the concerns is that they will
change education permanently, because LLMs can quickly solve home-
work/exam  questions  and  give  students  an  unfair  advantage.  In  fact,
solving  numerical problems and writing essays have been key  bench-
marks for evaluating LLM capabilities from the beginning. When GPT-4
was  launched  in  March  2023  (OpenAI,  2023),  its  groundbreaking
feature was its ability to pass standardized tests like the SAT, bar exams,
and MCAT by outperforming more than 90 % of human test-takers. In
this study, we focus on graduate level bioprocess engineering education
scenarios.  We  used  OpenAI  LLMs,  o1  and  GPT-4o,  to  solve  textbook
homework questions in each chapter of a bioprocess engineering text-
book (Table 1). In a typical course setting, the most common types of
homework  questions  are  conceptual  questions,  numerical  calculation
questions, and equation derivations. The distribution of these question
types  in the  textbook is  shown in Fig. 2a, with numerical calculation
questions  being  the  most.  In  chapters  covering  biological  basics,  cell
functions, metabolic pathways, and genetic alterations, most questions
are  conceptual.  Both  LLMs  demonstrated  exceptional  proficiency  in
answering these conceptual questions, achieving near 100 % accuracy.
This is not unexpected because scientific Q&A is a core benchmark for
LLMs,  and  developers  have  significantly  enhanced  LLM’s  factual
accuracy.

3.2. Insights from two real classroom exams

To examine the impact of generative AI on student performance in a
controlled classroom setting, we conducted an exam permitting AI/LLM
use for the first time in our university. The exam comprised 15 multiple-
choice  questions  (question  1–15),  8  short-answer  questions  (question
16–23), and 1 equation derivation (question 24). Numerical calculation
questions were excluded due to time constraints. The average score for
Part A (books and notes only) was 48.9 out of 100 (standard deviation
14.5).  Allowing  GPT-4o  in  Part  B  significantly  improved  student  per-
formance, raising the average score to 66.3 (standard deviation 11.1).
Interestingly,  increased  AI  usage  correlated  with  lower  overall  scores
(Fig. 3a).

A detailed analysis revealed that multiple-choice questions saw little
improvement  with  AI  assistance  (Fig.  3b).  In  contrast,  short-answer
questions showed substantial score increases (p < 0.001). The highest
gain  was  observed  in  the  equation  derivation  problem  on  non-
competitive enzyme inhibition. Additionally, in Fig. 3c, students with
lower baseline score in Part A seem to benefit more from AI assistance
(r = -0.39,  p = 0.061).  When  analyzing  the  proportion  of  questions
attempted  with  AI:  a  higher  proportion  of  AI-assisted  attempts  corre-
sponded  to  a  lower  success  rate  in  score  improvement  (r = -0.50,
p = 0.012). Although students gained more total score with more Part B
attempts,  those students  who heavily  relied  on AI  assistance  still had
lower total scores than those who did not. This data showed an urgent
need for AI literacy which involves understanding not only how to use
AI/LLM tools but also when and why to use them. Students need to learn
to  discern  which  tasks  benefit  more  from  AI  assistance  and  critically
evaluate LLM outputs.

After the first exam permitting LLM usage, students gained experi-
ence in formulating effective prompts and using LLM tools. We admin-
istered  a  second  exam  one  month  later.  Students’  usage  strategy
improved. In the first exam, students who used the LLM more tended to
score lower, suggesting that they initially struggled with effective usage.
However, in the second exam, their strategy improved (Fig. 4a), where
there is a moderate positive correlation between LLM attempts and total
score  (r = 0.40,  p = 0.056).  Fig.  4b  showed  a  similar  positive  trend,
demonstrating that students were able to achieve, on average, positive
gains  across  all  questions.  Fig.  4d  highlights  a  strong  correlation  be-
tween total score gain and LLM usage attempts (r = 0.78, p = 0.000), a
notable improvement compared to the first exam. LLM usage requires
practice, and the more students engage with it, the better they become.
To prepare students for future careers, it is essential to integrate AI lit-
eracy and practical application into higher education.

3.4. LLMs in zero-shot exam settings: performance, equality, and security

In addition to student test takers, nine large language models (LLMs)
were engaged in the exam under best practices by an experienced user.
Nine of these were cloud-based models—OpenAI o1, GPT-4o, GPT-4.5,
o3,  o3  mini,  DeepSeek  R1,  DeepSeek  V3,  Qwen2.5  Max,  and
Qwen3.5–235B-A22B—while three, DeepSeek R1 14B Qwen Distilled,
Qwen QWQ and Qwen 2.5 14B, were deployed locally on a consumer-
grade  GPU  (Table  1).  The  LLM  outputs  were  directly  graded  without
any processing. However, this study does not aim to provide a rigorous
LLM  benchmark.  LLM  outputs  are  inherently  stochastic,  and  various
parameters—such  as  quantization,  temperature,  top-k  sampling,  and
context length—can significantly influence performance. Our result is a
typical use case under zero-shot condition. This disparity among LLMs
can lead to social inequality where economically privileged students can
gain  more  from  paid  version  of  AI/LLM  tools.  Notably,  the  locally
deployed DeepSeek R1 14B and Qwen QwQ achieved scores comparable

to  some  cloud-based  models,  likely  due  to  recent  advances  in  model
distillation  and  chain-of-thought  reasoning.  Even  a  relatively  small
model like Qwen QwQ can now surpass the class average, suggesting
that  offline  exams  without  internet  access  may  be  compromised  by
students using local LLM on portable laptops with GPU (or Mac with
unified  memory).  This  may  lead  to  social  inequality,  where  students
with financial advantage can pay for better computation hardware to
win.

3.5. Designing difficult questions for LLMs

Through  constructing  real-classroom  homework  dataset  and  sys-
tematic analysis, we got some ideas about LLM’s behavior when solving
textbook questions. Now one question arises, how can instructor design
questions that are difficult for LLMs. We analyzed their performance on
real-classroom  homework  datasets  and  found  that  LLMs  sometimes
struggle with redundant information in question statements. By adding
irrelevant details such as extra constants and equations in the question
statements, o1’s correct rate was reduced by 31 %. Also, given that LLMs
lack specific course context, instructors can incorporate recent research
and publications. This approach encourages students to engage deeply
with the literature and course material rather than paying attention to
LLM-generated  contents.  In  the  class  exam,  LLMs  exhibited  several
common errors. We summarized our findings in Table 2.

(as the key component of DNA), indicating that subtle wording changes
can  exploit  weaknesses  in  vector-based  word  embeddings.  LLMs
frequently isolate single  factors while neglecting interdependent vari-

3.6. Feasibility of LLM as grader

One  of  the  time-consuming  works  in  higher  education  is  grading
student  homework, and  it is  more difficult to hire  teaching assistants
these days due to lack of qualified candidates. We explored the feasi-
bility of LLM as grader. When given answer keys, GPT-4o grader and o1
grader had average discrepancies of 46 ± 1 % and 27 ± 1 % compared
to a human grader. LLMs tend to assign scores rather than clearly label
answers as incorrect. Also, students may make small markings to assist

138

their  thinking process,  and  when  LLM reading  scanned  exams, LLM’s
attention mechanism cannot attend to all the markings. At this stage,
LLMs are not reliable graders, and human supervision is strongly rec-
ommended if they are to be used in this role.

3.7. Class survey about AI/LLM usage

94 %  of  the  students  on  the  course  found  ChatGPT  to  be  more
accessible and helpful throughout the course to assist with homework
and  exams  (Fig.  5).  Other  AI/LLMs  used  were  Copilot  and  Deepseek.
While most students didn’t require AI to solve excel calculation prob-
lems,  they  used  AI  to  solve  computer  programming  problems.  Many
students  reported  that  writing  an  effective  prompt  to  get  a  precise
answer requires significant time and effort. It also demands fundamental
knowledge of the subject to ask the appropriate questions and to proof-
check the answer. Learning the skills to format the right prompt requires
practice. Encouraging the use of AI can help students practice such skills.
Therefore, learning to use AI effectively can be useful for further cour-
sework  and  future  career.  It  was  further  mentioned  that  not  all  AI
models can process information with equal efficiency, and that it would
be fairer if everyone could use the same model. This highlights the po-
tential  inequality  faced  by  students  who  cannot  pay  for  advanced  AI
models, which may put them at significant academic disadvantage over
students who can afford expensive models. To promote equal opportu-
nities in academic performance, institutions should provide all students
with access to advanced AI models, as this would eliminate the dispar-
ities in performance caused by limited access to the advanced AI models.

3.8. MATLAB coding tasks in bioprocess engineering course

In bioprocess engineering course, one topic is on simulation of time
course  bioreactor  fermentation  data  by  solving  ordinary  differential
equations.  Chemical  engineering  education  curricula  has  emphasized
basic coding skills in MATLAB/Simulink and Python. MATLAB prepares
students for process simulation and differential equation solving, while
Python is essential for data-driven studies and machine learning. How-
ever, with the advent of coding LLMs like Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT
and Qwen code, basic coding courses have become less relevant. Stu-
dents  can  now  write  code  using  natural  language  instructions.  For
example,  our  course  had  a  MATLAB  assignment  for  simulating  two-
species  predator-prey  population  dynamics,  and  students  took  advan-
tage of GPT-4o for generating correct code instantly. Such assignments,
typically given as take-home homework, now need to be reconsidered.
Instructors  should  instead  demonstrate  LLM  code  generation  and
encourage students to pursue higher-level coding objectives.

3.9. Concluding remark: promoting AI literacy

This study reveals both the transformative potential and limitations
of  LLMs  in  bioprocess  engineering  education.  Classroom  exams
demonstrated that LLM access improves student grades over time, but
LLMs still struggle with long numerical calculations. Equality concerns
emerge from disparities in LLM quality, as premium models outperform
free alternatives, thereby granting academic advantages for financially
privileged students. AI/LLM’s vulnerability to contextually rich, critical-
thinking  questions  suggests  that  curricula  must  evolve  to  emphasize
higher-order  skills  and  integrate  AI  literacy.  To  ensure  that  students
develop practical skills, we designed a lecture module introducing LLMs
within the bioprocess engineering course (See GitHub page https://gith
ub.com/xiao-zhengyang/EECE506-SP2025).  These  lectures  cover  the
history of LLMs, key terminologies, and fundamental concepts necessary
for  understanding  LLM  capabilities  and  limitations.  In  addition,  we
incorporate  hands-on  activities  where  students  learn  how  to  interact
with LLMs across different platforms (online or on a personal computer).
A key component of this module is a live demonstration of GraphRAG,
an advanced retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system that reads
