The rapid advancement of GenAI has created unprecedented opportunities to enhance student
learning through large language models (LLMs). While LLM-based conversational agents have
demonstrated potential in improving accessibility and instructional support, evidence of their
impact on higher-order thinking remains limited, especially within the context of Chinese higher
education. This study introduces the Socratic Intelligent Conversational Agent (S-ICA), which
integrates Socratic questioning strategies with semantic generation mechanisms to promote
student learning and reflective thinking in disciplinary contexts. A total of 94 university students
from China participated in the study, with random assignment to an experimental group (EG)
using  the  S-ICA  and  a  control  group  (CG)  using  a  non-Socratic  conversational  agent  (nS-
ICA). The results showed that the EG outperformed the CG in both academic achievement
and reflective thinking, particularly in the dimensions of ‘‘reflection’’ and ‘‘critical reflection’’.
Cognitive network analysis revealed that the EG students activated more advanced reflective
pathways,  linking  understanding,  reflection,  and  critical  reflection  processes.  Although  no
significant differences were found in learning motivation between the groups, interviews with
students  indicated  that  the  S-ICA  facilitated  more  effective  learning  and  deeper  reflective
engagement. These findings contribute to research on integrating classical pedagogical strategies
into GenAI-based systems, offering insights into how such technologies can foster higher-order
thinking and guide the design of future collaborative human–AI learning systems.

1. Introduction

Reflective thinking, as a critical higher-order thinking ability, has been widely recognized as fundamental for solving complex
problems, fostering deep learning, and promoting innovation (Bauer, 1991; Dewey, 1922). It involves an active and sustained process
through which individuals critically examine and evaluate their own experiences and beliefs, allowing cognitive restructuring and
knowledge reconstruction (Schön, 1995). An increasing emphasis within higher education highlights the importance of cultivating
students’ ability to reflect to better prepare them for increasingly dynamic and uncertain environments (Harvey et al., 2016).
Empirical evidence has shown that learners with well-developed reflective thinking skills achieve deeper conceptual understanding,
greater transfer, and improved problem solving in novel contexts (Askell-Williams et al., 2007). To this end, diverse pedagogical

et al., 2021). Nevertheless, while digital technologies—and more recently advanced GenAI systems—offer promising opportunities

Recent advances in GenAI, particularly large language models (LLMs), offer new opportunities to address educational challenges

relevant dialogue, provide immediate feedback, and deliver personalized and adaptive learning support (Kasneci et al., 2023).
Empirical studies have reported encouraging results, showing that such agents can improve conceptual understanding, problem
solving abilities, and knowledge transfer (Wang et al., 2025). (Liu et al., 2024). However, concerns have also emerged that excessive

dimensions such as motivation and perceptions, areas that remain not adequately explored in authentic higher education contexts
(Yu, 2023). Therefore, the integration of GenAI into education must address both cognitive and affective dimensions to fully
understand its impact on student learning (Liu et al., 2024).

The integration of advanced AI technologies into the pedagogy requires careful alignment with robust instructional principles
(Barrot, 2023; Hu et al., 2025). Researchers are increasingly focusing on the fact that learners should be guided through exploratory
questioning and scaffolded instructions, rather than provided with ready-made solutions (Borchers et al., 2025). Among such
approaches, the Socratic method, rooted in probing questions that encourage learners to articulate reasoning, justify claims, and
confront inconsistencies, has long been recognized as effective for cultivating critical and reflective thinking (Hsu et al., 2022).
Recent work has begun to explore how Socratic strategies can be implemented through agents powered by LLM. For example, Kuo
et al. (2026) documented the application of the TALPer system in fifth-grade mathematics education, where it employed the Socratic
dialogue method and carefully designed questioning strategies to foster reflective thinking and enhance mathematical understanding
among students. However, most studies have focused on knowledge acquisition, with limited attention to how Socratic agents foster
higher-order cognitive processes such as reflection and metacognitive awareness. Moreover, while Socratic dialogue was designed
for one-to-one interactions, adapting it to group settings is essential for ecological validity. Although group dynamics may pose
challenges like unequal participation, they also facilitate collaborative reflection and critical thinking (Fiock, 2020). Our design
mitigated these risks by incorporating structured worksheets and turn-taking scaffolds to ensure active participation in small groups,
thus supporting personalized and collaborative learning.

Against this backdrop, the present study introduced a Socratic Intelligent Conversational Agent (S-ICA) that integrated Socratic
questioning strategies with LLM technologies to support inquiry-based learning in university classrooms. To rigorously evaluate its
effectiveness, we contrasted the S-ICA with a carefully defined non-Socratic ICA (nS-ICA). Both agents operated under identical
conditions in terms of tasks, time allocation, and interface; the only intended pedagogical difference was that the S-ICA employed
Socratic questioning while the nS-ICA provided direct answers and clarifications without reflective prompts. We emphasize that the
nS-ICA refers specifically to the control condition in this study and is not intended to be generalized to all non-Socratic agents. This
design isolates the contribution of Socratic questioning while minimizing potential confounding.

The study assesses both cognitive and affective outcomes. At the cognitive level, we investigate whether the S-ICA enhances
academic performance and reflective thinking. At the affective level, we explore how the S-ICA influences students’ motivation and
learning perceptions—critical yet underexplored dimensions in AI in education. Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) was employed
to model the co-occurrence patterns of cognitive categories in dialogue, a widely recognized method in educational research
for  examining  learning  processes  and  knowledge  construction.  By  analyzing  connections  among  reflective  categories  such  as
understanding, reflection, and critical reflection, ENA provides insights into how students’ cognitive engagement evolves during
collaborative tasks. In this study, we apply Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to reveal distinct cognitive patterns and the underlying
mechanisms through which ICAs integrating Socratic prompting promote deeper reflective engagement, shedding light on how
these cognitive pathways differ between the experimental and control groups. These outcomes are critical to understanding how
personalized and adaptive learning support in GenAI-powered agents can promote sustained engagement and deeper learning. To
this end, the following research questions were addressed:

1. Does the S-ICA approach improve learners’ academic performance compared to the nS-ICA?
2. Does the S-ICA approach foster learners’ reflective thinking compared to the nS-ICA?
3. Does the S-ICA approach enhance learners’ learning motivation compared to the nS-ICA?
4. How do students perceive their learning experience when interacting with the two types of ICAs?

2. Literature review

2.1. Reflective thinking

Reflective thinking is defined as the capacity to actively and consciously analyze, evaluate, and integrate one’s experiences,
knowledge, and cognitive processes (Boyd & Fales, 1983; Paul, 2013). From different theoretical perspectives, reflective thinking
has been conceptualized as comprising multiple levels and dimensions. For example, Mezirow (1998) and Kember (2000) categorized
reflective thinking into four progressive stages: habitual action, understanding, reflection, and critical reflection. Among these four
stages, the first two emphasize the construction of meaning from experience by learners and are considered non-reflective behaviors,

whereas the latter two involve the reevaluation of actions or ideas in response to problems or situations and are regarded as reflective
behaviors. Furthermore, Mezirow (1997) defined two hierarchical levels of reflective action, in which the higher and more essential
level—critical reflection—serves as the foundation for deep learning and the assessment of success by learners.

Reflective thinking has been widely recognized in higher education as a key psychological mechanism that promotes deep
learning, self-directed learning, and complex problem solving (Ghanizadeh, 2017). Learners who possess reflective thinking skills are
able to regulate their own learning and recognize the gap between what they know and what they need to know. Reflection plays a
vital role in the learning process, helping learners improve academic achievement (Menekse, 2020), improve metacognitive abilities
(Nobutoshi, 2023), and develop cross-context transfer capabilities. According to research on higher-order thinking, educational
approaches that foster reflective thinking can increase student engagement and participation in flipped learning designs (Chen
et al., 2019).

Among current instructional media, GenAI technologies are regarded as having new potential to support reflective learning due
to their semantic interactivity and language expression capabilities. Albert and Li (2023) noted that AI instructional agents such
as ChatGPT can provide immediate feedback, guide questions, and suggestions for the organization of ideas in contexts where
teacher–student interaction is limited, thus promoting reflective behaviors. However, current research on AI-supported reflection
has primarily focused on technological feasibility and user acceptance, with limited exploration of the underlying mechanisms by
which such tools contribute to the development of higher-order thinking.

2.2. Learning outcomes of university students

Academic performance and learning motivation are critical indicators for evaluating students’ learning outcomes and their
sustained engagement. Academic performance reflects the depth of knowledge acquisition of students, the proficiency of their
skills and the level of their problem-solving abilities (Alexandre & Enslin, 2017). Learning motivation directly influences the
ability of learners to self-regulation, their investment in learning activities, and persistence over time (Schunk & Zimmerman,
2012). In university learning environments, particularly, students’ ability to engage in self-directed learning and maintain long-term
motivation is essential for academic success.

However, with the widespread adoption of GenAI in education, significant changes have emerged in academic performance
and learning motivation. On the one hand, GenAI tools offer immediate feedback, personalized learning pathways, and adaptive
resources, which have been shown to improve academic performance and motivation (Derakhshan, 2025; Gao et al., 2024). For
example, ICAs have helped students clarify conceptual misunderstandings in real time, fostering deeper comprehension and mastery
of knowledge (Zhai & Wibowo, 2023). On the other hand, excessive reliance on AI-generated answers can lead to overdependence,
diminishing critical thinking, exploration, and potentially undermining intrinsic motivation (Han, 2026).

Moreover, understanding students’ learning perceptions—their attitudes and beliefs about GenAI tools, is crucial to evaluating
the effectiveness of these technologies. These perceptions are strongly influenced by the degree of personalization and adaptivity
offered by the learning tool. Studies have shown that students’ perceptions of AI’s role significantly affect their engagement and
motivation. Positive perceptions, in which students view AI as a supportive learning tool, increase motivation and foster engagement
(Liu et al., 2024). In contrast, students who perceive AI as a crutch may feel less confident in their abilities, reducing motivation
and engagement in the learning process. Therefore, further exploration of learning perceptions and motivation is essential, as these
factors influence the sustainability of AI-supported learning and its impact on higher-order thinking skills (Yu, 2023).

2.3. Socratic questioning strategy

Socratic questioning is a pedagogical philosophy and instructional strategy centered on inquiry, emphasizing cognitive stim-
ulation and intellectual challenge. It encourages learners to actively construct knowledge and engage in self-reflection through
a process of progressive questioning (Hu, 2023). At its core, it is a dialogic method of cognitive training (Clark & Egan, 2015),
designed to provoke learners to question, examine, and ultimately reconstruct their existing beliefs. The key characteristics of
Socratic questioning include open inquiry, progressive exploration, and the educator’s role as a facilitator of thought, elements
that make it particularly effective for fostering higher-order thinking (Dalim et al., 2022).

Empirical studies have shown the efficacy of Socratic questioning in improving students’ reflective awareness and critical thinking
skills. For example, Hsu et al. (2022) found that structured Socratic reflection prompts significantly enhanced elementary school
students’ critical thinking skills. In an experimental study with secondary school students, Yang and Chung (2009) reported notable
improvements in critical reasoning and self-expression after sustained Socratic dialogue sessions. Beyond the sciences, this method
has gained traction in fields such as English writing instruction, teacher education, and medical ethics, where it facilitates deeper
reflection on beliefs, judgments, and actions within authentic learning contexts (Dinkins & Cangelosi, 2019; Ho et al., 2023).

The pedagogical value of Socratic questioning has also been amplified through its integration with digital technologies. For
example, Chang, Hung, and Yang (2024) developed an online writing platform incorporating Socratic prompts, which significantly
increased students’ frequency of reflection and depth of written output. In a controlled experiment, Chang, Hung, and Yang (2024)
demonstrated that an AI-based dialogue system employing Socratic questioning outperformed traditional feedback mechanisms
in  supporting  students’  reflective  writing.  Despite  its  promise,  the  practical  implementation  of  Socratic  questioning  presents
challenges. Teachers must have strong questioning skills and classroom management skills to facilitate deep and flexible thinking.
Simultaneously, learners may experience cognitive overload, leading to disengagement or shallow responses. As a result, optimizing
the pacing of questions, personalizing prompts, and sustaining cognitive engagement through technological mediation have become
critical areas for future research on integrating Socratic strategies into contemporary instructional design.

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) in recent years has garnered significant attention for their application
in educational contexts (Chang et al., 2024). As a hallmark of GenAI, LLMs possess advanced semantic comprehension and natural
language generation capabilities, enabling their integration into intelligent educational agents that offer adaptive instructional
support, automated feedback, and personalized learning pathways (Hu et al., 2024). By leveraging the dialogic capabilities of LLMs,
these agents can engage in human-like interactions with learners, which are crucial to fostering personalized learning experiences
(Zheng et al., 2025).

Emerging research has demonstrated the efficacy of LLM-driven educational agents in tasks such as language writing, problem
solving, and critical thinking development (Liebenow et al., 2025; Razafinirina et al., 2024). However, the effectiveness of LLM-
powered agents in fostering higher-order cognitive skills and strategic learning remains contested. Some studies highlight the
advantages of AI-generated feedback in helping learners identify cognitive gaps and misconceptions (Naseer & Khawaja, 2025).
However, concerns have been raised that excessive reliance on such agents can reduce cognitive effort and hinder self-regulation
engagement, particularly in open tasks, where learners could substitute AI-generated responses for deeper cognitive processing (Zhai
et al., 2024).

Although some evidence suggests that agents driven by LLM can improve cognitive engagement by providing continuous feedback
and heuristic prompts (Liebenow et al., 2025), the mechanisms through which they influence multidimensional learning outcomes,
such as motivation, self-regulation, and perceptions of learning, remain underexplored. This gap underscores the need for agent
designs that effectively integrate LLM capabilities with dynamic pedagogical principles, balancing guidance, dialogic feedback, and
reflective stimulation within a multilevel cognitive support framework. Notably, while much of the research on conversational agents
has focused on traditional, non-LLM models, these agents often lack the personalized adaptability that LLMs can provide. Non-LLM
systems, while valuable for task-specific feedback, are generally less flexible in tailoring interactions to learners’ evolving needs,
which may limit their capacity to foster long-term cognitive and affective engagement.

In response, the present study introduces the S-ICA, a hybrid design that incorporates LLM capabilities within the structured
scaffolding of the Socratic method. This approach aims to enhance not only cognitive outcomes but also affective dimensions such as
motivation and learning perceptions, which are critical but often underexplored in current GenAI-enhanced learning environments.

3. LLM-based S-ICA learning approach

3.1. Architecture of the S-ICA

In this study, we developed an LLM-driven S-ICA to assist students in evaluating and refining their experimental research
proposals. The system architecture is depicted in Fig.  1. Instructors can provide students with learning resources and materials, such
as instructional documents on experimental research design, task instructions, question templates, and sample proposals, which are
integrated into the agent’s knowledge database for access on demand. Students interact with the S-ICA to evaluate and optimize their
original research proposals. This interaction takes place through a web browser, ensuring compatibility on both desktop and mobile
devices, without the need for a dedicated mobile app. They input various components of their proposals, such as research questions,
hypothesis statements and experimental methods, into the user interface. These inputs are then processed by the back-end system,
where the large language model generates contextually appropriate feedback to guide students in refining their design rationale.
The S-ICA employs predefined prompts and dialogue structures, with adaptive questioning based on students’ input. This enables a
personalized learning experience, as feedback and questions evolve according to the progress and needs of the learner. In this way,
the system adapts to the characteristics of individual students, providing tailored guidance that reflects their current understanding
and challenges.

User Interface. Fig.  2 illustrated the main interaction platform of the system: the Doubao web interface. Built on Doubao, a
ByteDance-affiliated LLM platform, the S-ICA interface embeds Socratic dialogue and questioning strategies at its core. The system
supports text input and multi-turn interaction, enabling students to engage in continuous reflection and iterative improvement
during the experimental proposal evaluation task. Although the system also supports voice input, all student interactions in this
study were conducted via text input through a web browser. After logging in to Doubao, students submit various components of
their proposals, such as research questions, hypotheses, and experimental methods, via text input. The system, guided by predefined
prompts and dialogue structures, generates real-time feedback, including counter-questions, clarification requests, and progressive
inquiries. These adaptive prompts encourage students to reflect on the logical coherence and completeness of their proposals, guiding
them through an iterative process of revision and refinement. Additionally, S-ICA tracks and stores the complete dialogue history,
allowing students to review previous exchanges at any time, which supports ongoing revisions and reflective analysis.

Core Functional Modules. The S-ICA system was developed with a strong pedagogical orientation, integrating the Socratic
method with LLM technology to construct an intelligent conversational agent with cognitive scaffolding capabilities. It incorporates
four core functional modules: input comprehension, dialogue generation, dynamic feedback, and structural memory to support the
ongoing reflection and knowledge construction during the evaluation of experimental research designs.

(1) input comprehension module: this module processes text input from students and performs semantic parsing, intent recognition,
and a preliminary assessment of student cognitive level. For example, when a student inputs a research question, the system identifies
whether it is descriptive (‘‘What is. . . ?’’) or causal (‘‘Why/How does...?’’) and uses this classification to determine the stage of inquiry.
Such classification provides the foundation for the generation of subsequent prompts. The design of this module follows adaptive

Prompt Engineering Design. The prompting mechanism operationalizes the dialogue generation and dynamic feedback modules
through a workflow-based design. Each workflow node corresponds to a component of the research design task (e.g., research
questions, hypotheses, variables, methods, etc.). When learner input contains specific trigger keywords (e.g., ‘‘hypothesis’’, ‘‘sample
size’’, ‘‘data collection’’), the system automatically activates the corresponding workflow and retrieves a tailored prompt template.
For example, in the hypothesis formulation stage, an input containing causal terms such as ‘‘effect’’ or ‘‘impact’’ triggers prompts
like: ‘‘What mechanism links your independent and dependent variables?’’. Similarly, in the methods stage, references to ‘‘sample size’’
activate prompts such as: ‘‘How will you justify the adequacy of your chosen sample for detecting significant effects?’’. These templates
are pre-configured but dynamically adapted in real time to align with the student’s context and previous dialogue history.

The design was iteratively refined through pilot testing. Students interacted with prototype versions of the system and provided
feedback on prompt clarity, difficulty, and relevance. Their feedback led to two key revisions: (1) broadening the knowledge
base of the system and integrating a controlled web search interface to expand the scope of responses, and (2) reorganizing the
prompt library into a matrix structure. The tiered structure progresses from clarification to justification (e.g., ‘‘Why is gamification
an appropriate intervention for this population?’’), and finally to refinement (e.g., ‘‘How will you control for differences in prior
knowledge?’’).

In addition to workflow rules, each prompt template encodes explicit skills and constraints. For example, skills include guiding
the student to articulate their reasoning, initiating clarification before advancing the dialogue, and encouraging reflection rather
than passive acceptance. Constraints ensure pedagogical integrity: all prompts must follow the ‘‘Thinking question:’’ format, and
the system is prohibited from directly providing answers to students. These specifications guarantee that the interaction remains
inquiry-driven, aligns with Socratic principles, and fosters adaptive learning through structured, yet open-ended questioning.

3.2. S-ICA learning approach

3.2.1. Application context

The instructional practice in this study was designed as an extracurricular self-directed learning activity that extended the formal
curriculum on research design theory and methodology. After completing foundational coursework, second-year undergraduates
majoring in Educational Technology engaged in a six-week task to collaboratively construct a comprehensive experimental research
proposal. The task was structured into weekly stages, each targeting a specific subtask: (1) clarifying the research question, (2)
formulating hypotheses, (3) identifying variables, (4) designing implementation procedures and (5) evaluating the overall design.
The students were organized into teams of five to six, each member responsible for contributing to the group proposal. To ensure
equity between conditions, all students followed identical task instructions, used the same learning materials, and accessed the
agents through a common institutional portal and browser-based interface.

Agent conditions and interaction characteristics. To evaluate the impact of ICAs on academic performance, reflective thinking and
learning motivation, students were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: an experimental group (EG) and a control group
(CG). Both groups received identical task requirements, time allocations, materials, and user interface. The sole intended pedagogical
difference concern the agent’s feedback strategy.

• S-ICA (EG). The S-ICA implemented a Socratic questioning strategy: it prioritized clarification, justification, and refine-
ment through reciprocal prompts rather than supplying immediate answers. Typical turns included brief counter-questions
(e.g., ‘What assumptions underlie this hypothesis’, ‘How would you operationalize this variable in measurable terms’) that
pressed students to articulate reasoning, examine coherence, and iteratively improve design decisions.

• nS-ICA (CG). The nS-ICA provided direct, task-aligned feedback and procedural clarifications without reflective or recursive
prompts. Its turns focused on definitional guidance, checklists, and examples relevant to the submitted content (e.g. ‘This
variable can be measured via standardized test scores’ ‘A feasible data collection procedure is a classroom survey with informed
consent’), without follow-up probing, metacognitive cues, or dialogic escalation. In other words, the nS-ICA could provide
practical cognitive, behavioral, or procedural guidance, but it deliberately did not engage in Socratic inquiry.

Model platforms and parity controls. The two agents were deployed with different LLM backends (Doubao for the S-ICA and
ERNIE for the nS-ICA), due to institutional access constraints and token-based interface compatibility, rather than deliberate
pedagogical differentiation. Despite this back-end divergence, both agents were delivered through an identical COZE-based web
platform interface, ensuring uniformity in access mode, operational procedures, and user experience. Parity controls were also
applied by aligning the task design, learning materials, and usage protocols between conditions. Consequently, the only intended
experimental manipulation concerns the feedback strategy, thus strengthening the attribution of any observed group differences to
the presence or absence of Socratic questioning, rather than to disparities in interface or system access.

Prior to the main study, the S-ICA was pilot-tested in two stages. First, five master’s and doctoral-level students in Educational
Technology reviewed the system to evaluate the clarity and consistency of Socratic prompts, leading to iterative refinements of
prompt wording and sequencing. Second, one undergraduate team engaged in a pre-class trial session, during which real-time
feedback was collected and used to further optimize the system’s usability and alignment with classroom dynamics. This multi-stage
pilot process ensured that the implemented version of the S-ICA was both pedagogically valid and technically robust.

3.2.2. Learning activities

Pedaste et al. (2015) propose an inquiry-based learning (IBL) framework comprising five general phases: Orientation, Concep-
tualization, Investigation, Conclusion, and Discussion. This cycle provides learners with a structured and scaffolded approach to
inquiry activities and has been shown to support the development of complex inquiry-driven learning processes, as well as inform
the design of instructional guidance that optimizes learning efficiency. Following the IBL framework, students begin the inquiry
process with Orientation, during which they not only familiarize themselves with the topic to be explored but also gain a deeper
understanding of the problem that needs to be addressed. In this study, a scaffolded inquiry-based learning model was adopted
for the self-practice phase. The experimental group used the S-ICA to facilitate each inquiry stage, enabling students to iteratively
refine and revise their experimental proposals. Given that the students did not have enough time to conduct actual experiments,
collect data, and perform data analysis, the original ‘‘Conclusion and Data Interpretation’’ phase was omitted. Instead, the student

4. Method

4.1. Research background and participants

This study was implemented during the fall semester of 2024 in a university of teachers in central China. Participants were
second-year undergraduates majoring in Educational Technology who were enrolled in the required course Research Methods in
Educational Technology. The course lasted 16 weeks and consisted of weekly lecture sessions and practical training sessions. The
instruction covered a variety of topics, including research design, literature review, survey and experimental methodologies, and
data analysis, ensuring that the students had a sufficient foundation to participate in the experimental task designed for this study. A
total of 94 students participated. Forty-eight students (28 female; mean age = 19.00 ± 0.22 years) were assigned to the experimental
group, the remaining 46 students (25 female; mean age = 19.00 ± 0.15 years) formed the control group. In both conditions, the
agents were accessed through the same institutional portal and browser interface to minimize extraneous differences; the intended
pedagogical contrast only concerned the feedback strategy, i.e., Socratic questioning versus direct clarifications.

All  participants  were  randomly  assigned  to  16  subgroups  of  five  to  six  students  each.  Within  these  subgroups,  students
collaborated to design and refine an experimental research proposal over a six-week intervention period. The use of group-based
tutoring reflected authentic classroom practices in the course and enabled manageable implementation across a relatively large
cohort. However, group work necessarily involves the potential interdependence of data between students within the same subgroup.
To address this issue, statistical analyzes explicitly incorporated group membership as a clustering factor and employed models that
account for nested data structures, thereby mitigating risks of inflated significance due to non-independence.

The intervention period lasted six weeks, during which the students iteratively developed their research proposals in weekly
stages. Although relatively brief in duration, the design was intentionally aligned with the temporal constraints of the semester
schedule and the scope of the course project. As such, the findings are interpreted as evidence of short-term impacts within a
defined academic context, and the limitations of long-term transferability and generalizability are acknowledged in the discussion.
Throughout the study, both groups were instructed by the same professor, who had over 20 years of teaching experience, and
followed an identical syllabus to ensure the equivalence of the instructional context. All participants had prior experience using
GenAI tools. Before starting the study, ethical approval was obtained from the university’s Institutional Review Board.

4.2. Experimental procedure

Fig.  8 illustrates the overall experimental procedure. Oriented to theoretical instruction and proposal design for experimental
research methods, we implemented a quasi-experimental intervention lasting six weeks. Each week consisted of two 45 min theory
sessions and two 45 min practical sessions. The intervention was divided into three phases: a pre-test phase, an intervention phase
using ICAs, and a post-test phase.

Phase 1 (Pretest):  All 94 students completed the pre-test measures, which included questionnaires that assessed reflective
thinking and learning motivation, as well as a knowledge pre-test on experimental research methods (30 min). Independent samples
t-tests confirmed that, before the test, there were no significant differences between the experimental and control groups in reflective
thinking (t = 0.45, 𝑝 = 0.65), learning motivation (t = 0.30, 𝑝 = 0.76), or knowledge test scores (t = 0.26, 𝑝 = 0.795 > 0.01).

Phase 2 (Intervention): Both groups participated in weekly theoretical lectures and self-practice sessions. During theory lectures
(90 min per week), students in both groups attended face-to-face instruction in a smart classroom, where they were taught the
fundamental principles, core concepts, and typical methodologies of experimental research. In each self-practice session, the students
were required to use the assigned ICAs to evaluate and progressively refine an experimental research design proposal.

Before starting the task, all participants received a 15 min training session that provided unified instruction on system operation,
task template structure, and the protocol to initiate interaction. Specifically, every student was instructed to start by entering the

learners and their respective agents and conducted semi-structured interviews with selected students to gain deeper insight into
their learning experiences.

4.3. Measurement instruments

The instruments used in this study comprised pre- and post-test knowledge assessments, a learning motivation questionnaire,
and a reflective thinking questionnaire. In addition to these standardized measures, all dialogue data were derived exclusively from
text-based interactions, thereby eliminating variability due to input modality, and were subsequently coded for cognitive network
analysis. Semi-structured interviews were also conducted to provide qualitative insights into participants’ learning experiences.
Finally, the academic performance of the students was evaluated through the quality of their completed experimental research
proposals.

4.3.1. Academic performance

Within  the  inquiry-based  learning  process,  a  student’s  mastery  of  conceptual  knowledge  reflects  both  the  depth  of  their
understanding  and  the  development  of  research  competencies.  Consequently,  conceptual  knowledge  scores  were  chosen  as  a
key indicator of student academic performance. The pretest and posttest instruments were designed around the core content of
experimental research methods in educational technology, covering essential concepts such as the logic of experimental design,
variable types and operational definitions, random assignment procedures, validity and reliability issues in experimental research,
and basic statistical analysis techniques. Both tests were co-developed by two experts with extensive experience in educational

technology research and teaching. They were then reviewed for content validity by one full professor and one associate professor;
their evaluation confirmed that the tests demonstrated strong validity.

In addition, the quality of the experimental research proposal of each group served as an indicator of academic performance and
research capability; it was therefore considered a critical student outcome. To evaluate the quality of the proposal, this study adapted
the research proposal scoring rubric developed by Zhang et al. (2019), which has been widely used in the instruction in educational
technology and research methods to assess students’ design skills and the quality of academic writing. In line with the context of the
course, the rubric was modified to encompass six core dimensions (see Appendix 7): (1) Research topic, (2) Research question, (3)
Theoretical rationale, (4) Research content, (5) Research methodology and (6) Research procedures. Each dimension was rated using
a five-level scale ranging from 2 to 10 points, with higher scores indicating better performance. Specifically, ratings were assigned in
increments of two points (2 = poor, 4 = needs improvement, 6 = satisfactory, 8 = good and 10 = excellent). Higher scores indicate
higher quality. The total possible score for each proposal was 60 points. The rubric was reviewed by the course instructor and an
additional expert in educational technology, both of whom confirmed its appropriateness for evaluating the quality of students’
research designs. To ensure objectivity, two teaching assistants independently scored each proposal. Inter-rater reliability, assessed
by Cohen’s kappa (𝜅 = 0.82), indicated substantial agreement between raters (Cohen, 1988; Landis & Koch, 1977).

4.3.2. Reflective thinking

To assess the reflective thinking ability of the students, we employed a scale adapted from Kember et al. (1999), which measures
four dimensions: Habitual Action, Understanding, Reflection, and Critical Reflection. Each dimension comprises four elements, for a
total of 16 elements. To suit the context and population of this study, the wording of the items was revised as needed. The validity of
the content was reviewed by two experts in educational technology, who confirmed the suitability of the scale to evaluate reflective
thinking in the context of experimental research design. For example, one element reads: ‘When I encounter a solution that appears
correct, I reflect on my original viewpoint’. Each item uses a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).
In this study, the Cronbach’s 𝛼 values for the four subscales, Habitual Action, Understanding, Reflection, and Critical Reflection,
were 0.82, 0.88, 0.86, and 0.89, respectively. The overall Cronbach’s 𝛼 for the reflective thinking scale was 0.86, indicating strong
internal consistency.

4.3.3. Learning motivation

To measure students learning motivation, we use the instrument developed by Chen et al. (2015), which is a revision of the
Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ; Pintrich et al., 1993). The scale consists of six items on two subscales:
Intrinsic Motivation (three items; e.g., ‘‘I enjoy learning challenging knowledge and materials because it allows me to discover new
things’’) and Extrinsic Motivation (three items; e.g., ‘‘Achieving good grades in experimental research methods is a major source
of satisfaction for me’’). All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher
scores reflecting stronger motivation. In the present study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s
𝛼 = 0.81. The validity of the content was reviewed by two domain experts, who confirmed its suitability for use in the context of
the instruction of experimental research methods.

4.3.4. Interview questions

In addition, a semi-structured interview protocol was developed to capture students’ experiences and perceptions of using the
intelligent conversational agent. The interview questions were adapted from Hwang et al. (2009) and included five specific questions.
The specific interview outline is provided below. Sixteen students — eight from the experimental group (labeled S1–S8) and eight
from the control group (labeled C1–C8) — were randomly selected for individual interviews lasting approximately 20 min each.
All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Two experienced researchers then performed qualitative content analysis.
In instances of coding disagreement, the researchers reviewed and discussed the coding until reaching consensus.

(1) In what ways does the S-ICA/nS-ICA learning approach differ from other learning methods you have used before?
(2) Overall, what are the main advantages of the S-ICA/nS-ICA learning approach?
(3) What are the greatest benefits you personally gained from using the S-ICA/nS-ICA approach? What did you learn most from

it? Please be specific.

(4) What aspects of the S-ICA/nS-ICA learning approach do you think need improvement? Please specify.
(5) Would you recommend the S-ICA/nS-ICA learning approach to your classmates or friends? Why or why not?

the online ENA tool (https://app.epistemicnetwork.org) to construct and visualize epistemic networks. The data were organized

visualizing the networks as graphs where nodes represented categories and edges represented co-occurrences. Group-level contrasts
were tested by comparing ENA dimensions (e.g., centrality, clustering, and density) using independent-sample t-tests to identify
significant differences in network structures between the experimental (S-ICA) and control (nS-ICA) groups. Key parameters included
a sliding window of 5 utterances, Z-score normalization, PCA for optimal separation of categories, and a threshold of 0.1 to filter
weak connections, ensuring focus on significant co-occurrences.

performed open coding, axial coding, and theme refinement, following the procedures of Zhang and Creswell (2013). Disagreements
were resolved through discussion until full agreement was reached, ensuring the reliability and transparency of the qualitative

of substantially higher quality than those developed with the nS-ICA, despite the small number of team-level observations. This
finding suggests that embedding Socratic questioning into LLM-driven agents can enhance the rigor and coherence of students’
collaborative research design outputs, even under authentic classroom constraints. However, the small sample size in this study
(n=8 per group) limits statistical power and may reduce generalizability of the findings. While the observed effects are statistically
significant, caution is needed when interpreting these results. Future research with larger and more diverse samples is necessary to
confirm the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

6. Discussion

6.1. Academic performance

The findings indicate a differentiated impact of the S-ICA across outcome domains. At the individual level, both groups achieved
substantial gains in conceptual knowledge, but the advantage of the S-ICA over the nS-ICA was modest and only marginally
detectable. This pattern is consistent with prior research showing that GenAI-based tools can enhance learner achievement, yet
their effects on conventional knowledge tests are typically small to moderate (Hwang & Chen, 2023). Such tests tend to privilege the
correctness of final answers rather than the justificatory and regulatory processes that Socratic dialogue is designed to cultivate (Lin
et al., 2023). As a result, the improvements in epistemic regulation and reasoning depth elicited by the S-ICA may not have been
fully captured by this form of assessment.

Taken together, the findings indicate that the educational benefits of Socratic scaffolding manifest differently across outcome
levels. While conceptual knowledge tests revealed only modest incremental gains for the S-ICA, the group-authored proposals
demonstrated a substantial advantage, underscoring the value of dialogic scaffolding in tasks that demand extended reasoning,
integration of perspectives, and collaborative knowledge construction. Pedagogically, this points to a productive sequencing strategy:
employing S-ICA to foster individual reflection and epistemic accountability prior to group collaboration, so that collective work
can build on stronger, more critically reasoned contributions. More broadly, the results illustrate how GenAI-driven conversational
agents, when designed with adaptive questioning strategies, can personalize the level of cognitive challenge and support learners in
ways that accumulate into higher-quality collective outcomes Hu & Shao, 2025. This dual role—providing individualized scaffolding
while enhancing group performance—resonates with established traditions in scaffolding and collaborative learning (Molenaar et al.,
2014) and highlights a promising direction for leveraging GenAI to advance both personalized and collaborative dimensions of
academic performance. Although this study provides valuable insights into the impact of Socratic questioning in LLM-driven agents,
the small sample size necessitates caution in drawing broad conclusions. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to
validate these results.

6.2. Reflective thinking

Results indicated that students supported by the S-ICA reported significantly higher levels of reflective thinking than those in the
control group, with the most pronounced differences emerging in the advanced dimensions of Reflection and Critical Reflection. This
suggests that embedding Socratic dialogue strategies into an ICA can foster deeper forms of self-reported reflective engagement. Such
an interpretation is consistent with prior studies showing that sustained questioning techniques stimulate learners’ critical reasoning
and  self-reflective  capacities  (Hu,  2023).  Similarly,  Rospigliosi  (2023)  demonstrated  that  LLM-based  agents  such  as  ChatGPT
can promote iterative cycles of ‘‘question–answer–reflection’’, thereby supporting students’ awareness of their own thinking. The
theoretical underpinnings of Socratic pedagogy also reinforce these findings: as Edmunds (2006) emphasized, the Socratic method
positions the teacher—or in this case, the agent—as a facilitator who provokes cognitive conflict and challenges prior assumptions.
Through this process, learners are encouraged not only to articulate what they already know but also to engage in higher-order
conceptual inquiry and metacognitive regulation. Other scholars have similarly highlighted how dialogic approaches promote
consensus-building and cognitive engagement through dialectical reasoning, social negotiation, and self-correction (Darmawansah
et al., 2024).

Beyond theoretical support, our ENA analyses provided empirical evidence for these patterns. The experimental group exhib-
ited stronger network connections among key pathways—notably Understanding–Reflection, Reflection–Critical Reflection, and
Understanding–Critical  Reflection—compared  with  the  control  group.  These  findings  suggest  that  the  S-ICA  more  effectively
activated mid- to higher-order reflective pathways. This observation accords with (Moon et al., 2000)’s model of reflective learning,
which characterizes reflection as an iterative and nonlinear process of reconstructing meaning. As Seeskin (1987) noted, Socratic
dialogue ‘‘thaws frozen thought’’, encouraging learners to pause, reconsider, and deepen their reflection. Accordingly, the present
study contributes to calls for integrating Socratic questioning and other philosophical frameworks into AI-mediated instruction to
cultivate reflective thinking.

Further examination of the ENA network differences reveals the underlying cognitive mechanisms at play. In the control
condition,  where  students  interacted  with  a  nS-ICA  that  provided  direct  feedback  without  any  prompting  for  reflection,  the
network exhibited fewer interconnections and weaker reflective pathways. This observation underscores the critical role of Socratic
questioning in stimulating deeper cognitive engagement. The S-ICA, by contrast, encouraged a more complex, recursive interaction

between students’ initial understandings, reflective thought, and critical evaluation. The iterative process of query–response–revision
observed in the experimental group represents the core pedagogical mechanism that enhanced reflective thinking. This process
mirrors concept of reflective practice, where students actively engage in self-regulation and self-correction to refine their reasoning
(Schön, 1995).

These results suggest that the observed improvements in reflective thinking and cognitive integration were not merely the product
of LLM technology but arose from the integration of Socratic scaffolding with LLM-based conversational agents. The S-ICA does not
simply serve as a passive content-delivery tool but actively shapes the learning process by fostering higher-order thinking. This
interaction between LLM technology and Socratic questioning offers a compelling model for AI-mediated instruction that supports
critical thinking and reflective learning. Besides, the ability of Socratic prompting to activate mid- to higher-order thinking pathways
is pivotal in deepening students’ understanding and critical thinking skills, which are essential for academic success in complex
learning environments.

6.3. Learning motivation

The present study found no statistically significant difference in reported learning motivation between students using the S-ICA
and those using the nS-ICA. This contrasts with previous research suggesting that intelligent conversational agents and GenAI tools
can, under certain conditions, enhance motivation and related affective outcomes such as self-efficacy and learning attitudes (Essel
et al., 2022). In our context, however, the motivational impact of the S-ICA appeared limited, despite its positive effects on cognitive
outcomes.

One plausible explanation lies in Self-Determination Theory, which emphasizes that sustained intrinsic motivation requires the
fulfillment of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs (Deci & Ryan, 2012). While the S-ICA’s progressive questioning may
have promoted cognitive engagement, it may not have adequately supported students’ sense of autonomy or personal relevance.
In other words, dialogic interaction with an agent, in the absence of stronger alignment with learners’ interests or values, may be
insufficient to trigger meaningful increases in motivation (Vansteenkiste et al., 2004). This limitation may have been amplified by
the nature of the course context—‘‘educational research methods’’—which many students perceive as a technical requirement rather
than an intrinsically appealing subject domain Hsieh et al. (2012).

Taken together, these results suggest a differentiated impact of the S-ICA: while effective in enhancing conceptual knowledge and
the quality of collaborative outputs, its influence on students’ motivational perceptions remained negligible. This finding highlights
the importance of designing GenAI-supported learning environments that combine cognitive scaffolding with targeted motivational
supports, for example, by incorporating greater personalization, adaptive goal-setting, or affective feedback mechanisms. Future
work should explore whether longer interventions or stronger personalization features enable S-ICAs to foster not only cognitive
development but also more robust motivational engagement.

6.4. Learning perceptions

To complement the quantitative findings, semi-structured interviews were conducted to capture students’ perceptions of their
learning experiences. Participants in the experimental group emphasized that the S-ICA, through sustained questioning, refutation,
and  heuristic  prompts,  encouraged  deeper  engagement  during  proposal  evaluation.  They  reported  that  the  system  facilitated
internalization of research concepts, clarification of variable relationships, and more active participation in knowledge construction.
In  contrast,  students  in  the  control  group  described  the  nS-ICA  as  useful  for  task  completion  but  noted  tendencies  toward
‘‘information dependence’’ and ‘‘cognitive passivity’’. Several admitted to directly adopting AI-generated responses without critically
engaging with the underlying research logic, which was reflected in weaker rigor and feasibility of their proposals. These accounts
align with prior work suggesting that while AI can expedite access to information, effective learning requires structured guidance
to transform information into deeper understanding (Lin, 2019).

Moreover, interviews revealed potential affective and motivational benefits of the S-ICA. Many students noted that, although
continuous questioning initially imposed cognitive load, it ultimately ‘‘forced me to think carefully’’, thereby heightening their
engagement with the task (Belland et al., 2013). This ‘‘cognitive challenge + guidance’’ model aligns with (Delen et al., 2014)
description of immersive learning environments, where strong interactivity and guided scaffolding alleviate feelings of isolation and
boost motivation. While the quantitative results did not show a significant overall increase in learning motivation, the qualitative
accounts revealed that some students nevertheless experienced heightened interest, persistence, and exploratory willingness when
interacting with the S-ICA. This divergence highlights the distinction between measurable motivational outcomes and students’
subjective  experiences.  It  also  underscores  the  need,  when  optimizing  intelligent  conversational  agents,  to  balance  cognitive
rigor with user-friendly interactions, thereby enhancing system acceptability and sustainable use. Future research should further
investigate how adaptive learning mechanisms—such as tailoring questioning frequency, depth, and feedback to individual learner
profiles—can translate these localized perceptions of increased engagement into sustained and generalizable motivational gains.

6.5. Research implications

Computers & Education 241 (2026) 105494

The findings of this study suggest that the integration of LLM-based intelligent conversational agents with Socratic questioning
strategies holds potential for enriching higher education learning processes, though the benefits appear differentiated across outcome
domains. At the individual level, conceptual knowledge gains were substantial across both conditions, with only a modest and at
best marginal advantage for the S-ICA. At the collective level, however, teams supported by the S-ICA produced significantly higher-
quality research proposals, indicating that dialogic scaffolding may be particularly effective for open-ended, collaborative tasks
requiring justification and integration of ideas. In contrast, no statistically significant improvements were observed in students’ self-
reported learning motivation, suggesting that the cognitive benefits of Socratic prompting did not automatically extend to affective
dimensions.

Theoretically, these findings contribute to ongoing debates on how GenAI can be meaningfully combined with established
pedagogical  traditions.  By  empirically  examining  the  role  of  Socratic  strategies  in  an  LLM-mediated  environment,  this  study
illustrates how dialogic scaffolding can strengthen epistemic processes such as justification and reflection, which may subsequently
accumulate into more coherent collective outputs. At the same time, the limited motivational impact observed underscores the need
to more explicitly align AI-supported learning designs with students’ autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs, as emphasized
in motivational theory. These nuanced outcomes highlight that GenAI’s educational value is not uniform but depends on the specific
learning processes targeted and the assessment methods employed.

Practically, the results yield implications for integrating AI-driven agents into university instruction. Replication of this approach
requires not only technical implementation of prompt-engineering strategies but also contextual embedding in authentic coursework,
ensuring alignment with curricular goals and student needs. Rather than being constrained by the study’s scope—a six-week
intervention and discipline-specific task—the findings point to promising avenues for extending such designs to broader contexts.
Future implementations should investigate how stronger personalization, adaptive feedback, and longer exposure periods affect both
cognitive and motivational outcomes, as well as whether the observed team-level benefits transfer to other forms of academic work.
In particular, adaptive learning systems could play a pivotal role in tailoring feedback to learners’ prior knowledge, interests, and
progress trajectories, thereby enhancing both performance and motivation. More broadly, the study underscores the importance of
designing GenAI-supported environments that balance immediate cognitive scaffolding with mechanisms for sustaining motivation
and promoting transferability across domains.

Overall, the present study highlights both the promise and the boundaries of embedding Socratic strategies into LLM-based
agents. While the approach demonstrated clear potential to enhance cognitive and collaborative outcomes, its effectiveness will
depend on careful contextualization, alignment with motivational principles, and sustained exposure. Future work should therefore
position such integrations not as universally transformative solutions, but as targeted pedagogical innovations whose value lies in
their capacity to enrich personalized and adaptive learning when thoughtfully implemented.

7. Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample was primarily composed of students majoring in educational
technology, and the gender distribution across participants was uneven. This sampling context may limit the generalizability of
the findings to learners from other disciplines and backgrounds. Future research should therefore aim to recruit more diverse and
representative samples to enhance external validity.

Second, the instructional tasks implemented in this study predominantly focused on research design-oriented activities. Although
this context aligns well with the development of reflective thinking and higher-order reasoning, it remains uncertain whether
comparable benefits would be observed in other domains of learning, such as discipline-specific content knowledge, professional
skills, or affective outcomes. Future studies should therefore extend the application of S-ICAs to a wider range of instructional
settings to examine transferability and ecological validity.

Third, the relatively short duration of the intervention limited the ability to examine the long-term effects of the AI-supported
Socratic dialogue agent on learners’ reflective practices and sustained behavioral change. Follow-up studies should adopt longitudinal
designs and repeated measures to capture whether the observed gains in reasoning and proposal quality persist, attenuate, or
compound over time.

Fourth, the limited number of group-level submissions (eight proposals per condition) constrained statistical power and reduced
the precision of effect estimates. While robust analytic techniques were employed, future research should increase the number of
groups to better account for group-level variance and enhance the reliability of inferences.

In  addition,  ethical  considerations  surrounding  LLM-based  learning  processes  were  not  comprehensively  addressed  in  this
research. Issues such as data privacy, content originality, and intellectual property rights pose potential challenges to the responsible
use of GenAI-generated content in educational contexts. Accordingly, future work should not only investigate how learners and
educators perceive these ethical risks, but also develop practical guidelines and institutional policies to ensure that GenAI tools are
adopted responsibly, transparently, and in ways that safeguard academic integrity.

8. Conclusion

Overall, the primary contribution of the present study lies in the design and empirical validation of the S-ICA that inte-
grates  Socratic  dialogue  strategies  with  LLM  technology  to  enhance  students’  research  design  skills,  reflective  thinking,  and
perceptions of learning tools within a discipline-specific course. Results indicate that the S-ICA confers significant advantages in
guiding students through higher-order cognitive tasks, stimulating deep reflection, and improving the scientific rigor of research
proposals—particularly along the dimensions of Reflection and Critical Reflection.

A distinctive feature of the S-ICA is its capacity to provide personalized and adaptive scaffolding: by semantically parsing
learner input and tailoring subsequent questions to individual reasoning stages, the system was able to align instructional support
with students’ needs, strengths, and levels of preparedness. This adaptive responsiveness ensured that feedback was neither overly
simplistic nor excessively demanding, thereby sustaining engagement and promoting productive struggle. In this sense, the study
contributes to the growing body of work on AI-supported personalized learning by demonstrating how dialogic scaffolding can adapt
in real time to heterogeneous learners.

In the context of the rapid advances in GenAI, a central challenge for educational research is how to leverage LLM-based
conversational systems to enhance learning without fostering cognitive dependency or encouraging unethical academic practices.
This study offers a model for integrating classical instructional strategies with cutting-edge AI technologies, providing empirical
evidence of their potential efficacy in educational settings. The Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) results demonstrated that Socratic
prompting effectively activated more advanced reflective pathways, indicating that such AI-driven systems can play a crucial role in
higher education by fostering critical thinking and reflective engagement. Furthermore, the findings suggest that adaptive Socratic
prompting could strike a balance between two essential goals in higher education: supporting personalized learning paths for
individual students while simultaneously promoting collective knowledge construction in collaborative tasks.

This approach addresses concerns about the potential risks of AI-generated content in education (Abbas et al., 2024; Ng et al.,
2024) by demonstrating how structured, question-driven dialogue can mitigate those risks while promoting deeper learning. Future
research should extend this line of inquiry by examining how adaptive Socratic ICAs can be scaled to diverse subject areas, longer
interventions, and varied learner populations, thereby advancing the integration of personalized and ethical AI practices in higher
education.
