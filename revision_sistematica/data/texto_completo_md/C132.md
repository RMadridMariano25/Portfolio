1. Introduction

Generative AI (GenAI) is believed to support generational learning. However, there is no single
study that examines the use of GenAI for self-directed learning (SDL), active learning (AL), and
critical thinking (CT) of different cohorts of adult learners. Specifically, in the field of adult ed-
ucation,  there  are  limited  studies  on  the  effect  of  GenAI  on  teaching  and  learning.  This  study
employs a cross-sectional quantitative design to examine the relationships between SDL, AL, and
CT  in  the  context  of  adult  learners’  use  of  GenAI  tools  like  ChatGPT.  It  considers  how  de-
mographic variables moderate these relationships. The study was underpinned by the SDL theory
by Tough in 1971. An analysis of 281 survey responses of adult learners grouped by generation
from five (5) adult higher education institutions in Ghana was performed using descriptive sta-
tistics,  ANOVA  (analysis  of  variance),  and  SEM  (structural  equation  modeling).  The  study’s
findings indicate that GenAI is perceived as having a significant positive effect on the SDL, AL,
and CT of adult learners. A significant positive interrelationship was observed between SDL, AL,
and CT. Demographic factors involving gender, year born (generational group), education level,
and mode of learning had a perceived significant positive effect on SDL, AL, and CT. Notably, no
statistical mean differences were found in the use of GenAI for SDL, AL, and CT across genera-
tional groups. The findings advocate for the effective adoption of GenAI to foster lifelong learning
among adult learners. Developers of learning technologies and higher education leaders should
ensure an inclusive GenAI design.

Adult higher education (AHE), the active participation of adults in formal degree or certificate programs of study in higher edu-
cation (Kasworm, 1993, 2011), is on the cusp of change in the face of the massive proliferation of generative artificial intelligence
(GenAI) technologies. GenAI technologies promise to transform old-fashioned adult higher educational practices unprecedentedly
(Adarkwah, 2024). A societal expectation of AHE is for it to be a laboratory of knowledge and create innovative learning spaces to
cultivate the twenty-first-century competencies (e.g., critical thinking, digital literacy, creativity, etc.) of adult learners for them to be
productive in the contemporary labor market. Ling and Fraser (2014) pose the question; “what is higher about higher education?” (p.

Expectations are that higher education involves higher learning outcomes in the cognitive sense, psychomotor domain, and aca-
demic  standards (Ling  &  Fraser, 2014).  As  advanced  technologies  are  increasingly  used,  AHE  becomes  more  important  for  adult
learners to sustain their employment, advance professionally, and adapt to career changes. In AHE where there are limited teacher
capacity and resources, GenAI can expedite the rapid creation of high-quality, personalized, and engaging materials for the purposes of
instruction and assessment in any given learning context (Cacicio & Riggs, 2023). GenAI can promote self-directed learning and the
active learning and critical thinking of adult learners (Ciampa et al., 2023; Lin, 2024).

Adult learners represent a student population that seeks formal degrees, certifications, or skills to enter the labor market and pursue
a fulfilling life (Gardner et al., 2022; Mason, 2020). To function effectively in contemporary society, adult learners need to be digitally
competent (Adarkwah, 2024; Cacicio & Riggs, 2023). GenAI has the potential to promote the accessibility of digital learning resources
(Adarkwah, 2024), self-directed learning (Adarkwah, 2024; Lin, 2024; Santos-Meneses et al., 2023), active learning (Pahi et al., 2024;
Salinas-Navarro et al., 2024), and critical thinking (Hamerman et al., 2024; Wood & Moss, 2024), which are essential skills that AHE
aims to foster in learners. Technology (e.g., GenAI tools) is crucial for reaching this goal by offering learners tools that facilitate
communication, collaboration, and problem-solving (Ruiz-Rojas et al., 2024). Conversely, some researchers have expressed concerns
regarding the diminishing ability of GenAI to promote self-directed learning, active learning, and critical thinking among learners,
especially when used irresponsibly (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Lin, 2024; Mogavi et al., 2024).

1.1. Past studies on generative AI in adult higher education

The study found only a few studies that look at the utilization of GenAI in adult learning. Milana et al. (2024) published an editorial
discussing the implications of AI and GenAI for adult education and research, but they did not provide empirical evidence regarding
adult  learners  or  specific  learning  contexts.  Ciampa  et  al.  (2023) also  published  a  discussion  paper  on  ChatGPT’s  capability  in
transforming digital literacy practices in education but only briefly mentioned adult learners alongside adolescents. Cacicio and Riggs
(2023) authored an opinion piece on how GenAI can help bridge resource gaps in adult education, yet they did not address different
types of adult education programs or adult higher education, nor did they present supporting data. The study by Kang (2023) con-
ducted a systematic literature analysis focused on AI’s impact on adult learning in China, without specifically addressing a GenAI tool.
Lastly, Lin (2024) wrote a commentary on how ChatGPT could enhance self-directed learning among adult learners, concluding with
several unanswered questions that require empirical data for evidence-based conclusions. That is, there is a lack of empirical study on
GenAI use specifically in AHE.

1.2. Research gap

HEIs have the responsibility to harness novel technologies to develop the AI skills of adult learners. However, how AHE leverages
groundbreaking technologies such as GenAI to facilitate self-directed learning among adult learners and cultivate their active learning
and critical thinking skills is unknown. Also, the impact of GenAI on self-directed learning remains unclear (Chiu, 2024a). Moreover,
there are also growing concerns about the implications of GenAI for self-directed learning (B. Li et al., 2024) and the active learning
and critical thinking skills of learners (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Tlili et al., 2023).

Additionally, in this intelligent era, many adult learners lack digital literacy skills and are not competent self-directed learners
(Morris & Rohs, 2021). Taking advantage of the powerful features of GenAI by adult learners will maximize self-directed learning and
opportunities to upskill their competencies for their professional work (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023; Chiu, 2024b; Lin, 2024; Oxford
Analytica, 2023). Particularly in the African region, including countries such as Ghana, higher education institutions are characterized
as late adopters and laggards of new technological innovations like GenAI (Adarkwah, Amponsah, et al., 2023). This study found no
empirical  study  that  specifically  focuses  on  the  unique  contributions  of  African  AHE  in  leveraging  GenAI  to  revolutionize  adult
learning.

Moreover, studies on technology use by adult learners often treat adult learners as a “black box” although there are different adult
learner populations with different learning styles. Different categories of adult learners experience distinctive apprehension in the use
of new technologies. The current study contributes to the understanding of how GenAI facilitates the self-directed learning, active
learning, and critical thinking skills of different cohorts of adult learners.

Furthermore, the recent buzz around GenAI has led to scholarly discussions about its potential role in education such as revolu-
tionizing traditional learning practices (Adarkwah, 2024). At the same, there have been doomsday predictions about its use (Rudolph
et al., 2023a) and a call for guidelines to facilitate its effective integration into education systems for different learner populations
(UNESCO, 2023). That is, there is a need to examine how AHE in the African context can safely and responsibly integrate digital
technologies like GenAI to enhance the self-directed learning experience of adult learners to achieve a high-income and sustainable
economy. Particularly in the African context such as in Ghana, there are fewer studies on the application and implication of GenAI in
higher education.

predominately work simultaneously (Biney & Salifu, 2024). However, because of the early completion of senior high school education,
individuals who are 18 years old are also enrolled in DE programs. The labor market dynamics such as the need to upskill one’s

likely to be involved with technology-enhanced learning compared to adult males (Adu-Marfo et al., 2024). In this study, we look at
GenAI use of adult learners (18 years and above) in the various AHE and the interplay of demographic factors.

1.4. Theoretical underpinning

The study is underpinned by the self-directed learning (SDL) theory, which has its roots in adult learning (Kim et al., 2021; Tough,
1971). SDL is a significant concept in adult education consisting of five main processes that promote AL and stimulate CT in learners;
establishing learning goals, locating and accessing resources, adopting and executing learning activities, monitoring and evaluating
performance, and reassessing learning strategies (Lin, 2024). For many decades, a large cohort of adults have engaged in self-directed
learning (SDL) as a practical method for acquiring knowledge and skills, reflecting their real-life needs and commitments at work
(Lalitha  &  Sreeja,  2020).  This  is  possible  because  SDL  enables  adult  learners  to  pursue  personalized  learning  and  be  either
goal-oriented, activity-oriented, or learning-oriented. SDL also fosters the academic outcomes of adult learners (Chukwuedo et al.,
2021). Past studies have linked SDL, AL, and CT in learning environments (ElSayary, 2024; Ghimire et al., 2024; Khoiriyah et al., 2015;
Song et al., 2022; Timothy et al., 2010). Below, each of the constructs in the topic is defined and how they can be tailored to GenAI in
transforming adult education and learning. The section concludes by looking at the connection between SDL, AL, and CT and how they
can be facilitated by the effective utilization of GenAI.

1.4.1. Self-directed learning and GenAI use

Knowles (1975) defines SDL as a process “…in which individuals take the initiative, with or without the help of others, in diag-
nosing  their  learning  needs,  formulating  learning  goals,  identifying  human  and  material  resources  for  learning,  choosing  and
implementing appropriate learning strategies, and evaluating learning outcomes” (p. 18). However, SDL can simply be defined as the
use of learning strategies to achieve a learning goal (Kim et al., 2021). SDL places emphasis on learning through experiences for
personal and professional development and empowers a learner academically, professionally, and psychologically to make decisions in
a learning environment (Chukwuedo et al., 2021). Self-directed learners take the initiative with or without assistance from people to
diagnose their learning needs, formulate their learning goals, identify human and material resources for their learning, choose and

Hutasuhut et al. (2021) mentioned that SDL does not occur in a vacuum but requires a supportive learning environment for it to
emerge. GenAI in adult education offers a supportive platform for adult learners to engage in SDL practices (Adarkwah, 2024; Lin,

considered a strategy that encourages open innovation by giving them access to external knowledge (Jayasinghe, 2025). Jayasinghe
adds that SDL-driven practices among such worker populations empower them to enhance their skills which enables them to adapt to
the changing dynamics in today’s workforce.

1.4.2. Active learning (AL) and GenAI use

AL is a method of instruction that involves a student in the learning process and demands them to engage in meaningful learning
activities and think about their activities (Martínez Casanovas et al., 2022). Genuine learning occurs when learners actively engage
with experiences such as using different GenAI platforms that allow them to explore and discover new knowledge beyond what is
taught  by  an  instructor (Hu,  2024). Adults  as  lifelong  learners  are  active  rather  than passive learners  (Chukwuedo  et  al., 2021).
Innovative pedagogical practices facilitated by technologies can lead to AL methodologies in AHE (Carvalho et al., 2021).

According to Ruiz-Rojas et al. (2024), there is a strong association between AI tools like ChatGPT and AL with increased active
participation of learners. For example, in a study that utilized GenAI technology, ChatGPT, to promote active learning, it was observed
that  ChatGPT  was  effective  in  enhancing  student  feedback  such  as  providing  clarification  to  examples  and  offering  motivational
support (Pahi et al., 2024). In another study, GenAI tools are used as active agents in the learning process to promote active partic-
ipation and active experimentation to improve learning experience and achievements (Salinas-Navarro et al., 2024). Notwithstanding,
other researchers have raised the question of whether students can remain active learners with GenAI in the classroom (Abdelghani
et al., 2023).

1.4.3. Critical thinking (CT) and GenAI use

CT is universally accepted as a crucial skill for solving the multifaceted challenges of modern society and recognized as a funda-
mental competency necessary for success in the 21st century (Din, 2020; Tang et al., 2024). CT refers to the ability to collect, evaluate,
and use information effectively and appropriately (Din, 2020). In contrast to force training and memory-based learning that results in
low-order  thinking  skills,  CT  is  a  prime  goal  in  adult  learning  because  it  is  vital  for  continuous  professional  development  and
social/interpersonal relations where decision-making and problem-solving are needed (Santos-Meneses et al., 2023). GenAI tools like
ChatGPT can improve the CT skills of adult learners (Adarkwah, 2024).

GenAI offers interactive and engaging learning experiences that promote deeper understanding, CT, and experiential activities
among learners (Salinas-Navarro et al., 2024). Learners develop their CT skills when using GenAI by being encouraged to evaluate and
analyze the AI-generated content and think deeply about its ethical considerations (Hamerman et al., 2024). An integration of GenAI
into a master-level course revealed that it promotes CT ability and a deeper understanding of instructional design principles in learners
(Wood &  Moss, 2024). Michel-Villarreal et al. (2023) also mentioned that ChatGPT can provide formative feedback and act as a
tutoring system to stimulate the CT of students. However, Tlili et al. (2023) asserted that GenAI tools like ChatGPT can diminish the
innovative capacities and CT of learners.

1.5. SDL, AL, CT and GenAI use

SDL, which is facilitated by digital technologies emphasizes CT and AL of learners (Khoiriyah et al., 2015; Song et al., 2022; F. Tang,
2024; Timothy et al., 2010) and is essential for social action (Timothy et al., 2010). In their study, Martínez Casanovas et al. (2022)
made a connection between experiential learning an SDL strategy, AL methodologies, and CT capabilities. Positive correlations have
also been found between SDL which involves AL engagement and CT capabilities of learners (Jin & Ji, 2021). SDL involves learning
strategies such as CT and advocates for AL such as reflecting on learning experiences and positive relations with peers and teachers
(van Woezik et al., 2021). Li et al. (2024) revealed that integrating GenAI into education such as ChatGPT-based Inquiry Learning
Assistant (InquiryGPT) facilitates SDL and promotes CT and AL among students.

GenAI pushes learners to be active and take control of their learning, and their effective implementation can be beneficial for
learners in terms of cognitive and metacognitive ability (Abdelghani et al., 2023). In a supporting statement, ElSayary (2024) add that
GenAI contributes to the development of key competencies in the twenty-first century such as AL methodologies, CT, and metacog-
nition in education which fosters SDL. GenAI in adult learning facilitates SDL which  promotes CT or metacognitive skills among
learners and enables them to engage in active recall (Lin, 2024).

However,  Abdelghani  et  al.  (2023) revealed  that  the  convenience  of  accessing  information  using  GenAI  can  lead  to  negative
learning as students tend to attribute cognitive success to GenAI instead of AL strategies such as questioning and exploration. Also,
learner’s perceptions of GenAI are associated with their active engagement, overall effectiveness, and their ability to incorporate such
tools into their learning to enhance their CT (Wood & Moss, 2024). The study by Ghimire et al. (2024) also showed that while GenAI
can promote AL approaches by enabling learners to personalize their learning, there are concerns about decreasing CT in learners.

Lin (2024) also affirms that using GenAI such as ChatGPT for SDL can make adult learners solely depend on it, leading to diminished
CT ability and their active interactions or engagement in the learning environment. (Michel-Villarreal et al., 2023) advocates for GenAI
to be used with AL pedagogies such as experiential learning which is emphasized in SDL. Therefore, an effective integration of GenAI in

1.6. Purpose of the study

From the aforementioned gaps in literature and educational concerns, the study seeks to investigate how different adult learner
populations (Gen Z, Millennials, GenX, and Baby Boomers) from AHE in Ghana assess their SDL, AL, and CT skills in using GenAI for
educational purposes. Specifically, the study is focused on examining the relationships between SDL, AL, and CT in the context of adult
learners’  use of Generative AI (GenAI) tools like ChatGPT, while also considering how demographic variables moderate these re-
lationships (see Fig. 2). This study is likely the first to adopt a comparative approach to examine GenAI use by different cohorts of adult
learners (n = 281). The study employs a cross-sectional descriptive quantitative survey to ascertain adult learners’ self-ratings in terms
of SDL, AL, and CT skills in using GenAI. This will be followed by a comparative analysis of the self-ratings across the three variables
among the different cohorts of adult learners in Ghana.

Hence, the main research questions guiding this study include;

1.  What is the relationship between self-directed learning, active learning, and critical thinking skills of adult learners in using GenAI?
2.  To what extent does the use of GenAI (e.g., ChatGPT) promote self-directed learning, active learning, and critical thinking among

adult learners in Ghana?

3.  How do demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, education level) moderate the relationship between GenAI usage and the three

learning constructs (SDL, AL, CT)?

2. Methods

2.1. Design

The study examined the use of GenAI by adult learners in relation to self-directed learning (SDL), active learning (AL), and critical
thinking (CT), as well as the interconnections among these variables. An online cross-sectional survey design was used to help reach a
larger and more diverse audience. “A cross-sectional study is a type of observational study design that involves looking at data from a
population at one specific point in time” (Wang & Cheng, 2020, p. 65). Specifically, the descriptive cross-sectional survey design was
used to characterize and assess the prevalence of the variables of the study in the adult learner population in higher education (Maier
et al., 2023).

One aim of cross-sectional survey design is to establish general models that link various elements under specific conditions, with
results serving to confirm or refute theories regarding these relationships (Hunziker & Blankenagel, 2024). In the context of this study
underpinned by the SDL theory, the relationship between SDL, AL, and CT were examined. The cross-sectional survey design was
appropriate  for  this  study  because  the  development  of  the  items  of  the  survey  allows  for  statistical  comparisons  that  provide  a

2.2. Population and sampling

random sampling approach was used to identify adults in these institutions who were familiar with GenAI use in education. The final

% and that of females was 56.6 %. The participants were further categorized into year generations based on their responses: Gen Z,

2.3. Data collection procedure

Academics (e.g., professors, lecturers, and researchers) affiliated with one or more of the AHE institutions were sent an introductory
letter and a link to the online structured survey to assist in collecting data from the learners. The link to the survey was shared on
WhatsApp  class  groups  of  the  learners  for  participants  interested  in  the  study  to  provide  their  responses.  The  participants  were
encouraged to share the survey link with peers in other AHE institutions in the country. Data was collected from June to October 2024.
During  the  data  collection  period,  academics  who  were  acting  as  informants  for  the  study  were  contacted  twice  to  remind  their
students to respond to the survey. In all, the duration for completing the online survey was 10–15 min.

2.4. Ethical considerations

The survey consisted of an introductory statement detailing the aims of the study and obtaining the consent of the participants.
Participants were assured of confidentiality and anonymity. The survey data was also stored in a secure location and encrypted to
prevent access from a third party. Participants were informed they could withdraw from the study at any stage. The study did not
require ethical approval. However, all recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed.

2.5. Measures

The survey had two main sections: 1. Demographic information and 2. Questions. The demographic questions measured birth year
(generational group), gender, institution, education level, mode of learning, areas of specialization, the kind of Gen AI tool used, and
two questions measuring GenAI usage involving average time spent in using GenAI and level of familiarity with GenAI. The second part
of the survey containing the main questions was further split into three (3) sections to reflect the three variables of the study. How the
study constructs were measured is outlined below.

SDL: An adapted version of the self-directed learning with technology (SDLT) (Lee et al., 2014) was used to measure participants’
SDL using GenAI. The scale consisted of five questions each measuring the extent to which students integrate technology into their
learning. The items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale, (1 – strongly disagree, 2 – disagree, 3 – slightly disagree, 4 – neither agree
nor disagree, 5 – slightly agree, 6 – agree, 7 – strongly agree). The original scale used “computer” to represent the technology used. In
the adapted version, “computer” was replaced with “GenAI”. For example, item 1 “In this class, I use the computer to get ideas from
different websites and people to learn more about a topic” was rephrased as “In my class, I use the GenAI to get ideas from different
sources to learn more about a topic”. Reliability analysis shows that the Cronbach’s Alpha value for this construct is 0.922.

2.6. Data analysis

A multifaceted statistical approach was used for the analysis. First, descriptive statistics were performed to reveal the frequencies
and percentages of the demographic information of participants. Second, an ANOVA (analysis of variance) test was performed to
compare  the  mean  scores  of  different  generational  groups  across  the  three  study  constructs  (SDL,  AL,  and  CT).  Finally,  a  SEM
(structural equation modeling) analysis was performed to show the interrelationships between the study constructs, the effect of GenAI
on the study constructs, and the effect of demographic moderators on the study constructs. A confirmatory factor analysis was also
performed using SEM to validate the factor structure of the scales used in the study.

SEM was selected for its capacity to (1) validate latent constructs through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and (2) estimate
causal relationships among the constructs. In the current study, it helped in understanding how SDL, AL, and CT are interrelated in the
context of GenAI usage. The exogenous variable, GenAI, operated as a latent construct; the endogenous variables, SDL, AL, and CT,
were treated as outcomes influenced by GenAI and moderated by demographics. The demographic variables were tested as moderators
by comparing multi-group models (e.g., generational cohorts) and incorporating interaction terms. For instance, year born (as a proxy
for generational cohort) was analyzed to assess whether age (year of birth/generational group) altered the strength of GenAI’s effect on
SDL, AL, or CT. Although CFA does not confirm causality, it enables the testing of theoretical models that suggest causal pathways. For
instance, the study hypothesized that GenAI influences SDL, AL, and CT, and SEM facilitated the exploration of these assumptions.
Additionally, by incorporating demographic variables (e.g., year born, gender, educational level, mode of learning), CFA allowed for
analyzing how these factors moderate the relationships between GenAI and the study constructs. This is essential for understanding the
dynamics of how different adult learner populations engage with GenAI.

In the current study, the focus was on learners’ perceived impacts of GenAI on SDL, AL, and CT, as measured through self-reported
ratings. While perceptions are not equivalent to actual or objective reality, they are critical to understanding behavioral intentions and
adoption patterns in educational technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003). It is therefore important to underscore that in this study, the
self-reported perceptions of adult learners reflect their subjective evaluations of their skills and experiences, which may differ from
externally observed behaviors or competencies. An example is that even though adult learners may perceive that GenAI enhances their
self-directed learning, active learning, and critical thinking, this might not be the case during an external assessment of their SDL, AL,
and CT. Notwithstanding, the self-reported ratings provide insights into how adult learners’ interpretations of their interactions with
GenAI influence their adoption of such technologies for educational purposes.

4. Discussion

As a result of the paucity of empirical studies on GenAI use by adult learners, this study investigated how different cohorts of adult
learners utilized GenAI for self-directed learning (SDL), active learning (AL), and critical thinking (CT). The constructs of the study
were specifically chosen to address a theoretical, evidence, empirical, population, and methodological gap. The SDL theory by Tough
(1971) provided a theoretical foundation for conducting the study. First, there are concerns that different SDL approaches can have
varied effects on learning. For example, it was mentioned that using GenAI for SDL can negatively affect CT (Lin, 2024; C. Wang et al.,
2024). Mogavi et al. (2024) also expounded that although ChatGPT was used in performing certain SDL tasks, there are concerns about
its harmful effects on learning, such as AL, CT, and other GenAI-related ethical issues like academic integrity. Particularly, adult
learners  who  adopt  SDL  using  GenAI  are  expected  to  proactively  search  for  information  and  interact  with  technology,  but  they
experience difficulties in doing so, which impede the effectiveness of their self-directedness (Lin, 2024). This is because although the
classic andragogical model of adult learning posits that adults are self-directed learners (Knowles, 1975), which is always not the case,
especially in a technology-enhanced learning (TEL)environment. Some learning conditions for SDL can result in a negative effect on an
aspect of learning (Vaiˇci¯unien ˙e & Kazlauskien ˙e, 2023). That is, SDL with technology can be different from SDL without technology.
The use of GenAI, such as ChatGPT, for SDL, also differs depending on the age group (Dizon, 2023). As a contribution, the measurement
model and items of the instruments in the current study illustrate some of the SDL approaches that can positively contribute to learning
in terms of AL and CT as observed in the analysis of the self-reported experiences of adult learners in the present study.

The current study revealed that there is a perceived strong positive relationship between SDL, AL, and CT of adult learners in Ghana
in a digital learning environment. This agrees with the notion that SDL learners have the ability to take control of their own learning,
are motivated, have a sense of autonomy, and are actively engaged in learning activities (Jin & Ji, 2021; Knowles, 1975; Lin, 2024). As
reviewed earlier, applying SDL principles in a supportive learning environment, such as TEL contexts (Hutasuhut et al., 2021) can
result in enhanced AL and CT of learners (ElSayary, 2024; Ghimire et al., 2024; Khoiriyah et al., 2015; Song et al., 2022; Timothy et al.,
2010). The perceived positive relationship between the study constructs (SDL, AL, and CT) across all generational cohorts also suggests
that  regardless  of  the  year  born  or  age  group,  adult  learners  perceive  applying  SDL  principles  (e.g.,  collaboration,  motivation,
problem-solving, etc.) to foster active engagement and critical thinking during learning as essential. This is confirmed in the ANOVA
test result, which showed that there are no statistical mean differences in the study constructs across all generations. This could imply
that  the  learning  preferences  and  needs  of  adult  learners  in  Ghana  may  be  more  influenced  by  factors  other  than  generational

4.1. Implications

The study’s findings call for the effective adoption of GenAI to foster lifelong learning and higher-order thinking skills among adult
learners in higher education. This assertion is in consensus with the finding that incorporating digital technologies in higher education
institutions in Ghana will transform pedagogical approaches and build the digital literacy of adult learners in Ghana, ensuring that they
are not left behind in this digital era (Ussher &  Oheneba-Sakyi, 2023). Sustainable Development Goal Four (SDG4) advocates for
inclusive and lifelong opportunities for all learners irrespective of their background, age, status, or geographical location (UNESCO,
2020). GenAI can promote inclusivity in learning in higher education by ensuring ease of accessing digital learning resources for all
learner populations in different physical locations and from different socioeconomic homes (Dwivedi et al., 2023). If this goal is to be
realized, developers of technology for higher education should adopt an inclusive AI or GenAI design.

Adarkwah (2024) highlighted the dangers of GenAI in adult learning, involving concerns over cheating, plagiarism, overreliance on
GenAI, and inaccuracy in responses. Therefore, clear ethical guidelines or regulations should be established to guide the integration of
GenAI in higher education. Before integrating GenAI, a pilot test and feasibility analysis can be conducted to ensure that adult higher
education institutions have adequate technological infrastructure and information on the learning preferences of learners to tailor
andragogical practices and curricula to educational needs. Additionally, customized versions of specific GenAI tools can be developed
for specific  institutions in order  to adapt GenAI  functionalities and  interfaces  to existing learning management systems  (LMS) or
platforms to promote optimal user experience. Ongoing professional development is needed for both educators and students to equip
all  users  with  GenAI  skills.  GPT  detectors  that  are  efficient  in  combating  plagiarism  and  cheating  in  assessment  tasks  are  highly
recommended to ensure academic integrity in higher education systems (Perkins et al., 2024).

To enhance the effectiveness of GenAI-supported learning in higher education, adult educators should design GenAI practices or
interventions that consider the features and functionality of different generational models. Different GenAI models may be more suited
to a particular cohort of adult learners or for a specific type/subject of learning. Additionally, different/improved GenAI models may
be more effective than others. Rudolph et al. (2023b) described what they labelled as the “war of the chatbots” to mean the rapid
developments of GenAI models since the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022. In their study, a comparison of Bard, Bing Chat,
ChatGPT, and Ernie revealed that although all these models are not “intelligent” yet, GPT-4 performed the best, while models like Bing
Chat and Bard were comparable to students at risk of failing, with averages that would be considered failing grades. Nowadays, there
are new and/or improved GenAI models in the market, such as DeepSeek, Grok, LLaMA-2, Claude 2, etc., that all have different ca-
pabilities in terms of processing speed, level of security risk, cost of subscription or procurement, accessibility, accuracy, versatility,
structure of responses, multimodality etc.

5. Conclusion, limitations, and recommendations

The current study is one of the first studies to investigate different generational cohorts of adult learners’ use of GenAI for SDL, AL,
and CT. The findings suggest that embracing SDL approaches in education when using GenAI can enhance the AL and CT of learners.
That is, amid the concerns about GenAI’s negative influence on active engagement and high-order cognitive skills, effective integration
of GenAI in adult higher education can facilitate self-directedness in learning and ultimately promote active learning and critical
thinking of learners. The analysis of the self-reported experiences of adult learners in the study revealed the perceived impact of several
demographic factors on SDL, AL, and CT in using GenAI. The results of the study make a significant contribution to the ongoing debate
on whether higher education institutions should adopt or ban GenAI in teaching and learning practices. The study advocates for in-
clusive AI/GenAI design, developing digital/GenAI competencies of learners, and validating GenAI systems prior to implementation to
curtail possible negative effects on teaching and learning.

As a limitation of the study, it can be observed from the final sample of the study that Baby Boomers only formed a small fraction of
the participants of the study. Future studies should involve a more diverse and representative group of participants for all generational
cohorts. The study only focused on adult learners in higher education institutions in Ghana, which limits the generalizability of the
findings to other educational contexts. Researchers replicating the study should test the measurement model in higher education
institutions in different cultural and regional settings. A cross-cultural comparative study can provide valuable insights into the de-
mographic dynamics at play in how adult learners use GenAI for SDL, AL, and CT. Further analysis may be needed to ascertain the
differential effects of GenAI on the study constructs for each generational group. An experimental (other than self-reported data) and
