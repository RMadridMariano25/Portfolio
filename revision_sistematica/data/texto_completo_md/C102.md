The integration of GenAI into higher education risks promoting passive information acceptance
over critical thinking (CT), a challenge that is acutely felt in ill-structured problem-solving con-
texts such as engineering design. While cognitive scaffolds are recognized as a solution, limited
research  provides  process-oriented  evidence  on  how  they  mediate  student  engagement  with
GenAI.  Currently,  typical  GenAI integration  often leads  to  cognitive offloading  where  students
bypass  critical  evaluation.  To  counter  this  automation  bias,  this  study  introduced  a  novel
instructional framework that integrated the Preparation for Future Collaboration (PFC) paradigm
with the Target-Criteria-Source-Analogy (TCSA) cognitive strategy to structure GenAI-supported
learning.  By  analyzing  the  critical  thinking  patterns  of  77  engineering  undergraduates  across
high-,  medium-,  and  low-performing  groups,  Epistemic  Network  Analysis  (ENA)  identified
distinct cognitive co-occurrence patterns across GenAI-supported preparation and subsequent AI-
free  collaboration.  High-performing  students  showed  stronger  connections  among  Analysis,
Evaluation, and Self-regulation, a pattern we interpretively describe as a “Triangulation Loop,”
whereas  low-performing  students  showed  stronger  connections  between  Interpretation  and
Analysis, described as a “Descriptive Loop.”  The latter pattern was consistent with greater reli-
ance  on  AI-generated  information.  Within  the  integrated  PFC-TCSA  instructional  design,  these
observed patterns were characterized by stronger evaluative and self-regulatory co-occurrences,
providing practical implications for designing human–AI collaboration.

1. Introduction

The integration of GenAI into higher education presents a critical pedagogical dilemma for ill-structured problem solving in do-
mains characterized by ambiguous goals and multiple viable solutions, such as strategic planning and design (Jonassen, 1997; Lee
et al., 2024; Shahzad et al., 2024). While GenAI tools could offer opportunities to enhance students’  productivity via supporting
data retrieval and simulation, information synthesis and ideation, GenAI still risks promoting passive information acceptance and
enabling students to bypass the necessary cognitive struggles required for critical thinking (Habib et al., 2024; Johri, 2022; Singh,
2024).  This  competency  specifically  entails  the  core  skills  of  interpretation,  analysis,  and  evaluation  (Facione,  1990).  Instead  of

engaging in a rigorously structured human-AI partnership, students usually allow the GenAI tools to substitute their higher-order
thinking rather than supplement it (Rana et al., 2025; Sajadi et al., 2024).

The challenges of fostering these human-AI collaborations in the complex problem solving domains could be fundamentally un-
derstood through the lens of Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) (Sweller, 1988). According to CLT, solving ill-structured problems required a
high intrinsic cognitive load (Sweller et al., 2019). When GenAI is introduced, students would face additional extraneous cognitive
load arising from the need to navigate the tool interface, prompt effectively, and discern hallucinations through source vetting (Dell
et  al.,  2023).  If  collaborative  demands  are  added  simultaneously,  the  aggregate  load  could  easily  overwhelm  students'  working
memory  and  leave  no  capacity  for  the  germane  cognitive  load  required  for  schema  construction  and  in-depth  critical  analysis
(Kirschner et al., 2018). Consequently, without structured guidance, students with lower prior knowledge are likely to resort to su-
perficial processing strategies.

To address this challenge, this study proposed an integrated instructional framework that combined the Preparation for Future
Collaboration (PFC) paradigm and the Target-Criteria-Source-Analogy (TCSA) cognitive strategy. First, PFC works as a macro-level
structural scaffold to manage cognitive load. By sequencing individual engagement with GenAI before collaboration, PFC acts as a
cognitive pre-loading phase (Mende et al., 2021). It allows students to activate prior knowledge, clarify core concepts, and formulate
initial ideas, thereby theoretically reducing the extraneous cognitive load during the subsequent collaboration phase (Lam & Kapur,
2018; Lyu et al., 2025). This ensures students' limited working memory is not consumed by basic comprehension but is available for
high-level negotiation and knowledge co-construction (Lam & Kapur, 2018). Second, during the individual preparation phase, TCSA
functions that channels the freed working-memory resources into germane cognitive load, namely schema construction, information
organization, and in-depth critical analysis (Lavi et al., 2023). Specifically, defining the Target helps students clarify the focal problem
and establishing information connections, and creating diagrams; creating Criteria clarifies the level and direction of problem solving
and provides standards for evaluating information; examining the Source supports the validation of GenAI-generated content and
mitigates hallucination risks; and drawing Analogies supports schema construction and transfer from similar cases to the current
problem. It structures the human-AI partnership by guiding students through a rigorous dialectic that involves defining the Target,
establishing evaluation Criteria, vetting the Source to mitigate AI hallucinations, and drawing Analogies, thereby fostering the critical
thinking processes (Ivanov, 2023; Johri et al., 2023). This strategy is designed to shift GenAI usage from a passive query-response
interaction into an active and evidence-based inquiry. PFC was selected as the macro-level sequencing framework. Its core function
is to guide students on when to engage with GenAI and with collaboration, and to regulate cognitive load by placing the GenAI-based
individual preparation phase before peer collaboration. TCSA was selected as the micro-level evaluation framework, grounded in
engineering-domain criteria checking and case-based analogy. Its focus is to support learners' problem-solving reasoning and to guide
students in how to critically analyze GenAI output. While the PFC-TCSA framework offers a theoretically sound approach to structure
GenAI use that could balance cognitive load and foster critical thinking, empirical evidence regarding its process-level patterns re-
mains limited. Existing research focuses on outcome-based measures, failing to capture how critical thinking patterns differ across
learning phases. It also remains unclear whether critical-thinking patterns differ among students with different levels of learning
performance.

To  address  this  gap,  this  study  employed  Epistemic  Network  Analysis  (ENA)  to  quantify  and  visualize  77  engineering  un-
dergraduates' critical thinking patterns in a PFC-TCSA structured, GenAI supported environment. Using engineering design as the ill-
structured problem solving context, this study aimed to characterize differences in cognitive co-occurrence patterns among students
with  different  levels  of  learning  performance  in  individual  and  collaboration  phases.  Specifically,  this  study  defined  human-AI
collaboration not merely as the operational use of a GenAI tool, but as a cognitive partnership grounded in the theory of Distrib-
uted Cognition (Hollan et al., 2000; Salomon, 1997). In this partnership, GenAI functions as an epistemic resource that extends stu-
dents' cognitive capacity. However, learners should also engage in a dialectical process of evaluating, refining, and integrating GenAI
responses rather than passively accepting them. Thus, this study shifts GenAI integration from tool adoption and prompt-response
optimization  toward  scaffold  design,  emphasizing  how  learners  define  problems,  establish  criteria,  verify  sources,  and  transfer
ideas through analogy when using GenAI. The research questions are as follows:

RQ1. What were the critical thinking patterns of engineering undergraduates during the individual preparation and group collab-
oration phases within the PFC-TCSA instructional flow?

RQ2. How  did  the  critical  thinking  patterns  during  the  individual  preparation  phase  differ  among  high-,  medium-,  and  low-
performing students?

RQ3. How did the critical thinking patterns during the group collaboration phase differ among students at different performance
levels?

2. Literature review

2.1. Dual impact of GenAI in education and human-AI partnership

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is fundamentally reshaping pedagogical approaches by offering unprecedented capa-
bilities for design ideation, simulation optimization, and personalized learning pathways (Habib et al., 2024; Johri, 2022). While early
discussions focused on GenAI tool adoption for productivity, recent theoretical perspectives advocate for a shift toward Human-AI

Teaming and Co-Creativity (Otto et al., 2025). Creative problem-solving emerges from the interaction between an individual and their
environmental resources (Lubart, 2005). In this context, GenAI can be conceptualized as a partner within a joint cognitive system
(Woods & Roth, 1988)or as an interactive agent that supports sustained engagement with learners (Sidner et al., 2005).

In  this  partnership,  GenAI  functions  as  an  extender  of  human  cognition,  offering  diverse  perspectives  that  trigger  divergent
thinking (Rana et al., 2025; Sajadi et al., 2024). However, this transformative potential is coupled with significant pedagogical risks,
primarily  stemming  from  unstructured  implementation.  A  significant  risk  is  automation  bias,  a  tendency  to  over-rely  on
GenAI-generated solutions without critical evaluation (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). When students treat GenAI merely as an oracle
rather than a teammate, they bypass the necessary cognitive struggle, leading to diminished analytical reasoning and undermining the
development of independent decision-making and creativity (Hou et al., 2025; Ivanov, 2023). This overreliance directly threatens the
development of students' evidence-based reasoning practices essential for solving ill-structured problems, which are competencies
explicitly required by accreditation standards (ABET, 2025).

Furthermore, unstructured GenAI use introduces risks to academic integrity and solution quality due to the propagated algorithmic
biases or technically inaccurate information arising from training data limitations (Ivanov, 2023). This creates a fundamental tension
between the efficiency of rapid solution generation provided by GenAI and the cognitive depth required for critical evaluation and
knowledge construction, a dichotomy that necessitates pedagogical frameworks to foster balanced human-AI collaboration (Park,
2026).

2.2. Critical thinking as core competencies in the GenAI era

In the GenAI era, the defining of competency has shifted from merely knowing to skillfully interrogation. This demands a fusion of
critical thinking (CT) and self-regulatory capabilities. Critical thinking (CT), defined as "purposeful, self-regulatory judgment that
involves interpretation, analysis, evaluation, and inferenc” (Facione, 1990), has been widely acknowledged as a competency directly
aligned with ABET accreditation requirements for "informed judgment" in problem-solving (ABET, 2025). However, the traditional
model of CT, which typically focused on critiquing human-generated content is insufficient for GenAI interactions. GenAI has intro-
duced transformative yet challenging impacts on CT development, such as hallucinations, algorithmic opacity, and the ease of passive
answer consumption that diminishes verification and self-monitoring (Gunawardena & Wilson, 2021; Hou, 2025; Karaoglan Yilmaz,
2023).

When engaging with GenAI, students with stronger CT skills critically assess the utility of an AI output, check it against their own
knowledge and predefined criteria, and adjust their problem-solving approach accordingly (Hou et al., 2025). Without iteratively
questioning AI-generated content, students might become vulnerable to overreliance. Empirical research indicates that students with
stronger prior CT skills tend to use GenAI as a critical thinking partner, while those with weaker CT skills exhibit a tendency for
uncritical acceptance (Hou et al., 2025). Therefore, shifting the instructional focus from mere GenAI access to decision-making quality
necessitates  designs  that  externalize  self-regulatory  and  metacognitive  processes,  for  instance,  by  embedding  pre-defined  criteria
setting and sustained, criteria-guided reasoning into AI interactions in education (Lee & Palmer, 2025; Zydney, 2008).

2.3. An integrated framework: synthesizing PFC and TCSA to manage cognitive load

This study introduced the PFC-TCSA framework, which moved beyond prompt engineering to function as an integrated cognitive
and instructional scaffold. The Preparation for Future Collaboration (PFC) paradigm provides the macro-level instructional sequence
primarily aimed at reducing extraneous cognitive load. By sequencing individual preparation before collaboration, PFC encourages
students to activate prior knowledge and grapple with the problem structure before facing the coordinative demands of collaboration
(Lam & Kapur, 2018). A systematic review by (Mende et al., 2021) synthesized experimental studies on individual preparation for
collaborative learning and showed that preparation can support key cognitive subtasks of collaboration, including retrieval, infer-
encing, and mutual referencing. The review also suggested that generative preparation tasks and cognitive group awareness support
can strengthen the benefits of preparation, although the effects vary depending on task design and collaboration context. Although the
empirical  base  of  PFC  is  still  developing,  recent  studies  have  provided  initial  evidence  for  its  value  in  engineering  collaborative
learning. In engineering design education, (Lyu et al., 2023) found that individual preparation improved students’ design-solution
quality and shaped their design behavior patterns, including problem definition, solution generation, analysis, and evaluation. (Lyu
et  al.,  2025)  further  showed  that  students  in  the  With  individual  preparation  condition  achieved  higher  collaborative  learning
scores and demonstrated more elaboration and synthesis, whereas students in the without individual preparation condition tended to
engage  in  more  superficial  exchanges.  Related  evidence  from  online  collaborative  learning  suggests  that  when  students  enter
collaboration with greater familiarity or readiness, they spend less effort regulating task-related activities and develop more critical
and exploratory group norms (Janssen et al., 2009). Together, these findings suggest that preparation before collaboration can reduce
coordination and comprehension demands, thereby freeing working-memory resources for productive peer interaction and knowledge
co-construction (Farrokhnia et al., 2019).

The  Target-Criteria-Source-Analogy  (TCSA)  strategy  provides  a  micro-level  cognitive  scaffold  that  explicitly  directs  the  freed
cognitive capacity toward germane cognitive load, which is required for schema construction and deep learning. TCSA operationalizes
the Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) cycle by providing a four-step protocol that operationalizes CT, which are defining the Target,
establishing Criteria, vetting the Source, and drawing an Analogy (Lavi et al., 2023). The four steps of TCSA correspond to the logic of
problem solving, analogical reasoning, and engineering design. In engineering education, design is commonly framed as a process of
identifying  needs,  developing  requirements,  generating  and  evaluating  alternatives  against  criteria  and  constraints,  and  making

trade-offs under standards and contextual limitations (ABET, 2025; Dym et al., 2005). The Target step supports problem representation
by requiring students to clearly identify the triggering causes, contextual scenarios, and scope of the target problem. This clear problem
representation provides the basis for evaluating AI-generated ideas and searching for relevant source cases. The Criteria step estab-
lishes standards for judging solution quality and identifying deep structural similarity beyond surface resemblance. The Source step
corresponds to source-problem retrieval and evidence seeking, where students need to identify a previously solved problem that shares
key criteria with the target problem and verify the credibility of the source information. The Analogy step then requires students to
explicitly link the target problem and the source problem through shared criteria, using this mapping to support creative engineering
design (Holyoak et al., 2001). Therefore, these four components were chosen because they collectively constitute a complete cognitive
process from problem framing, criteria-based evaluation, and source validation to analogical transfer. Prompt engineering mainly
supports users in formulating effective inputs, iteratively improving AI outputs, and critically evaluating whether generated responses
are accurate, relevant, applicable, and aligned with task requirements (Lee & Palmer, 2025; Walter, 2024). It primarily improves how
learners communicate with GenAI to obtain higher-quality AI responses. Whereas TCSA structures how learners think and supports
their  reasoning  process  by  guiding  them  to  define  the  problem  and  establish  evaluative  criteria,  with  GenAI  serving  only  as  an
accelerator for case retrieval, information verification, and analogy generation. The representation of self-determination based on
problems is fundamentally different from the template based and structured expression of ordinary prompt words. It reduces extra-
neous load associated with problem ambiguity by explicitly guiding students to plan interaction, structures the germane cognitive
work of evaluation via monitoring GenAI’s output against set standards, and engages students in the essential germane cognitive
activities such as reflecting on the applicability of solutions.

Thus, the synergy of PFC and TCSA represents approach not only sequential but also functional to structure human-AI collabo-
ration. Specifically, PFC creates the necessary cognitive space by minimizing extraneous load, and TCSA occupies that space with
productive, germane cognitive activities. This integrated approach transforms GenAI from a source of potential cognitive overload into
a tool for scaffolded knowledge construction.

Significant research gaps persist on their combined effect. Existing studies have examined PFC without GenAI integration, GenAI
use  without  structured  scaffolds  like  TCSA,  or  scaffolding  tools  devoid  of  generative  models  (Farrokhnia  et  al.,  2019).  Crucially,
performance-based variations in how students engage with this scaffolded AI environment remain largely unexplored. Little is known
about  whether  the  integrated  framework  could  benefit  learners  uniformly  or  amplifies  divergent  CT  patterns  between  high-  and
low-achieving students. This study aimed to address these gaps by proposing the unified PFC-TCSA-GenAI workflow and employing
Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to model the critical thinking patterns across high-, medium-, and low-performing student groups.

3. Methodology

3.1. Research context

This study was conducted in a course, Applications of Computers in Materials Science, offered to engineering students at a large
research university in central China. This course was selected because the design tasks involved are representative of ill-structured
problems  found  in  many  professional  disciplines,  requiring  iterative  decision-making  under  uncertainty.The  sixteen-week  course
focused on teaching and learning in an information-driven environment, aiming to enhance students’ ability to use computer tech-
nology to solve practical problems in materials science and engineering. The first eight weeks focused on knowledge learning through
engaging students in a variety of learning activities, such as attending instructor's lectures, Q &As, engineering simulations, and case
study, which supported the project-based learning (PjBL) based upon authentic engineering problems in the final eight weeks.

3.2. Participants

A total of 77 engineering students enrolled in this course participated in this study. The participants had already acquired foun-
dational professional knowledge but were unfamiliar with GenAI-enhanced engineering design activities; none had prior experience
with PFC or TCSA. All participants provided informed consent.

3.3. Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Wuhan University of Technology (Approval No. MSE20250919). The
study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to data collection, all participants
were provided with an information sheet explaining the study's purpose, procedures, potential risks, and their right to withdraw at any
time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All interaction logs, group discussion recordings,
and academic performance data were anonymized and stored securely to ensure confidentiality.

3.4. Procedure

This study was conducted within a project-based learning (PjBL) context, following the Preparation for Future Collaboration (PFC)
paradigm to structure a GenAI-supported instructional workflow (Fig. 1). Following this paradigm, the instructional workflow was
sequenced  into  two  distinct  phases:  an  individual  preparation  phase  with  GenAI  support,  followed  by  a  GenAI-prohibited  group
collaboration phase. Seventy-seven engineering students voluntarily formed groups of three to deal with an authentic engineering

design problem.

During the individual preparation phase, students engaged with the problem independently using GenAI tools (Kimi and Metaso),
guided by the Target-Criteria-Source-Analogy (TCSA) cognitive strategy. Rather than functioning merely as a prompting guide, this
framework was designed to operationalize a cognitive partnership by enforcing a structured Human-AI dialectic where epistemic
agency circulated between the learner and the machine to prevent passive acceptance (Table 1). The workflow began with a human-led
Target phase where the learner set the strategic intent and problem space, followed by a jointly negotiated Criteria phase where
students leveraged Kimi to broaden evaluation standards while retaining the authority to select the most relevant ones. The process in
the Source and Analogy phases, which were AI-supported but strictly human-evaluated, helped to mitigate the risk of AI hallucina-
tions. The Source step mandated the use of Metaso to retrieve verifiable and authentic precedents. This methodological constraint
established the credibility of the source problem material, allowing the learner to focus on determining the logical validity of the
Analogy for solution transfer. This TCSA scaffolded interaction aimed to foster students’  critical evaluation of GenAI outputs and
prepare for deeper collaboration. To consolidate their ideas, students engaged in identifying the two or three most novel ideas from
their overall thought process and providing a justification for why they selected them over other alternatives.

In the subsequent collaborative phase, students discussed their individually developed ideas in groups of three without access to
GenAI. This prohibition was intentional, shifting the focus to knowledge co-construction based on their preparatory work, thereby
assessing  the  efficacy  of  the  individual  cognitive  preparation.  The  discussion  was  guided  by  additional  two  reflective  questions
prompting the selection and justification of a final solution based on their established criteria: (c) From your selected novel ideas, select
the one you believe is most useful (valuable and effective) based on your established success criteria. Explain how it can be applied in
reality, and (d) Explain why your idea is useful and how it meets your success criteria for the target problem.

Three tools were adopted to build an intelligent learning environment. The Xiaoya platform (Fig. 2) served as the hub for task and
resource management. During the individual preparation phase, students interacted mainly with Kimi, a large language model (LLM),
while Metaso, a retrieval-augmented tool, was used specifically for the source-problem finding step in the TCSA process (Fig. 3). Kimi
was selected for its strong performance and free access. To address potential GenAI hallucinations, Metaso provided citation-linked
evidence  by  displaying  information  sources,  highlighting  key  passages,  and  matching  related  data,  which  helped  students  effi-
ciently locate and verify authentic references. After confirming a credible source problem and its solution in Metaso, students returned
to Kimi to continue the TCSA-guided individual preparation. Group collaboration was conducted on Tencent Meeting (Fig. 4), where
teams integrated insights generated during the individual phase into engineering design.

3.5. Data collection

Three types of data were collected to capture the learning process and outcomes. Process data included fine-grained logs of all
student interactions with the GenAI tools during the individual preparation phase, and audio recordings of the subsequent group
discussions, which were transcribed for Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA). To support students’ implementation of the TCSA strategy,
students were required to complete a structured reflection form after the task. The form prompted them to articulate each step of the
TCSA process, including the Target, Criteria, Source, and Analogy, and to document their interaction logs with AI tools. The reflection
form was submitted as part of the course materials and was used for instructional assessment. The reflection form used to scaffold
students’ TCSA-based reasoning is provided in Appendix.1. Performance data included students’ academic achievement, represented
by course grades that summarized performance on weekly assignments (15%), engineering simulations (35%), engineering design
reports (35%), and the final examination (15%). Weekly assignments mainly consisted of objective questions assessing basic con-
ceptual understanding. Engineering simulations and design reports were weighted more heavily because they were closely associated
with critical thinking. These tasks required students to interpret engineering scenarios, evaluate alternatives, justify decisions with
evidence, and reflect on design limitations. The final examination included both objective and subjective items. These course grades
were used for performance grouping. Therefore, the grouping criterion was independent of the study outcomes, including ENA-based
discourse patterns and the expert-rated solution score for the focal task, thereby avoiding circular reasoning. In order to compare and
analyze the critical thinking patterns of students with different levels of performance, they were divided into high, medium, and low
performance groups.

3.7. Data coding and analysis

The  core  analysis  was  conducted  on  the  transcribed  discourse  data  from  both  the  Human-AI  interaction  logs  and  discussion
transcripts. To analyze the temporal structure of students’ cognitive processes, a coding scheme based on Facione’s critical thinking
framework  (Facione,  1990),  which  includes  six  dimensions:  Interpretation,  Analysis,  Evaluation,  Inference,  Explanation,  and
Self-Regulation, was employed. Coding examples are provided in Table 4. Two trained researchers independently coded the entire
dataset. Inter-rater reliability, calculated using Cohen’ s Kappa, yielded a value of 0.802, indicating substantial agreement. Discrep-
ancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

The final dataset comprised a total of 1,638 coded discourse segments derived from both the Human-AI interaction logs in indi-
vidual preparation phase and the discussion transcripts in group collaboration phase. For the frequency-based analyses of the six
critical-thinking dimensions, binomial models were used to compare the prevalence of coded contributions across performance groups.
The original frequency-based binomial GLMs treated the coded discourse contributions as binomial trials and did not account for the
dependence arising from repeated contributions by the same student or, during collaboration, clustering within discussion groups.
Therefore, multilevel binomial generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) were used as the primary analyses. The preparation-phase
GLMMs included a student-level random  intercept, whereas the collaboration-phase GLMMs included random intercepts  for both
students and discussion groups. The frequency-based GLMs were retained as supplementary analyses. In addition, equal-weight stu-
dent-level proportion analyses were conducted as sensitivity checks to reduce the influence of differences in the number of coded
contributions generated by individual students. Pairwise comparisons among the high-, medium-, and low-performing groups were
adjusted using the Holm procedure. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and Holm-adjusted p values were reported.
Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) was selected as the primary analytical method to quantify the different critical thinking patterns
within the discourse, moving beyond mere frequency counts. ENA generates dynamic network models that represent how frequently
cognitive dimensions co-occur in conversation, thus revealing the structure of students’ problem-solving thinking (Arastoopour et al.,
2016).

4.2. Phase-specific prevalence of critical thinking segments

This section further addresses RQ1 by comparing the phase-specific prevalence of critical thinking segments across performance

During  individual  preparation,  the  primary  GLMM  identified  three  significant  pairwise  contrasts  for  Analysis  and  Explanation

CI [1.195, 4.573], * p_adj = .039). In contrast, the high-performing group had lower odds of Explanation contributions than both the
low-performing group (OR = 0.141, 95% CI [0.026, 0.779], * p_adj = .049) and the medium-performing group (OR = 0.130, 95% CI
[0.027, 0.631], * p_adj = .034). No other GLMM pairwise contrasts were statistically significant.

The supplementary frequency GLM  also identified  these three contrasts but yielded additional significant  differences in Inter-
pretation, Analysis, Inference, and Self-Regulation (Table 6). These additional contrasts were not significant in the GLMM and were
therefore not treated as primary findings. The equal-weight student-level model produced directionally similar estimates for the three
primary contrasts, although none remained significant after Holm adjustment (Appendix 2).

performing.  The  primary  GLMM  analysis  identified  significant  differences  only  in  Self-Regulation  (Table  7).  The  odds  of  Self-
Regulation were higher in the high-performing group than in the low-performing group (OR = 16.416, 95% CI [4.290, 62.821], *

not  significant. No  significant  group  differences  were found  in  the  other successfully  fitted  GLMMs.  The Analysis  GLMM  did  not
converge, and the Explanation GLMM produced a singular fit; estimates from these models were therefore omitted.

The supplementary frequency GLM identified the same Self-Regulation contrasts and additional differences involving Interpre-
tation, Analysis, and Explanation. Because these additional findings were not confirmed by valid GLMM estimates, they were treated as
supplementary evidence. The collaboration-phase estimates should be interpreted cautiously because the low-performing group was
small. These results provided complementary frequency-based evidence alongside the structural patterns identified through ENA.

5. Discussion

This  study  examined  critical  thinking  patterns  associated  with  a  scaffolded  GenAI  integration  framework  combining  the  PFC
paradigm and TCSA thinking strategy. The results suggest that this framework was associated with different patterns of engagement
across performance groups. Crucially, Epistemic Network Analysis uncovered fundamental disparities in their critical thinking pat-
terns. High-performing students showed stronger connections among analysis, evaluation, and self-regulation. We descriptively refer
to this observed pattern as a “Triangulation Loop.” In contrast, the network of low-performing students was more strongly concen-
trated  around  connections  between  interpretation  and  analysis,  with  fewer  prominent  connections  involving  evaluation  and  self-
regulation. We descriptively refer to this pattern as a “Descriptive Loop.”

5.1. Performance-group differences in critical-thinking patterns across phases

This section addresses RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 by synthesizing how students’ critical-thinking patterns differed across the individual
preparation  and  group  collaboration  phases  and  across  performance  groups.  The  labels  used  below  were  developed  post  hoc  as
interpretive descriptions of the observed network configurations. They are not intended to represent validated cognitive states, or
developmental stages. Distinct critical-thinking co-occurrence patterns were observed across performance groups within the scaffolded
PFC-TCSA-GenAI workflow. Across the two phases, high-performing students showed an individual preparation pattern descriptively
labeled the “Triangulation Loop” and a collaborative pattern descriptively labeled “Collective Regulation.” In the preparation phase,
learners  tightly  coupled  analysis  and  evaluation  to  triangulate  GenAI  outputs  with  valid  external  sources  and  their  own  domain
knowledge. In the collaborative phase, self-regulation emerged as the dominant organizing component, showing strong structural
integration with analysis and interpretation. Regulatory moves were embedded within core analysis processes, and were associated

14

D. Pan et al.

with more active guidance of collective sense-making. From a descriptive perspective, the cross-stage differences were characterized
by greater emphasis on evaluating AI-generated information during preparation and coordinating collaborative discussion during the
subsequent group phase.

From a descriptive perspective, the medium-performing group showed a preparation-phase pattern labeled “Transitional Inte-
gration” and a collaboration-phase pattern labeled “Interpretive Coordination.” In the preparation phase, Inference played a central
hub role and frequently co-occurred with Explanation and Self-Regulation, a pattern descriptively consistent with the integration of
inferential, explanatory, and self-regulatory discourse. During the collaboration phase, interpretation became the primary organiza-
tional hub, with analysis and self-regulation embedded in explanation driven communication. Self-regulation here appeared to be
associated with the continuity and coherence of group discourse, helping to maintain common understanding throughout the entire
collaborative phase. This configuration marks a shift from individual meaning construction to collective construction and maintenance
of common understanding.

From a descriptive perspective, the low-performing group showed a preparation-phase pattern labeled “Descriptive Loop” and a
collaboration-phase pattern labeled “Uncoordinated Elaboration.” During individual preparation, their discourse showed prominent
Explanation–Analysis co-occurrences, with comparatively weaker connections involving Self-Regulation. This reflects their tendency
to restate GenAI outputs rather than question them. This pattern is consistent with previous studies on automation bias (Parasuraman
& Manzey, 2010) and illusion of competence (Dell et al., 2023), which suggest that the fluency and apparent rationality of AI generated
responses can reduce learners’ perceived need for validation. This superficial participation continues into collaboration, with domi-
nant explanations and analysis and a lack of self-regulation indicating that their analysis and evaluation are often isolated judgments or
defenses of specific viewpoints, rather than efforts to coordinate and perfect the group. This trajectory describes an observed limitation
pattern where learners functioned as passive recipients during preparation and became unregulated analysts during collaboration,
engaging in local critique but failing to reach strategic consensus.

5.2. Performance differences in GenAI-supported engineering reasoning

This section mainly addresses RQ2 by interpreting the performance-based differences observed during the GenAI-supported in-
dividual preparation phase through the lens of the engineering expert-novice divide. From a distributed cognition perspective, the key
difference across performance groups in how cognitive responsibility was distributed between the learner and GenAI. In the high-
performing  group,  Analysis  was  more  strongly  integrated  with  Evaluation  and  Self-Regulation,  suggesting  that  GenAI  primarily
functioned as an epistemic resource while learners retained responsibility for evaluating, verifying, and regulating the use of AI-
generated  information.  In  contrast,  the  low-performing  group’s  network  was  more  concentrated  around  Interpretation  and  Anal-
ysis, suggesting that a larger proportion of their activity centered on understanding and organizing AI-generated information, with less
explicit evaluative and regulatory processes.

This suggests that in the AI era, monitoring is becoming more critical than generating. Although GenAI creates content easily, high-
achieving students demonstrate stronger metacognitive regulation. They utilized the "Self-Regulation" dimension of critical thinking to
actively monitor the AI’s output according to established criteria, transforming raw AI suggestions into systematic analysis, and make
appropriate  adjustments.  Similarly,  although  the  critical-thinking  patterns  of  medium  performers  were  more  fragmented,  their
discourse also showed self-regulation alongside iterative design refinement. The differences were primarily reflected in the extent to
which Analysis co-occurred with explicit evaluative and self-regulatory discourse.

5.3. The crucial role of self-regulation in collaboration and the paradox of AI scaffolding

This section addresses RQ3 by discussing how performance-group differences in self-regulation became evident during the AI-free
collaboration phase. Because cognitive load was not directly measured, the following account should be understood as a theoretically
informed  interpretation  of  the  observed  discourse  patterns  rather  than  as  evidence  of  a  cognitive-load  mechanism.  A  significant
divergence emerged in the collaborative phase, centered on self-regulation, amplifying the differences in critical thinking patterns
across performance groups. The intentional exclusion of GenAI in this phase provided an opportunity to explore whether the individual
preparation guided by TCSA might be reflected in more internally regulated collaborative reasoning. The high-performing groups’
high level of integration of self-regulation with analysis and interpretation was consistent with this possible transition, indicative of
effective engineering teamwork. These groups appeared better able to manage conflicts, weigh trade-offs, and move toward optimal
solutions (ABET, 2025). Their discourse suggested that they drew on knowledge developed during the individual preparation phase
and received more diverse feedback from their peers, which was accompanied by more detailed analysis and evaluation of solutions.
Conversely, the relative absence of regulation in low-performing groups’ discourse may help account for their tendency toward un-
coordinated idea elaboration rather than knowledge co-construction. This is consistent with Geng et al. (2024)’s finding that actively
accepting  others'  viewpoints  including  opposite  ideas  with  the  motivation  to  complete  complex  tasks  was  the  key  to  achieving
high-quality peer interactions.

This disparity could also be coherently explained through the integrated lenses of Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), especially by
examining the relationship between scaffold-induced load and cognitive offloading. Collaborative learning demands more cognitive
resources from learners than individual learning because students not only need to engage with learning materials but also need to
access externalized information from their peers (Elen & Clark, 2006; Kolfschoten et al., 2014; Webb, 2013). For higher-performing
students, the TCSA scaffold appeared to serve as a structure for offloading metacognitive processes and effectively externalized the SRL
cycle, guiding them to navigate the cognitive load of interacting with GenAI and direct their resources toward deeper metacognitive

D. Pan et al.

Thinking Skills and Creativity 63 (2027) 102380

engagement. Conversely, for lower-performing students, the introduction of TCSA may have added cognitive demands. The combined
cognitive demand of the complex task, the GenAI tool, and the TCSA scaffold itself may have contributed to cognitive overload. It is
within this context that GenAI may have increased the risk of superficial reliance. Specifically, their observed tendency to restate
GenAI  outputs  rather  than  question  them  is  consistent  with  automation  bias  (Parasuraman  &  Manzey,  2010)  and  the  illusion  of
competence  (Dell  et  al.,  2023).  Under  cognitive  strain,  the  fluency  and  apparent  rationality  of  AI-generated  responses  may  have
masked the need for validation and encouraged reliance on less demanding, superficial thinking as a cognitive coping strategy. This
finding contributes to the literature by showing that while the PFC paradigm generally reduces extraneous load (Lam & Kapur, 2018),
cognitive scaffolds alone are insufficient to overcome the illusion of exploratory depth created by GenAI's eloquent responses; the
cognitive cost of the specific cognitive scaffold (TCSA) must be carefully calibrated to avoid overloading novice learners in high-stakes
AI environments.

6. Implications

This study moved beyond conceptual arguments to provide process-oriented evidence regarding the integration of the Preparation
for Future Collaboration (PFC) paradigm with a specific cognitive strategy (TCSA) within a GenAI-supported learning environment.
The  integrated  design  was  associated  with  distinct  critical-thinking  patterns  across  the  individual  preparation  and  collaborative
phases. Higher-performing learners showed stronger integration of analysis, evaluation, and self-regulation. These findings suggest
that the PFC-TCSA framework may provide a practical model for organizing human-AI collaboration and shift attention from tool
adoption to the design of cognitive partnerships. Consequently, the PFC-TCSA framework offered a practical model for human-AI
collaboration, shifting the focus from tool adoption to the design of cognitive partnerships.

Crucially, this implies a fundamental pivot in AI pedagogy: future AI education should not merely focus on technical 'prompt
engineering,' but rather on 'cognitive orchestration,' which involves instructing students on how to design and monitor their own
cognitive processes. Based on the direction and degree of learners' thinking and problem-solving, prompting GenAI to provide similar
cases may encourage low-performing students to engage in the Evaluation step of TCSA, breaking their passive Descriptive Loop. With
greater support for this regulation, learners may function as active epistemic gatekeepers who verify and evaluate AI outputs to co-
construct understanding with others, rather than becoming passive recipients.

7. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single regional and instructional context, which may
limit  generalizability.  Second,  discourse  data  are  sequentially  dependent,  and  this  dependence  may  not  be  fully  captured  by  the
frequency-based binomial GLM analyses. Therefore, the GLM results should be interpreted as supplementary evidence alongside ENA
models  and  qualitative  excerpts.  Third,  cognitive  load  and  internalization  were  not  directly  measured.  As  a  result,  claims  about
cognitive  overload  should  be  understood  as  theoretically  informed  interpretations  rather  than  direct  evidence,  and  the  AI-free
collaboration  phase  can  only  explore  whether  TCSA-guided  preparation  was  reflected  in  later  collaborative  reasoning,  not  verify
internalization. Fourth, alternative explanations, such as prior knowledge, motivation, GenAI familiarity, and collaborative disposi-
tion, may also have shaped students’ critical thinking patterns. Finally, the collaboration subsample was not proportionally repre-
sentative of the full performance-group distribution, with high-performing students overrepresented and low-performing students
underrepresented  (n = 6). Therefore, collaboration-phase findings, particularly comparisons involving the low-performing  group,
should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should use larger and more balanced samples, directly measure cognitive load and
internalization, and include comparison groups such as PFC without TCSA, TCSA without PFC, GenAI use without either scaffold, and
conventional prompt-engineering guidance. They should also incorporate an independent fidelity check to examine whether and how
consistently students engage with the four steps of TCSA.

8. Conclusion

By synthesizing the PFC paradigm with the TCSA cognitive strategy, this study utilized GenAI in engineering undergraduates'
collaborative project-based learning. Epistemic network analysis revealed that the performance based difference on critical thinking
patterns lay on the integration of analysis and evaluation with metacognitive self-regulation. These findings should be interpreted as
PFC-TCSA framework relevant rather than evidence that PFC, TCSA, or GenAI independently produced these patterns.
