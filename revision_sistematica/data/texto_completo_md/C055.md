Abstract
Despite  the  increasing  use  of  generative  artificial  intelligence  (GAI)  in  educational  settings,  there  is  still  a  lack  of  con-
sensus  in  the  literature  regarding  its  specific  impact  on  various  components  of  college  students’  critical  thinking  (CT)
disposition. This research seeks to address this uncertainty by exploring the dual nature of GAI usage on CT disposition.
It  examines  perceived  self-efficacy  and  perceived  dependence  as  mediators,  which  influence  how  GAI  usage  affects  CT
disposition, and considers time pressure as a moderator that may alter these relationships. We applied organismic integra-
tion theory as the theoretical framework and used structural equation modeling to analyze data from 587 college students.
The  findings  indicate  that  GAI  usage  positively  affects  CT  disposition  through  perceived  self-efficacy,  likely  because  it
enhances students’ confidence in their abilities. However, it undermines CT disposition through perceived dependence, as
students  may  become  overly  reliant  on  GAI  for  problem-solving. When  comparing  the  two  mediating  pathways,  it  was
found  that  the  positive  influence  of  GAI  on  CT  disposition,  as  measured  by  increased  confidence  and  problem-solving
skills, outweighed the negative effects of perceived dependence. Additionally, time pressure weakened the positive influ-
ence of GAI on perceived self-efficacy and CT disposition. This study offers valuable insights into educational technology,
highlighting the connection between GAI usage and CT disposition and providing recommendations for integrating GAI
to foster CT skills.

Keywords  generative artificial intelligence · perceived dependence · perceived self-efficacy · time pressure · organismic
integration theory

vital for university students, helping them analyze informa-
tion to make informed decisions and fostering independent

With  the  swift  progression  of AI,  GAI  has  emerged  as
an  important  resource  for  many  college  students  in  both
their  studies  and  work,  significantly  impacting  their  cog-
nitive  processes,  including  creativity  and  problem-solving
(Mittal et al., 2024). Research suggests that GAI may alter
human thinking patterns by influencing creativity and prob-
lem-solving approaches (Mittal et al., 2024). For instance,
Huang’s (2024) study indicates that students’ creativity and
design thinking can be enhanced through GAI support. Sim-
ilarly, a study by Shanto et al. (2024) found that GAI use was
beneficial for idea generation and critical analysis compared
to independent work. Although these studies offer valuable
insights  into  how  GAI  affects  college  students’  CT,  there
is still debate about whether GAI enhances or hinders cog-
nitive  processes;  more  importantly,  existing  studies  rarely
explain why such inconsistent effects occur, resulting in a
lack of theoretical clarity (Abbas et al., 2024). Some schol-
ars  argue  that  GAI  does  not  foster  personal  development,
as  it  leads  to  over-reliance  on  technology  and  diminishes
self-directed  learning.  For  example,  excessive  reliance  on
GAI may reduce students’ self-directed analytical skills, as
they might become accustomed to AI-generated responses
instead  of  engaging  deeply  with  the  content  (Tlili  et  al.,
2023;  Yang  et  al.,  2025).  Furthermore,  excessive  reliance
on GAI could undermine independent thinking, as students
might  prioritize  ease  over  critical  engagement,  leading  to
a  reduction  in  self-directed  analytical  skills  (Yang  et  al.,
2025).

In reality, the influence of GAI on students’ CT disposi-
tion is not simply positive or negative, but depends on how
students internalize their reasons for using it. To address this
limitation, this study draws on organismic integration the-
ory (OIT), a sub-theory of self-determination theory (SDT),
which  explains  how  individuals  internalize  their  motiva-
tions when using technology. OIT views extrinsic motiva-
tion as existing on a continuum, from being fully controlled
by  outside  rewards  and  pressures  (external  regulation),  to
being more internalized and aligned with one’s own goals
and  values  (identified  or  integrated  regulation)  (Deci  &
Ryan, 1985, 2012). Put simply, if students see GAI as per-
sonally  meaningful  and  connect  it  to  their  learning  aims,
its use can boost their sense of competence, support active
engagement, and strengthen CT dispositions (Sun & Sugi-
sawa, 2024; Hmoud et al., 2024; Yun et al., 2025). However,
if  students  rely  on  GAI  mainly  to  meet  external  demands
or  to  reduce  stress,  their  motivation  remains  controlled  or
partly  internalized  (introjected  regulation),  which  is  more
likely to increase dependence and weaken independent ana-
lytical skills (Hasanein & Sobaih, 2023; Yilmaz & Yilmaz,
2023;  Wang  et  al.,  2024).  Using  OIT,  therefore,  helps  to
explain why GAI can, at the same time, both promote and

undermine CT disposition, depending on the depth of moti-
vational internalization.

Moreover, in the context of academic learning, time pres-
sure  is  a  common  factor  that  can  notably  affect  how  stu-
dents interact with educational tools, including GAI (Abbas
et al., 2024). Time pressure influences cognitive processes
by impacting attention, information retention, and decision-
making  (Phillips-Wren  & Adya,  2020;  Rojo  López  et  al.,
2021). From the perspective of OIT, time pressure moder-
ates  the  relationship  between  self-efficacy  and  CT  dispo-
sition.  When  students  are  under  time  pressure,  extrinsic
motivations  (such  as  completing  tasks  quickly)  become
more dominant, undermining intrinsic motivation (such as
the desire to engage deeply in analysis). As a result, the pos-
itive  influence  of  self-efficacy  on  CT  disposition  is  weak-
ened  because  students  prioritize  efficiency  over  reflective
thinking  (Kruglanski  &  Freund,  1983; Abbas  et  al.,  2024;
Kapoor et al., 2025). Meanwhile, time pressure exacerbates
the negative impact of perceived dependence on GAI. When
students feel reliant on GAI for quick solutions, their extrin-
sic  motivation  increases,  further  reducing  their  intrinsic
motivation to engage in independent, critical analysis. This,
in turn, weakens their CT disposition (Abbas et al., 2024;
Kapoor et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025). While prior studies
have explored various effects of GAI use, they have largely
overlooked  contextual  pressures,  such  as  time  constraints,
that  shape  these  dynamics  in  authentic  academic  environ-
ments.  Investigating  the  moderating  role  of  time  pressure
could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how
intrinsic and extrinsic motivations lead to varying CT out-
comes under different time constraints.

Given the increasing prevalence of GAI, understanding
its influence on college students’ CT is crucial. This research
seeks  to  explore  the  influences  of  different  motivations
behind  GAI  usage  and  examine  how  situational  factors,
such as time pressure, moderate this relationship. Drawing
on  OIT,  this  study  explores  how  different  forms  of  moti-
vational internalization shape the outcomes of GAI usage,
distinguishing  between  self-efficacy  enhancement  when
motivation is internalized and dependency when motivation
remains  externally  regulated.  This  research  contributes  in
three ways: (1) it analyzes the dual role of GAI, evaluating
its potential to support CT and the risks of dependency; (2)
it clarifies the role of motivation in the relationship between
GAI usage and CT disposition; (3) it introduces time pres-
sure  as  a  moderating  variable,  enhancing  our  understand-
ing  of  GAI’s  impact  in  academic  contexts. These  findings
offer the field of educational technology a new perspective
on how GAI can effectively support the development of CT
among university students, potentially guiding the design of
AI-driven educational tools and strategies.

GAI usage and CT disposition

GAI  refers  to AI  systems  capable  of  generating  creative
content,  such  as  text,  images,  and  music,  based  on  user
input or existing data. Unlike traditional AI, which relies
on predefined rules, GAI uses deep learning models to pro-
duce innovative outputs, mimicking human-like creativity
and  reasoning  (Cui  et  al.,  2024).  The  core  advantage  of
GAI lies in its ability to learn from vast datasets, allowing
it to generate highly relevant content tailored to the needs
of users. GAI has widespread applications, especially in
education, where it enhances learning efficiency, provides
personalized feedback, and boosts creativity (Yang et al.,
2025; Mittal et al., 2024). For example, ChatGPT, a widely
used GAI tool, offers instant academic support and assists
students  in  completing  various  academic  tasks  (Yang  et
al., 2025). However, while GAI provides efficient learn-
ing support, existing research has predominantly focused
on  outcomes  such  as  performance  and  creativity,  with
limited  attention  to  its  impact  on  students’  CT  disposi-
tion. Specifically, whether GAI can help maintain or even
strengthen  independent  thinking  while  supporting  learn-
ing is still an open question.

Research on GAI in education has mainly focused on its
ability  to  enhance  academic  performance,  creativity,  and
problem-solving skills (Mittal et al., 2024). However, there
has been limited exploration into how GAI usage impacts
students’ CT disposition. CT disposition refers to the habit-
ual tendency to engage in reflective, open-minded analysis
and critical evaluation of information (Sosu, 2013). While
some studies suggest that GAI can enhance students’ con-
fidence  and  problem-solving  abilities,  thereby  indirectly
supporting  their  CT  development  (Maedche  et  al.,  2019;
Mahande et al., 2026), excessive reliance on GAI may lead
to increased dependence on these tools, potentially diminish-
ing independent thinking (Hasanein & Sobaih, 2023; Daha
& Altelwany, 2025). Students may start to depend on GAI-
generated solutions rather than engaging in deeper analysis
and critical evaluation. This over-reliance could undermine
the  very  skills  required  for  developing  CT. Therefore,  the
impact  of  GAI  on  CT  disposition  is  not  one-dimensional;
it may both enhance and inhibit CT, depending on how stu-
dents interact with the tool.

To  address  this  limitation,  this  study  adopts  OIT  to
explain how different levels of motivation internalization
shape  the  dual  effects  of  GAI  usage  on  CT  disposition.
OIT posits that motivation exists along a continuum, rang-
ing from external regulation driven by external pressures

to  more  self-determined  internal  regulation  that  aligns
with  personal  goals  and  values  (Deci  &  Ryan,  2000).In
the context of GAI usage, if students view GAI as a tool
to  enhance  their  academic  abilities  and  integrate  it  into
their  learning  objectives,  it  can  boost  their  self-efficacy,
thereby  promoting  CT  disposition  (Hmoud  et  al.,  2024;
Yun et al., 2025). However, if students primarily use GAI
to  meet  external  demands,  such  as  completing  assign-
ments quickly or addressing time constraints, their moti-
vation remains externally regulated, leading to increased
dependency on the tool (Daha & Altelwany, 2025). This
external  reliance  weakens  their  independent  analytical
skills, thus inhibiting CT. Thus, the effect of GAI on CT
disposition depends not only on the tool itself but also on
the internalization of students’ motivation. The dual role
of GAI highlights the need for a balance, while GAI can
promote  CT  disposition  through  enhanced  self-efficacy,
excessive  dependence  can  suppress  the  development  of
independent thought.

The mediating role of perceived self-efficacy
between GAI usage and CT disposition

Self-efficacy  refers  to  an  individual’s  belief  in  their  abil-
ity to accomplish tasks or achieve goals, which profoundly
influences  motivation,  behavior,  and  cognition  (Bandura,
1989). According to OIT, self-efficacy is not solely a result
of intrinsic feelings of competence but also stems from the
way individuals internalize extrinsic motivations (Deci &
Ryan, 1985, 2012). OIT outlines a continuum of motivation,
where extrinsic motivations range from external regulation
to more autonomous forms, such as integrated regulation,
which align with an individual’s values and goals (Deci &
Ryan,  1985;  Ryan  &  Deci,  2000).  When  individuals  per-
ceive that their efforts are supported by tools that facilitate
autonomy  and  competence,  such  as  GAI,  their  motiva-
tion  becomes  more  intrinsic,  enhancing  their  perceived
self-efficacy.

In the context of GAI usage, tools like AI-driven educa-
tional platforms offer students opportunities for autonomous
learning  by  providing  real-time  feedback  and  resources
(Hess  et  al.,  2024).  This  autonomy  allows  students  to  set
personalized  goals,  independently  tackle  challenges,  and
expand their skills, which aligns with more self-determined
forms of motivation (Yan et al., 2024; Hmoud et al., 2024;
Zheng et al., 2024). As a result, GAI can increase students’
sense  of  competence  and  control,  reinforcing  their  confi-
dence in accomplishing tasks. Some studies suggest that AI-
based applications enhance students’ perceived self-efficacy
by enabling them to handle complex tasks more effectively,

offering  them  a  sense  of  mastery  (Maedche  et  al.,  2019;
Bayaga,  2024; Yun  et  al.,  2025;  Bai  &  Wang,  2025).  For
example, AI-supported learning approaches, such as those
used in AI-based games, have been shown to improve stu-
dents’ computational thinking and learning abilities, further
fostering a sense of competence (Bayaga, 2024; Mahande et

propose the following hypothesis:

H1a  GAI usage can positively influence the perceived self-
efficacy of college students.

Additionally,  perceived  self-efficacy  has  been  shown  to
play  a  critical  role  in  shaping  individuals’  CT  disposi-
tion, which is defined as the habitual tendency to analyze
information and approach problems logically (Sosu et al.,
2013). The  OIT  offers  a  useful  lens  to  explain  this  rela-
tionship: when students experience stronger self-efficacy,
they are more likely to feel competent, thereby satisfying a
basic psychological need that supports more autonomous
forms of motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci,
2000). This heightened sense of competence strengthens
their  willingness  to  engage  with  demanding  intellectual
activities,  including  CT.  Empirical  studies  provide  fur-
ther evidence for this mechanism: individuals with higher
self-efficacy demonstrate greater capacity to evaluate and
synthesize information (Yurt, 2025), show less avoidance
of cognitive challenges, and exhibit stronger tendencies to
question  assumptions  and  analyze  complex  issues  (Deh-
ghani  et  al.,  2011).  Recent  findings  reinforce  this  link-
age across diverse contexts, confirming that self-efficacy
is a reliable predictor of CT disposition (Fu et al., 2023;
Bai & Wang, 2025). Therefore, we propose the following
hypothesis:

H1b  Perceived self-efficacy can positively influence the col-
lege students’ CT disposition.

The  preceding  discussion  suggests  that  perceived  self-
efficacy  may  serve  as  a  key  mediating  mechanism  in
the relationship between GAI usage and CT disposition.
Empirical research indicates that GAI tools enhance stu-
dents’  self-efficacy  by  fostering  competence  and  auton-
omy  through  personalized  feedback  and  opportunities
for mastery (Maedche et al., 2019; Bayaga, 2024). At the
same time, higher levels of self-efficacy have been consis-
tently linked to stronger CT dispositions, as students with
greater confidence are more likely to engage in complex
reasoning,  question  assumptions,  and  evaluate  informa-
tion  critically  (Dehghani  et  al.,  2011;  Fu  et  al.,  2023).
Therefore,  these  findings  suggest  that  the  positive  effect
of  GAI  usage  on  CT  disposition  operates  partly  through

students’ perceived self-efficacy. Accordingly, we propose
the following hypothesis:

H1c  Perceived  self-efficacy  mediates  the  positive  rela-
tionship  between  GAI  usage  and  college  students’  CT
disposition.

The mediating role of perceived dependence
between GAI usage and CT disposition

Perceived dependence refers to the feeling that one cannot
effectively  complete  tasks  without  relying  on  a  particular
tool or technology (Campion et al., 1993; Shu et al., 2011).
Such  dependence  develops  when  external  resources  are
treated as indispensable for performance. The OIT suggests
that  external  motivation  can  range  from  doing  something
purely  to  meet  outside  demands  to  doing  it  because  one
has fully accepted it as part of their own goals and values
(Ryan & Deci, 2000; Deci & Ryan, 2012). When reliance on
tools remains at the less internalized end of this continuum,
behavior  is  maintained  by  external  pressures  rather  than
autonomous  commitment,  thereby  fostering  dependence.
Applied to GAI, this means that frequent use may encour-
age students to lean on its ready-made answers and instant
feedback  instead  of  internalizing  skills  as  their  competen-
cies  (Gammoh,  2024).  Empirical  studies  have  shown  that
frequent use of AI-related technologies can increase users’
dependence on these tools, as they become accustomed to
AI-generated assistance (Hasanein & Sobaih, 2023). There-
fore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2a  GAI  usage  can  positively  influence  the  perceived
dependence of college students.

Furthermore,  increased  perceived  dependence  on  GAI
may  weaken  students’  CT  disposition  (Gammoh,  2024).
CT disposition refers to the willingness to engage in inde-
pendent,  reflective,  and  evaluative  thinking  (Sosu  et  al.,
2013;  O’Reilly  et  al.,  2022).  When  students  rely  heavily
on  GAI,  they  are  less  likely  to  practice  these  skills  them-
selves  because  the  tool  supplies  immediate  answers  and
evaluations (Hasanein & Sobaih, 2023). OIT proposes that
external  influences  on  behavior  can  be  taken  up  at  differ-
ent  levels:  when  internalization  is  weak,  actions  are  car-
ried out mainly to satisfy external demands or obtain quick
outcomes, rather than from genuine personal endorsement
(Ryan & Deci, 2000; Deci & Ryan, 2012). In this situation,
reliance  on  GAI  keeps  students’  learning  at  these  lower
levels  of  regulation. As  a  result,  they  may  approach  tasks
with the goal of obtaining solutions rather than engaging in
reasoning, which reduces opportunities to question, evalu-
ate, and reflect. Over time, this pattern reinforces a passive

less  frequent  (Gammoh,  2024;  Daha  & Altelwany,  2025).
Empirical  studies  confirm  this  mechanism,  showing  that
strong  dependence  on  external  tools  often  correlates  with
declines in critical engagement and analytical ability (Hasa-
nein & Sobaih, 2023; Singh et al., 2023). Therefore, we pro-
pose the following hypothesis:

H2b  Perceived  dependence  can  negatively  influence  col-
lege students’ CT disposition.

Building  on  the  above  arguments,  perceived  dependence
can  be  understood  as  a  psychological  mechanism  linking
GAI usage to students’ CT disposition. According to OIT,
when external tools are habitually used, reliance may pre-
vent full internalization of learning practices, keeping regu-
lation  at  externally  controlled  levels  (Ryan  &  Deci,  2000;
Deci & Ryan, 2012). This sustained reliance, while initially
driven  by  the  convenience  of  immediate  solutions,  gradu-
ally  reduces  opportunities  for  autonomous  engagement  in
reasoning  processes  (Hasanein  &  Sobaih,  2023).  In  this
way, perceived dependence serves not only as an outcome
of  GAI  usage  but  also  as  an  explanatory  pathway  for  its
potential adverse effects on CT. Therefore, we propose the
following hypothesis:

H2c  Perceived  dependence  mediates  the  negative  rela-
tionship  between  GAI  usage  and  college  students’  CT
disposition.

The moderating role of time pressure

In modern work and life, time pressure, defined as the per-
ception of insufficient time to complete tasks, is one of the
most pervasive stressors ( Szollos, 2009; Gusy et al., 2021).
It often compels individuals to prioritize speed and efficiency
over  in-depth  analysis,  which  can  limit  reflective  engage-
ment such as CT (Phillips-Wren & Adya, 2020; Rojo López
et al., 2021; Kruglanski & Freund, 1983). Although short-
term focus may sometimes improve performance (Kronen-
wett & Rigotti, 2019), the overall effect of time scarcity is
to constrain systematic processing. Organismic Integration
Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000) highlights that this effect is not
purely  cognitive  but  also  motivational.  When  motivation
is  externally  regulated  or  introjected,  time  pressure  rein-
forces  surface-level  strategies  aimed  at  mere  completion.
In contrast, when motivation is internalized to identified or
integrated regulation, individuals are more likely to sustain
reflective engagement despite temporal constraints.

& Freund, 1983; Decker et al., 2022). In contrast, when effi-

viduals are better able to sustain deeper processing despite
temporal  constraints,  consistent  with  findings  that  autono-
mous  motivation  supports  persistence  and  higher-quality
learning under pressure (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Evans et al.,
2024; Kapoor et al., 2025). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3a  Time  pressure  moderates  the  relationship  between
perceived  self-efficacy  and  CT  disposition,  such  that  the
greater the time pressure, the weaker the positive relation-
ship between perceived self-efficacy and CT disposition.

Perceived dependence generally inhibits CT disposition, as
individuals who feel reliant on others may be less inclined to
engage in independent analysis or problem-solving (Hasa-
nein & Sobaih, 2023; Singh et al., 2023). Within organismic
integration theory, perceived dependence-controlled regula-
tion (external or introjected) is associated with a heterono-
mous perceived locus of causality and with surface-oriented
rather than reflective processing (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Deci
& Ryan, 2012). Under low time pressure, contextual slack is
associated with limited appraisal and partial internalization
toward identified regulation, so highly dependent individu-
als may still exhibit some CT disposition (Bilancini et al.,
2024).  Under  high  time  pressure,  temporal  constraints  are
associated with stronger controlled regulation and reduced
perceived autonomy, and limited time is linked to resource
depletion and greater reliance on routines or external input,
patterns more evident among those high in perceived depen-
dence;  temporal  scarcity  also  depletes  cognitive  resources
required  for  deliberation,  further  biasing  against  reflective
processing (Bilancini et al., 2024; Kapoor et al.2025; Fan et
al., 2025). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3b  Time  pressure  moderates  the  relationship  between
perceived dependence and CT disposition, such that higher
time pressure strengthens the negative relationship between
perceived dependence and CT disposition.

Method

Analysis strategy

In studies related to exploring the use of GAI and college
students’ propensity for CT, previous researchers have used
a variety of research design methods, including cross-sec-
tional surveys (Abbas et al., 2024; Yun et al., 2025; Mah-
ande et al., 2026), experimental designs (Huang et al., 2024;
Shanto et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025), and longitudinal stud-
ies (Leite, 2025).

Each  research  design  method  has  its  advantages  and
limitations when exploring the impact of GAI on students’
CT  disposition.  Cross-sectional  surveys  mainly  collect
self-reported data through questionnaires, allowing for the
rapid gathering of large volumes of data and revealing cor-
relations between GAI usage and students’ CT tendencies.
However,  cross-sectional  designs  cannot  provide  causal
inferences,  limiting  the  in-depth  understanding  of  GAI’s
mechanisms (Wang & Cheng, 2020). Experimental designs,
by  controlling  experimental  conditions  and  manipulating
independent variables, allow for a clearer understanding of
causal relationships. Yet, since experiments are often con-
ducted  in  artificial  environments,  their  ecological  validity
is low, making it difficult to fully reflect the complexity of
real-world academic contexts. In contrast, longitudinal stud-
ies  can  track  the  long-term  effects  of  GAI  usage,  offering
a more comprehensive understanding. However, they typi-
cally require more time and resources, and may face chal-
lenges such as sample attrition.

Considering  factors  such  as  cost,  time,  and  research
resources, this study adopts a cross-sectional online survey
approach and uses PLS-SEM for data analysis. Online data

Participants and procedure

In this study examining the influence of GAI usage on col-
lege  students’  CT  disposition,  we  employed  a  geographi-
cally  stratified  sampling  approach  for  data  collection.
Specifically, we collaborated with student organizations at
six universities across different regions of China: two in the
eastern coastal areas, two in the central region, and two in
the western region. This stratified approach enabled a more
accurate representation of regional differences, thus enhanc-
ing the reliability and validity of the findings.

The  survey  was  distributed  online  using  the  Question-
naire Star platform and further promoted by student associa-
tions through social media to reach a broad range of target
respondents  regardless  of  geographic  location.  The  ques-
tionnaire consisted of two main sections: the first collected
demographic information and data on GAI usage to assess
sample  characteristics;  the  second  focused  on  five  core
constructs of this study, serving as the basis for subsequent
data analysis and hypothesis testing. To minimize common
method bias, items within each construct were randomized

respondents should be at least 18 years of age to complete it.
The study was approved by the author’s institutional ethics

participants to withdraw at any time.

Data collection took place from May to June 2024, ini-

cleaning,  including  the  removal  of  responses  completed

51.45% male and 48.55% female participants. Most respon-

Measures

The survey instrument in this study is adapted from widely
recognized  and  validated  scales  in  international  research.
These  scales  were  selected  for  their  established  reliability
and  validity  in  previous  academic  studies. To  ensure  their
relevance to the current research context, we made appro-
priate  modifications  to  the  original  scales. A  7-point  Lik-
ert  scale  was  used  for  all  items,  ranging  from  “strongly
disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (7). Before formal distri-
bution, two quantitative research experts reviewed the ques-
tionnaire, followed by a pilot test with 35 students, yielding
positive feedback.

The  GAI  usage  scale  was  adapted  from  Abbas  et  al.
(2024),  originally  designed  to  measure  ChatGPT  use,
and consists of eight items that assess the extent to which
students  utilize  GAI  for  various  academic  purposes. The
perceived dependence scale, adapted from Campion et al.
(1993), includes 3 items measuring the degree of reliance
on  GAI  tools  in  completing  academic  tasks.  This  scale
primarily captures functional dependence in task comple-
tion, which aligns with the focus of this study on students’

Page 7 of 16  1033

academic  use  of  GAI.  The  perceived  self-efficacy  scale,
based  on  Pintrich  and  De  Groot  (1990),  consists  of  nine
items  that  assess  individuals’  confidence  in  their  ability
to use GAI to complete academic tasks, achieve goals, or
attain desired outcomes within specific academic contexts.
The time pressure construct was measured using four items
adapted from prior studies (Amiruddin, 2019; Gusy et al.,
2021),  grounded  in  the  conceptualization  of  time  con-
straints  and  urgency  proposed  by  Dapkus  (1985).  These
items  were  contextualized  to  reflect  students’  perceived
time pressure in academic tasks. The CT disposition scale
was  adopted  from  Sosu  (2013)  and  consists  of  11  items
measuring  individuals’  tendency  to  think  critically.  This
scale includes two dimensions: critical openness (7 items),
which  reflects  openness  to  new  ideas  and  a  willingness
to  revise  one’s  viewpoints;  and  reflective  scepticism  (4
items), which captures the tendency to question evidence
and  learn  from  past  experiences. All  measurement  items
are  presented  in Appendix  Table  5.  In  the  questionnaire,
“ChatGPT”  was  used  as  an  illustrative  example  of  gen-
erative AI  tools.  Participants  were  instructed  to  consider
generative  AI  systems  with  similar  functionalities  (e.g.,
AI writing assistants or large language model-based tools)
when responding to the items.

Control  variables  included  gender,  age,  education
level, and disciplinary background. These were included
to  account  for  potential  confounding  influences,  as  they
may be associated with both GAI usage patterns and CT
dispositions. For instance, disciplinary background (e.g.,
humanities  vs.  STEM)  often  shapes  academic  practices
and  emphasis  on  critical  inquiry,  while  education  level
may  reflect  differences  in  cognitive  development  and
autonomy. Controlling for these factors helps isolate the
unique contribution of the primary predictors to variations
in CT.

Common method bias(CMB) and multicollinearity

To address CMB, we employed Harman’s single-factor test.
The  results  indicated  that  a  single  factor  explained  only
32.63% of the variance. Thus, CMB has minimal influence
on  the  findings  (Podsakoff  et  al.,  2012).  Furthermore,  to
assess multicollinearity, we computed the VIF for each vari-
able in the inner model. In general, a VIF value exceeding
5.00 indicates significant multicollinearity issues (Hair et al.,
2019). The analysis showed that all VIF values ranged from
1.00 to 1.30, indicating no serious multicollinearity issues.
This demonstrates the robustness of the model estimates.

Model explanatory, predictive power, and model fit

In  PLS-SEM,  the  coefficient  of  determination  (R²)  and
cross-validated  redundancy  (Q²)  are  crucial  indicators  for
evaluating the explanatory power and predictive relevance
of  a  model,  respectively.  In  this  study,  all  the  R²  values
surpass  the  recommended  threshold  of  0.10,  as  suggested
by Bagozzi and Yi (1989), indicating that the model has a
good explanatory power. Regarding the model’s predictive
relevance,  the  Q²  values  are  examined,  with  the  threshold
for  acceptable  predictive  power  being  greater  than  zero.
Bagozzi and Yi (1989) emphasize that when Q² values for
endogenous  variables  exceed  zero,  the  model  effectively
demonstrates predictive relevance. This study confirms that
all Q² values are greater than zero, supporting its capacity to
predict  the  endogenous  variables  effectively. Additionally,
the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) value
is 0.07, indicating a good model fit as it is below the gener-
ally accepted threshold of 0.08, further validating the good
overall model fit (Hair et al., 2019).

Discussion

Discussion of the results

The  results  of  this  study  show  that  the  GAI  usage  can
enhance students’ CT disposition by increasing their per-
ceived self-efficacy. This finding aligns with the principles
of OIT, which suggests that as motivation becomes more
internalized, individuals engage more proactively in learn-
ing and experience improvements in their cognitive abili-
ties and self-efficacy (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2012). Recent
studies  also  support  this,  indicating  that  GAI  tools,  by
providing  immediate  feedback  and  personalized  support,
boost students’ confidence and enhance their CT when fac-
ing complex academic tasks (Hmoud et al., 2024; Shanto
et  al.,  2024;  Yun  et  al.,  2025).  Specifically,  GAI  offers
students  a  tailored  learning  experience,  which  increases
their sense of control over academic tasks, thereby foster-
ing the application of CT (Maedche et al., 2019; Mittal et
al., 2024; Mahande et al., 2026). This process is consistent
with OIT, as students with more confidence are more likely
to  actively  engage  in  problem-solving  using  CT(Deci  &
Ryan, 1985, 2012).

However,  despite  the  positive  effects  of  GAI  on  self-
efficacy,  excessive  use  of  GAI  tools  appears  to  create  a
negative  impact  by  increasing  students’  perceived  depen-
dence  on  the  technology,  which  in  turn  inhibits  their  CT

disposition. This result is consistent with recent studies by
Gammoh (2024) and Hasanein and Sobaih (2023), who note
that over-relying on technological tools can reduce students’
capacity for independent analysis and problem-solving. The
“external regulation” concept within OIT explains this, sug-
gesting that when students’ motivations are primarily driven
by external support, their dependence on the tool increases,
limiting  their  internal  motivation  for  independent  analysis
(Ryan & Deci, 2000). While GAI tools provide convenience,
this  study  highlights  that  long-term  over-dependence  on
these  tools  could  undermine  students’  ability  to  engage  in
CT independently(Gammoh, 2024).

Moreover, this study reveals the moderating role of time
pressure  in  the  relationship  between  GAI  usage  and  CT
disposition, particularly in terms of perceived self-efficacy.
We  found  that  time  pressure  weakens  the  positive  impact
of  perceived  self-efficacy  on  CT  disposition.  This  finding
aligns  with  Kruglanski  and  Freund’s  (1983)  study,  which
suggests that under time pressure, individuals tend to adopt
simplified decision-making strategies rather than engage in
deep  analysis,  which  reduces  the  application  of  CT  (Phil-
lips-Wren  & Adya,  2020).  OIT  further  explains  this  phe-
nomenon,  emphasizing  that  when  students’  motivation  is
externally  regulated,  time  pressure  intensifies  the  external
motivators, causing students to prioritize completing tasks
quickly  over  engaging  in  reflection  and  deeper  thinking
(Kruglanski  &  Freund,  1983;  Decker  et  al.,  2022). There-
fore, even when students have high self-efficacy, they may

still prioritize efficiency over in-depth analysis under time
pressure, which diminishes the application of CT.

Interestingly,  time  pressure  did  not  moderate  the  nega-
tive  effect  of  perceived  dependence  on  CT  disposition.
From the standpoint of OIT, perceived dependence reflects
a  relatively  stable  form  of  controlled  regulation,  in  which
behavior is driven by reliance on external tools rather than
autonomous motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Deci & Ryan,
2012). Once such dependence becomes habitual, individu-
als tend to adopt an externally oriented locus of causality,
which  consistently  suppresses  reflective  and  independent
thinking (Ryan & Deci, 2000). As a result, its negative effect
on  CT  disposition  becomes  relatively  insensitive  to  situa-
tional factors such as time pressure. In addition, perceived
dependence on GAI can be understood as a form of cogni-
tive offloading, whereby individuals routinely delegate ana-
lytical  effort  to  external  systems  (Risko  &  Gilbert,  2016).
This habitual reliance reduces the need for active cognitive
engagement  regardless  of  temporal  constraints.  Accord-
ingly, time pressure weakens the positive pathway but does
not  further  intensify  the  negative  pathway,  suggesting  an
asymmetric moderating pattern.

Theoretical implications

This study builds upon OIT, expanding its application to the
context of GAI and students’ CT disposition. OIT, as devel-
oped by Deci and Ryan (1985), posits that motivation is not
dichotomous  but  exists  on  a  continuum  from  extrinsic  to
intrinsic forms, where behaviors are driven by both external
rewards  and  internal  values. This  framework  allows  us  to
examine how GAI usage influences student motivation and,
in turn, their CT disposition. Previous studies have empha-
sized the importance of autonomy in fostering deep learning
and CT (Deci & Ryan, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2000). By show-
ing that GAI can enhance CT through perceived self-efficacy
(an  intrinsic  motivational  pathway)  while  simultaneously
hindering  it  through  perceived  dependence  (a  controlled
motivational pathway), this study provides new insights into
how GAI impacts student cognition beyond its mere func-
tionality. This aligns with existing literature on the role of
technology in education, which highlights that the benefits
of  educational tools  like GAI  are not solely dependent on
their capabilities but on how they are integrated within the
motivational  landscape  of  learners  (Huang,  2024;  Hmoud
et  al.,  2024).  The  study  thus  advances  our  understanding
of  how  motivational  processes,  particularly  self-efficacy
and dependence, influence the effectiveness of educational
technologies.

In line with this, the study introduces the “double-edged
sword” effect of GAI on students’ CT disposition. Previous
research  on  educational  technology  has  highlighted  that

digital tools can have both positive and negative effects on
learning  outcomes  (Hmoud  et  al.,  2024).  In  this  context,
GAI usage positively influences CT by enhancing perceived
self-efficacy,  which  strengthens  students’  motivation  and
engagement (Bandura, 1989; Bai & Wang, 2025; Mahande
et al., 2026). However, the study also finds that excessive
reliance on GAI, as indicated by increased perceived depen-
dence,  can  negatively  affect  CT,  as  students  may  become
overly reliant on the tool for answers rather than engaging
in  reflective,  independent  thinking  (Gammoh,  2024). This
dual  mediation  model  supports  that  the  learning  tool  can
also lead to dependency and reduced cognitive engagement.
By  confirming  that  the  positive  influence  of  self-efficacy
outweighs the negative influence of dependence, this study
underscores  the  importance  of  balancing  autonomy  and
control  in  the  design  and  implementation  of  educational
tools like GAI.

Furthermore,  this  study  enriches  the  theoretical  under-
standing  of  GAI’s  impact  on  CT  disposition  by  introduc-
ing time pressure as a moderating factor. Time pressure is
a well-documented factor that can influence cognitive pro-
cesses  and  learning  outcomes.  Under  high  time  pressure,
students are often forced to prioritize efficiency over reflec-
tive thinking, which can undermine the benefits of compe-
tence-supporting tools like GAI (Ryan & Deci, 2000). The
findings show that time pressure weakens the positive effect
of perceived self-efficacy on CT disposition, supporting the
idea that external constraints can interfere with the motiva-
tion  to  engage  deeply  with  learning  materials  (Kruglanski
& Freund, 1983; Decker et al., 2022). However, the study
also finds that time pressure does not moderate the negative
influence  of  perceived  dependence,  suggesting  that  once
dependence on GAI is formed, it remains resistant to situ-
ational factors like time pressure. This finding adds a new
layer to the understanding of how  GAI  interacts with stu-
dents’ cognitive and motivational processes, indicating that
while time pressure can reduce the benefits of GAI, it does
not diminish the risks associated with dependence.

Practical implications

This  study  demonstrates  that  GAI  tools  can  effectively
enhance students’ self-efficacy, a critical factor in fostering
CT  disposition.  Therefore,  educational  administrators  and
curriculum  developers  should  strategically  design  courses
and teaching activities based on the characteristics of GAI
tools  to  enhance  students’  independent  thinking  abilities.
Specifically, GAI tools, such as ChatGPT and other text gen-
eration tools, can be used as auxiliary tools for information
collection and initial analysis, helping students expand their
thinking  boundaries.  However,  educators  should  clearly
define the contexts in which GAI tools are used to prevent

1 3Current Psychology (2026) 45:1033students from over-relying on these tools for direct answers.
To avoid dependence on these tools, teachers should encour-
age students to engage in deeper analysis and critique after
using GAI tools, prompting them to integrate AI-generated
content with their independent thinking.

Furthermore,  instructional  strategies  should  focus  on
how to ensure GAI effectively supports learning while rein-
forcing students’ independent thinking. The excessive reli-
ance on GAI can undermine students’ analytical skills, thus
affecting their CT disposition. Teachers should design tasks
that use GAI as an auxiliary tool rather than the main tool
for  completing  tasks.  Specific  practices  include  designing
multi-phase  tasks  that  require  students  to  conduct  inde-
pendent  critical  evaluations  and  in-depth  exploration  after
using GAI for initial information gathering or analysis. For
example,  project-based  learning  or  problem-based  learn-
ing could be incorporated, where students first use GAI to
gather  information  but  are  required  to  independently  con-
duct research and problem-solving, thus enhancing their CT
abilities.

Additionally,  this  study  emphasizes  the  impact  of  time
pressure on students’ CT, particularly through its influence
on self-efficacy. Students are less likely to engage in deep
and reflective thinking when they are under temporal con-
straints. Educators should manage time constraints in learn-
ing  tasks  to  support  students’  cognitive  engagement,  for
example by breaking tasks into manageable steps, allowing
flexible timelines, and providing sufficient opportunities for
reflection. However, it is important to note that time pres-
sure does not moderate the relationship between perceived
dependence and CT disposition. This suggests that students’
reliance on GAI is not primarily driven by situational time
constraints. As a result, reducing time pressure alone may
not effectively mitigate the negative impact of dependence
on CT. Educators should therefore complement time man-
agement strategies with instructional approaches that guide
students to engage more actively with AI-generated content,
rather than relying on it passively.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that also point to  direc-
tions  for  future  research.  First,  although  perceived  depen-
dence  and  time  pressure  were  measured  using  established
scales, both constructs were treated in a relatively simplified
manner.  In  reality,  GAI  dependence  may  involve  multiple
dimensions, including not only functional reliance but also
emotional  and  cognitive  dependence,  while  time  pressure
may  differ  in  forms  such  as  perceived  urgency  or  chronic
constraints.  Future  research  could  therefore  develop  and
validate  more  comprehensive,  GAI-specific  measurement
scales to capture these nuances more accurately.

Page 13 of 16  1033

Second, the present study primarily focuses on individ-
ual-level motivational mechanisms and does not explicitly
consider the broader learning context in which GAI is used.
In particular, GAI-facilitated peer interaction and collabora-
tive  learning  may  shape  students’  motivation  and  engage-
ment in ways that interact with self-efficacy and dependence.
Future studies could further examine how these social and
motivational  processes  operate  in  AI-supported  learning
environments.

Finally, the sample was drawn from Chinese university
students, which may limit the generalizability of the find-
ings. In addition, as the study is based on cross-sectional
data, causal inferences should be interpreted with caution.
Future research could adopt longitudinal or experimental
designs to better capture the dynamic relationships among
GAI usage, motivation, and CT disposition and to provide
stronger  evidence  of  causality.  Given  that  educational
practices and cultural contexts can influence how students
engage  with  GAI,  future  research  is  also  encouraged  to
test  the  model  across  different  cultural  and  institutional
settings.

Conclusions

This study examined the influences of GAI usage on col-
lege  students’  CT  disposition,  focusing  on  the  mediating
roles of perceived self-efficacy and perceived dependence,
and  the  moderating  role  of  time  pressure.  By  using  the
PLS-SEM method, we explored these relationships based
on OIT The primary findings reveal that GAI usage posi-
tively  influences  CT  disposition  through  enhanced  per-
ceived  self-efficacy,  while  it  negatively  influences  CT
disposition through increased perceived dependence. The
positive influence of perceived self-efficacy outweighs the
negative influence of perceived dependence, indicating an
overall positive influence of GAI usage on CT disposition.
Additionally, time pressure weakens the positive influence
of perceived self-efficacy on CT disposition but does not
moderate the relationship between perceived dependence
and CT disposition.

These  findings  offer  several  practical  and  theoreti-
cal  implications.  Practically,  educators  and  policymak-
ers should balance the use of GAI with opportunities for
independent  learning  to  prevent  over-reliance  and  main-
tain  CT  skills.  They  should  also  manage  time  pressure
and foster intrinsic motivation to maximize the benefits of
GAI. Theoretically, the study extends OIT by highlighting
the dual effects of GAI on CT disposition and the moder-
ating role of time pressure, providing an understanding of
the complex interplay between GAI usage and cognitive
development.
