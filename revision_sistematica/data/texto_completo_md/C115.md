Using AI in Life Cycle Assessment Education: Insights from Higher
Education and Guidelines for Responsible Integration

Abstract

As artificial intelligence tools become increasingly present in  academic contexts, with the advancement of large language models, their impact
on  the  teaching  and  learning  of  life  cycle  assessment  raises  both  opportunities  and  concerns. This  paper  presents  the  results of  a  case  study
conducted across higher education institutions, involving surveys and interviews with students and educators. The objective was to investigate
how  AI  tools  are  currently  used  in  LCA-related  courses,  assess  their  perceived  benefits  and  limitations,  and  identify  recommendations  for
responsible pedagogical integration. Findings reveal that AI supports accessibility, creativity, and exploration in learning LCA, but also introduces
risks such as oversimplification, loss of critical thinking, and the reproduction of inaccurate or non-transparent results. Based on the collected
insights, a structured set of recommendations was proposed in the form of a preliminary set of guidelines tailored for both educators and students.
These guidelines aim to foster meaningful, ethical, and methodologically robust uses of AI in LCA education. By aligning AI-driven approaches
with the rigor of LCA principles, this work contributes to shaping future pedagogical strategies that balance the benefits of AI with the integrity
of sustainability education.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) broadly refers to the discipline of
creating  machines or software capable of intelligent behavior
that  is,  performing  tasks  that  normally  require  human
intelligence  [1].  AI  is  classified  based  on  their  capability
(Artificial Narrow Intelligence, Artificial General Intelligence,
and  Artificial  Superintelligence)  or  by  methodology
(supervised  learning,  Unsupervised  learning,  Reinforcement
learning,  Deep  learning,  Natural  Language  Processing,  and
Computer vision). AI use was accelerated for the grand public
with the release of the model GPT-3.5 of OpenAI in November
2022.  These  AI  models  are  deep  learning  approaches  that
power  today’s  large  language  models  (LLMs).  These  LLMs
fall under generative AI, a branch of limited-memory AI that

produces new content (text, images, etc.) by leveraging patterns
learned  from  vast  datasets  [2].  These  models  have  impacted
most of the human activities including education and life cycle
assessment (LCA).

LCA  is  a  methodology  for  quantifying  the  environmental
impacts  of  products  or  processes  over  their  entire  life  cycle,
from  raw  material  extraction  to  end-of-life.  The  interest  to
integrate  AI  into  LCA  has  been  increasing.  The  previous
approaches to 2022 were predominantly ML to handle complex
data and streamline analysis. A review of Romeiko  et al. [3]
analyzed forty peer-reviewed articles that reported the joint use
of  ML and  LCA  for quantitative sustainability assessment at
multiple  LCA  stages:  for  generating  or  estimating  life  cycle
inventory  data,  predicting  life  cycle  impact  indicators,

Applications of AI in education varied from chatbots to AI-
driven personalized learning platforms, and provided a broad
spectrum of tools [4]. The usage of AI in science education has
shown  that  it  encourages  participation  in  the  educational
process,  but  it  limits  ability  to  understand  particular  subject

remain limited and not structured [6]. To understand the risks
and opportunities  of integrating AI into  LCA education, it is
necessary  to  understand  the  frameworks  that  have  been
developed for teaching LCA.

This article aims to explore the pedagogical potential of AI
in  LCA  education,  and  to  formulate  guidelines  for  their
effective and ethical use.  The present study seeks to identify
how students and educators use AI to support tasks in an LCA
course and what benefits and drawbacks arise from these uses.
In addition,  it examines the potential risks and limitations of
using AI in the LCA classroom and how can these be mitigated
thought  guidelines  to  assist  educators.  This  was  conducted
through  a  mixed  empirical  design  combining  an  initial
questionnaire and structured interviews with teachers. First, a
literature  review  was  carried  out  on  the  use  of  AI  in  LCA
education, following the framework of Viere et al. [8] (Section
2).  Second,  an  empirical  study  was  conducted  through
structured interviews with  teachers,  using examples from  the
review  to  guide  discussion  across  the  framework  topics
(Section  3).  Finally,  the  findings  were  further  examined
through follow-up interviews to compare perceptions, and were
discussed  in  relation  to  the  recent  French  policy  on  AI
education and the LCA skills framework proposed by Burnley
et al. [7] (Section 4).

The literature review was structured around the framework
proposed by Viere et al. [8] to identify how AI is applied in (i)
educational  activities  in  LCA,  (ii)  case-study/project  work
related  to  LCA,  and  (iii)  software/databases  used  in  LCA
contexts.  The  results  were  classified  in  three  groups:  AI  in
LCA/Sustainability  Education (explicit
linkage  of  AI,
education,  and  LCA),  Potential  Case-Study  Tool (AI–LCA
applications  without  explicit  educational  framing),  and  AI  in
Education (AI in education without a direct LCA/sustainability
focus). Given the scope of this conference paper, the analysis
was focus on Group 1. A brief synthesis of Group 2 was added
to highlight transferable insights for LCA teaching, and reserve
a full treatment of Groups 2-3, with Group 3 outside our LCA
scope, for a separate journal article.

Nguyen et al. [12] approach pedagogical chatbots through a
value‑sensitive  design  framework.  They  foreground identity,
connectedness, well‑being, trust, and accountability as design
values that should shape prompts, feedback, and data practices.
Their findings show that value‑aligned chatbots can increase
learner agency and reduce uncritical acceptance of outputs by
making assumptions and limits visible. Płoszaj‑Mazurek and

that can  guide architects in optimizing design parameters for
sustainability, and educating designers about the environmental
implications  of  their  decisions.  They  also  expose  fragile
integration  points:  database  mapping  mismatches,  unit  and
normalization  errors,  version  drift,  and  weak  audit  trails.
Without  rigorous  data  management and  versioning,  such
pipelines compromise reproducibility and risk double counting
or biased scenario design.

3.

Empirical findings (from interviews)

The  empirical  phase  began  with  an  online  questionnaire
designed  around  Viere  et  al.’s  framework  [8],  distributed
through  established  networks  in  eco-design  and  sustainable
engineering education. Due to a low initial response rate, this
approach was replaced by one-to-one structured interviews to
encourage participation and collect richer qualitative insights.

15 teachers from seven institutions and engineering schools
(14 in France and one in Italy) participated in these interviews.
While  the  sample  predominantly  represents  French  higher
education,  it  also  includes  one  international  case,  allowing
limited yet valuable comparative reflection.

The interviews explored whether, in the teachers’ view, their
students  had  already  made  use  of  LLMs  in  these  areas,  and
whether  they  would  recommend  or  not  their  usage.  To
stimulate  discussion,  selected  examples  of  AI  usage  drawn
from the literature review were presented, enabling participants
to reflect on both observed practices and potential applications
within their teaching context. The findings are reported in the
following subsections.

understanding  of  impacts.  Regarding  life  cycle  thinking,
purposes, and terminology,  teachers often associated AI with

3.3.

Reported risks:

Teachers  consistently  cautioned  against  significant  risks,
particularly in methodological and quantitative areas of LCA.
AI outputs were often considered too generic or insufficiently
scientific.  One  teacher  noted  that  results  were  “not  enough
precise,  and  not  compliant  with  the  ISO  standards”.  The
scientific risk here is that inaccurate or vague guidance could
lead students  to  misunderstand core frameworks that support
the credibility of LCA studies.

Teachers  implied  AI  would  be  inappropriate  to  their
students  for  LCI  and  modeling.  LCI  construction  requires
traceable  datasets,  unit  processes,  and  allocation  procedures.
Since  AI  cannot  access  or  manipulate  actual  databases  with
methodological  rigor,  reliance  on  it  would  compromise
reproducibility,  which  is  a  fundamental  principle  in  both
science and education.

Teachers worried about the unverifiable nature of AI outputs
in interpretive tasks, especially in regards to impact assessment
and  sensitivity  analysis.  While  AI  might  generate  plausible
narratives, such texts lack empirical grounding. This represents
a pedagogical risk in which students may confuse fluency with
correctness, undermining the development of critical judgment.
Almost  unanimously,  teachers  rejected  AI’s  relevance  to
special  types  of  LCA  and  mathematical  foundations.  These
topics  involve  complex  methodological  or  mathematical
reasoning where approximation or oversimplification leads to
substantial scientific errors. Allowing students use AI in these
areas  risks  degrading  their  competence  in  the  technical
backbone of LCA.

Regarding case studies and applications, teachers expressed
concern about over-reliance. Drafting case study reports with
AI  may  reduce  effort  in  communication  but  also  diminishes
opportunities
to  articulate  methodological
reasoning themselves, which is a key component of assessment
in LCA education.

Discussion

The  benefits  and  risks  map  strongly  onto  the  linguistic
versus  methodological  divide  in  LCA  teaching.  Teachers
acknowledged  AI’s  ability  to  support  textual  fluency  and
exploratory engagement (definitions, introductions, case report
writing). However, they converged in identifying serious risks
when AI intersects with core methodological competence (ISO
compliance,  database  use,  quantitative  modeling).  From  a
scientific perspective, this reflects the epistemological nature
of  LCA.  The  field  rests  on  standards  (ISO  14040/44),  data
transparency,  and  methodological  reproducibility.  These  are
domains where probabilistic text generation cannot s ubstitute
for empirical rigor. In contrast, the introductory dimensions of
LCA,  such  as  introducing  sustainability  concepts  or  framing
interpretations, are more open to linguistic mediation, where AI

4.1.

Preliminary guidelines for AI use in LCA education

Teachers  recommended  integrating  AI  cautiousl y  and
transparently, under supervision, and only after students master
core  LCA  principles.  From  the  15  responses,  a  consensus
emerged on using AI for rephrasing or summarizing, delaying
its introduction until foundational competence is secured, and
systematically verifying outputs.

Risks  to  avoid  include  over-simplification,  early  or
uncontrolled  use,  and  substitution  of  reasoning  with  AI-
generated text.

From  these  insights  and  extended  discussions  during  the

interviews, differentiated guidelines emerge:

For educators:

● Require manual work before permitting AI use.
● Encouraging  peer  comparison  between  AI-assisted

and manual work was also proposed.
● Provide criteria for evaluating AI outputs.
● Use in-class presentations or handwritten components

to ensure mastery of core skills.

● Establish institutional rules for when and how AI can

be integrated.

For students:

● Always  cross-check  AI  content  against  course

materials and trusted sources.

● Reflect on its influence on the learnings.
● Practice  critical  comparison  of  AI suggestions  with

traditional methods.

Overall, the evidence points toward a balanced integration, in
which AI can enhance comprehension and discussion, but only
under strict supervision and after foundational methodological
skills are secured.

Conclusions

Analysis  of  the  results  shows  that  AI  is  mainly  used  by
students  in  introductory  and  textual  tasks,  such  as  basic
understanding  of  environmental  impacts (73%),  life-cycle
thinking (60%), and wider context framing (47%), while its use
in  methodological  or quantitative  domains  is  nearly  absent
(<15%).  Reported  benefits
include  summarization  and
rephrasing (67%), time-saving support (53%), and stimulation
of debate (40%). The main risks are oversimplification (80%),
unverified reliance on AI outputs (67%), and bypassing critical
reasoning (60%).  AI  can  support  learning  efficiency  and
communication,  but  only  after  students  acquire  manual
competence, with systematic verification of outputs and under
teacher supervision.

Overall,  the  analysis  confirms  that  AI’s  pedagogical
contribution in LCA education remains primarily linguistic and
organizational
than  methodological.  Teachers
consistently recommend conditional use of AI, but only once
students  have  achieved  manual  competence,  with  systematic
verification and under close supervision.

A  limitation  of  this  exploratory  work  is  its  restricted
geographical scope and sample size; future research will extend
the  empirical  base  and  refine  the  practical  guidelines  into  a
more comprehensive framework for AI-assisted LCA teaching.
