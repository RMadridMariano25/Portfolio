Abstract

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is rapidly reshaping higher education, especially
in technology-oriented programs where critical thinking and complex problem solving are
core outcomes. This study triangulates global and local evidence on performance/efficiency,
usage, autonomy, critical-thinking engagement, and ethics by combining a systematized
review informed by Kitchenham and structured using selected PRISMA 2020 elements
(2020–2025; last search: May 2025; 49 studies; Scopus, ACM Digital Library, IEEE
Xplore, and SpringerLink; not prospectively registered) with an anonymous survey
of 302 computing and engineering students from a single university in Ecuador. The
expert-reviewed instrument showed acceptable internal consistency for most scale-based
dimensions (McDonald’s ω), whereas institutional and ethics-related items were analyzed
primarily at the item level. Results showed near-universal academic GenAI use (96%), with
47% of students reporting weekly use and 26% daily use. Research-related work was the
most frequent application (81.5%), followed by homework (48.3%), report writing (43.7%),
and exam preparation (41.7%). Although students reported perceived efficiency gains,
concerns persisted about reduced analytical engagement and technological dependence
(84.1%). Ethical concerns centered on dependence, authenticity, and data privacy, while
institutional responses pointed to the need for formal training (96.7%) and clearer guidance.
Based on this triangulation, we propose a context-bounded interpretive framework
suggesting that GenAI’s educational value depends on instructional and governance
conditions that preserve autonomy, critical thinking, integrity, and equity.

Keywords: Generative AI; computing education; higher education; technology-oriented
programs; student autonomy; critical thinking; academic integrity; assessment design;
teacher training

The rapid adoption of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), particularly tools
such as ChatGPT, is reshaping learning practices in higher education. A growing body
of work reports benefits in student writing, programming support, and conceptual
comprehension [1–3]. At the same time, the literature also highlights concerns related
to cognitive dependence, shallow learning, and ethical dilemmas around authorship and

authenticity [4–6]. Across these perspectives, a consistent conclusion is that GenAI’s
educational value depends less on the tool itself than on pedagogical guidance and
institutional governance, especially when authorship, data privacy, and oversight remain
insufficiently addressed [7].

These tensions are especially salient in technology-oriented programs such as
computing and engineering, where students must develop not only technical proficiency
but also higher-order competencies, including critical thinking, complex problem solving,
and learning autonomy. In these contexts, GenAI can be beneficial when it supports iterative
work—such as idea checking, experimentation, debugging, or code validation—and when
students are required to justify decisions and verify outputs; however, without guidance
or assessment constraints, it may reduce analytical engagement and displace reasoning
processes central to professional formation in computing disciplines [8–12]. Understanding
how GenAI relates to core learning dimensions in technology curricula is therefore essential,
particularly in programs that seek to preserve intellectual autonomy, interpretive validity,
and cognitive effort in authentic tasks [13,14].

To situate this problem, our systematized review synthesized 49 studies published
between 2020 and 2025. The evidence shows a recurrent dual pattern: positive
outcomes—most often reported as perceived efficiency gains and improved task
performance—are more consistently observed when GenAI use is instructor-mediated
and embedded in activities requiring verification, reflection, and justification [15–17].
In contrast, passive or unguided interaction is repeatedly linked to reduced cognitive
involvement and increased confidence in erroneous outputs [9,18]. These findings
align with established perspectives in educational research, including self-regulated
learning [13,14], cognitive load theory [19], and academic integrity debates [20].

Recent work in computing education has begun to triangulate literature and
stakeholder evidence on GenAI adoption, pointing toward more integrative analyses
of benefits, risks, and instructional responses [21]. Nevertheless, important issues remain
unresolved: studies focused specifically on technology-oriented programs are still limited,
and combined analyses of perceived academic efficiency/performance, usage patterns,
autonomy, critical-thinking engagement, and ethical considerations remain comparatively
scarce. These gaps motivate the present five-dimensional analysis.

Despite the rapid growth of GenAI research in education, empirical evidence
remains geographically uneven. Much of the published work originates from Europe,
North America, and Asia, while contributions from Latin America—and Ecuador in
particular—remain limited [22,23]. This imbalance is not only geographic; it also limits
understanding of how GenAI is adopted in higher education settings where institutional
regulation, digital infrastructure, teacher preparation, and governance conditions may
differ substantially from those reported in the Global North. As a result, contextualized
evidence remains limited on how the widely reported benefits of GenAI coexist with risks
related to autonomy, critical thinking, academic integrity, data privacy, and institutional
governance in Latin American technology-oriented programs.

Accordingly, the research gap addressed in this study is threefold: first, empirical
evidence focused specifically on computing and engineering students remains limited;
second, relatively few studies examine the combined relationship between perceived
academic efficiency/performance, usage, autonomy, critical-thinking engagement, ethical
concerns and institutional conditions related to guidance, trust, and privacy; and third,
evidence from Latin American higher education remains underrepresented. These gaps
matter because the educational implications of GenAI depend not only on the technology
itself, but also on disciplinary expectations, instructional practices, and institutional
conditions.

In response, this study provides contextualized empirical evidence from a Latin
American, technology-oriented higher education setting. By combining the review with
survey data from 302 students enrolled in computing and engineering programs at a
university in Ecuador, this study analyzes how GenAI use is associated with perceived
academic efficiency/performance, usage patterns, autonomy, critical-thinking engagement,
and ethical concerns in this population, while using the review component to interpret
these findings against the broader international evidence base. This triangulation allows
perceived efficiency gains to be interpreted alongside pedagogical tensions involving
analytical engagement, autonomy, and integrity, while highlighting the role of instructional
mediation and institutional conditions in sustaining cognitive depth in technology-oriented
training. In doing so, the article contributes integrated evidence from an underrepresented
regional context and frames GenAI adoption as a pedagogical and governance issue rather
than merely a matter of tool availability.

2. Methodology

This study followed a sequential mixed-methods design combining a systematized
review with a cross-sectional survey of higher education students enrolled in
technology-oriented programs (computing and engineering). The design supported
triangulation: the review component identified the main themes, tensions and gaps
reported internationally, while the survey captured students’ practices and perceptions in a
local context.

2.1. Systematized Review

The planning and execution of the review were informed by the methodological
guidance proposed by Kitchenham [24], widely adopted in software engineering and
computer science to structure review processes and support traceability [25]. The reporting
of the review was structured using selected PRISMA 2020 elements [26] to improve
transparency and methodological traceability across the stages of identification, screening,
eligibility assessment and synthesis.

2.1.1. Research Questions

The research questions (RQ) were derived from gaps and recurring tensions in the
literature on GenAI in higher education, with particular emphasis on technology-oriented
programs. They guided both phases of the study by structuring the review screening
and synthesis and informing questionnaire design. For this review, the target population
was limited to undergraduate students in university-level computing and engineering
programs, excluding secondary and postgraduate populations.

2.1.2. Sources and Search Strategy

The review covered studies published between January 2020 and May 2025, a period
in which GenAI tools became widely accessible and began to scale in higher education,
particularly after the release and diffusion of systems such as ChatGPT. Database searches
were conducted iteratively between March and May 2025, and the final update of the search
set was completed in May 2025. The counts reported in Appendix A correspond to the
outputs recorded during the original search process in 2025 and used during screening.

The search strategy used Scopus as the main interdisciplinary source and was
complemented by ACM Digital Library and IEEE Xplore to capture domain-specific
literature in computing- and engineering-related higher education. SpringerLink was
searched as an additional publisher platform to identify potentially relevant studies
available through that source. Because the search was organized around RQ-specific
constructs and a bounded disciplinary scope
focused on computing- and
engineering-oriented higher education, it should be interpreted as focused rather than
comprehensive. Accordingly, this study is presented as a systematized review and does
not claim exhaustive retrieval of all GenAI-in-higher-education studies.

Search strings combined GenAI terms (i.e., “Generative AI”, “ChatGPT”, “LLM”) with
higher education keywords and dimension-specific concepts aligned with RQ01–RQ05,
autonomy and verification,
including performance/efficiency, usage patterns,
critical-thinking engagement, and ethical/institutional conditions such as integrity, policies,
training, and trust. To maintain focus on technology-oriented training, terms such as
computing, engineering, and programming were also included. Whenever possible,
screening prioritized studies explicitly focused on undergraduate students enrolled in
university-level computing and engineering programs.

As an illustrative example of the query-construction logic, the search strategy
combined GenAI-related terms with higher education keywords and dimension-specific
concepts aligned with each research question. Depending on the platform, additional
database-level filters supported by the source interface were applied during execution.
Source-specific search formulations and the corresponding recorded outputs are reported
in Appendix A.

2.1.3. Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied at three stages—title, abstract, and
full-text screening—to ensure alignment with the review objectives. The criteria were
organized into six dimensions (see Table 1), with full details provided in Appendix A.
To improve traceability, the criteria were operationalized progressively across screening
stages. Title screening was used to remove records that were clearly outside the scope
of the review based on population, educational context, GenAI focus, publication type,
language, or publication period. Abstract screening then assessed whether the remaining
records addressed at least one analytical dimension aligned with RQ01–RQ05 and provided
sufficient relevance to justify full-text assessment. Full-text screening was finally used to
confirm eligibility and resolve borderline cases.

These criteria ensured consistency between the review objectives and the selected
studies and provided the basis for subsequent quality appraisal using the Mixed Methods
Appraisal Tool (MMAT) and evidence certainty considerations using GRADE.

2.1.4. Quality Assessment

To ensure methodological rigor, the evaluation was carried out at two complementary
levels:
individual quality of the studies using the MMAT [27] and the GRADE
framework [28], incorporated to strengthen the synthesis of findings and align them with
best practices in reviews within education and interdisciplinary contexts.

•

Evaluation with MMAT

Included studies were assessed with MMAT across qualitative, quantitative, and
mixed-methods designs. Three researchers applied the criteria independently and resolved
discrepancies by consensus. Overall judgments considered both the number of criteria
met and the presence of critical methodological weaknesses, such as limited group
comparability, measurement validity, or analytical grounding.

Studies were classified into four categories: high (4–5 criteria met without critical
flaws), medium (2–3 criteria with at least one relevant weakness), low (≤2 criteria or
multiple deficiencies), and not applicable (reviews or studies without primary data, used
only as context). Appendix B reports the MMAT criteria (Table A3) and the complete
evaluation matrix (Table A4).

•

Certainty of Evidence with GRADE

Certainty of evidence was synthesized by considering risk of bias, indirectness,
consistency, precision, and publication bias. MMAT served as an input for these judgments
rather than as a standalone metric. An overall certainty level was assigned for each
analytical dimension linked to RQ01–RQ05.

Risk of bias across studies, including potential publication bias, was assessed
qualitatively within GRADE. Because of methodological heterogeneity and the absence
of pooled quantitative effect measures, formal statistical procedures such as funnel plots
were not applicable. Publication bias and indirectness were therefore incorporated into the
overall certainty judgments.

The results indicated moderate certainty for perceived academic efficiency/performance
(RQ01), use and frequency (RQ02), and critical-thinking engagement (RQ04), although these
findings were limited by sample size and between-study variability. Certainty was low for
autonomy and understanding (RQ03), reflecting the smaller number of available studies
and the predominance of indirect evidence. Likewise, certainty was low for ethics and
integrity (RQ05) because of heterogeneous outcomes and likely publication bias. Studies

2.1.5. Results of the Selection Process

The study-selection process was structured using PRISMA 2020 reporting elements.
After deduplication, titles/abstracts and full texts were independently screened by three
reviewers; disagreements were resolved by consensus, and no automation tools were used.
A total of 463 records were identified across four databases. After removing 70 duplicates
(15.12%), 393 articles remained for title and abstract screening. At this stage, 328 records
(70.84%) were excluded, mainly because they fell outside higher education; did not focus
on undergraduate students; did not address GenAI/LLM-based tools; were not situated in
technology-oriented contexts related to computing, programming, or engineering; did not
address the analytical dimensions defined in RQ01–RQ05; or corresponded to ineligible
publication types. Title screening removed clearly out-of-scope records, whereas abstract
screening assessed alignment with the target population, intervention, and outcomes of
interest. This process left 65 records for full-text assessment.

Of these 65 reports, 16 articles (3.45%) were excluded after full-text reading because
they were not aligned with the predefined inclusion scope in terms of population, domain,
or educational level (n = 8); did not sufficiently address the research dimensions defined in
RQ01–RQ05 (n = 5); or had methodological limitations that prevented their integration into
the synthesis (n = 3). The full-text stage was especially important for studies that appeared
potentially relevant during abstract screening but, upon detailed examination, did not
provide sufficient methodological or substantive evidence for inclusion. Detailed reasons
for exclusion at this stage are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

In total, 49 records were included: 44 primary empirical studies synthesized in the
evidence analysis, plus 5 systematized reviews used exclusively for contextualization
(i.e., not included in the MMAT scoring or GRADE synthesis). Data extraction from
the included full-text reports was performed independently by three reviewers using a
predefined extraction form aligned with RQ01–RQ05. Discrepancies were resolved by
consensus, and no automation tools were used.

application of scope-related criteria at title screening, substantive eligibility criteria

2.2.1. Instrument Design

The questionnaire was derived from the analytical categories identified in the review
and aligned with RQ01–RQ05. Its dimensions were grounded in established educational
frameworks, including self-regulated learning [13,14], cognitive load theory [19], and
academic integrity debates [20], ensuring construct relevance and coherence with prior
research in computing and technology-oriented education.

The instrument included five-point Likert items (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly
agree), multiple-choice questions, dichotomous items, and open-ended questions. It was
organized into four blocks: (i) sociodemographic data, (ii) frequency and purposes of
GenAI use, (iii) perceived impact on learning (operationalized as perceived academic
efficiency/performance, autonomy, and critical-thinking engagement), and (iv) ethical
aspects and academic integrity together with institutional conditions (i.e., training needs
and guidance). The full questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Open-ended responses were analyzed through directed content analysis [29], with
independent coding by three researchers and consensus-based resolution. The coding
framework was deductively derived from RQ01–RQ05 and from the five analytical
dimensions established in the review: perceived academic efficiency/performance,
usage patterns, autonomy and verification practices, critical-thinking engagement, and
ethical/institutional concerns. Responses were first assigned to these a priori categories,
and recurrent subthemes were then identified within each dimension during the coding
process. All responses were anonymized beforehand. Qualitative findings are reported in
Section 3.2.4, where the thematic categories and representative testimonies are presented
in relation to the research questions.

2.2.2. Coding and Grouping of Items and Dimensions

Responses were transformed into numerical values following a standardized coding
scheme. Likert items were mapped onto a 1–5 scale, with higher values reflecting a stronger
presence of the evaluated trait [30]. Negatively worded items were reverse-coded to avoid
consistency bias [31]. Dichotomous questions were coded as binary (0 = No, 1 = Yes) and
multiple-choice questions were converted into independent dummy variables [32].

The analysis distinguished scale-based dimension scores and categorical variables,

aligned with the RQs, as follows:

(autonomy), and RQ04 (critical-thinking engagement).
Categorical variables: RQ02 (frequency and purposes of GenAI use, including declared
use, frequency, and academic activities) and RQ05 (ethical and institutional items,
including multiple-response ethical concerns, reported descriptively; see Appendix C,
Table A7).

For data processing, initial coding was performed in Excel and subsequently in Python

2.2.3. Validation of the Instrument

The validity and reliability of the questionnaire structured into five dimensions derived
from the systematized review and anchored in pedagogical frameworks of self-regulated
learning, cognitive load, and academic integrity were assessed following best practices
for the development of educational instruments [31]. The process comprised three phases:
content validity, internal reliability, and treatment of non-ordinal items.

•

•

•

Content validity. In the first phase, three experts in educational innovation and
emerging technologies evaluated the relevance and clarity of the items. Subsequently,
a pilot test (n = 10) was conducted to refine wording and comprehensibility. This
procedure aligns with classical guidelines on content validity and reduces threats of
semantic ambiguity [31].
Internal reliability. Internal consistency was estimated using McDonald’s ω, which
is more robust than Cronbach’s α for short scales or heterogeneous loadings [34].
Although both indices were computed, interpretation prioritized ω due to lower
α values, consistent with exploratory research where content validity and external
triangulation are emphasized over strict internal homogeneity [31,35]. Results are
reported in Section 3.2.1.
Treatment of non-ordinal items. Multiple-choice items (i.e., purposes of use or
ethical concerns) and dichotomous variables were analyzed separately and excluded
from internal consistency estimates, as their non-ordinal nature does not satisfy
the assumptions of Likert-type scales [36]. Accordingly, the ethics dimension was
treated as a non-latent, multidimensional construct encompassing heterogeneous but
related aspects (i.e., trust in generated information, need for training, and curricular
integration) and was therefore analyzed descriptively at the item level rather than
as a composite score in order to preserve construct validity rather than internal
homogeneity.

This procedure ensured content validity, acceptable reliability, and overall psychometric
coherence. By complementing the review with students’ perspectives from a higher
education context in Ecuador, the instrument enabled a nuanced, context-sensitive
examination of reported practices and perceptions related to the academic use of GenAI
tools, including perceived benefits, risks, and institutional challenges.

pedagogical rules and assessment criteria [1,2]. In technical activities such as debugging,
software testing, and networking labs, reported benefits relate mainly to task efficiency and
student confidence, particularly when GenAI supports—rather than replaces—problem
solving [3,37,38]. Comparative evidence suggests no clear learning advantage over
alternative resources (i.e., instructional videos), although GenAI often increases perceived
autonomy and reduces effort, supporting a complementary role [39]. Although GenAI’s
immediacy of feedback is appreciated, interactivity remains limited [17].
Its benefits
are more likely when use is structured and instructor-guided [23], whereas substituting
core cognitive work with GenAI is associated with superficial or even negative learning
outcomes [4,5]. Findings that ChatGPT can match high-performing students on specific
tasks underscore the need to interpret performance gains cautiously and to maintain human
oversight [40,41].

•

RQ02. Patterns of use and frequency

Across the literature, GenAI use is described as frequent and transversal, particularly
for writing under time pressure, resolving technical questions, and organizing academic
tasks [42–44]. In programming and algorithm-focused courses, perceived usefulness tends
to be higher, although it is moderated by prior knowledge and prompt quality formulated
by students [22,45,46]. Beyond technical activities, several studies report extended use in
exam preparation and project development, suggesting that GenAI has become embedded
in students’ academic routines rather than confined to isolated tasks [47–49].

•

RQ03. Autonomy and understanding of complex concepts

Findings related to autonomy and conceptual understanding are mixed and highly
context dependent. In advanced courses, GenAI is commonly used to verify intermediate
results, support self-explanation, and assist self-regulation strategies, with students
reporting greater perceived understanding and benefits from immediate feedback [15].
Consistent with this, several studies suggest that GenAI integration can foster learning
autonomy through increased motivation and technological acceptance, particularly
when generative strategies are used to scaffold complex concepts [50].
In contrast,
introductory contexts frequently show less favorable outcomes: in the absence of explicit
teacher guidance, students report frustration, excessive dependence, and limited gains in
productivity or self-efficacy. Multiple studies also document violations of human–AI
interaction principles that undermine students’ sense of control over their learning
processes [51–53].

•

RQ04. Critical-thinking engagement

Evidence related to critical-thinking engagement highlights a clear distinction between
active and passive uses of GenAI. When learning tasks require students to evaluate outputs,
compare alternatives, or design and refine prompts, improvements in analytical reasoning
and self-regulation are reported [8,10]. Similar benefits are observed in programming
courses when students are required to review, justify, and explain their decisions [54]. By
contrast, passive reliance on GenAI is associated with reduced cognitive engagement and
increased dependency [9,16,18]. While GenAI performs adequately in tasks involving minor
modifications or pattern-based responses, it shows clear limitations in assessments that
demand abstraction, transfer, or real-world application, which are central to higher-order
cognitive skills [55–57]. Evidence from non-technical domains further indicates that
increased participation or acceptance does not necessarily translate into deeper critical
thinking [58].

RQ05. Ethical and institutional concerns

The literature identifies ethical and institutional concerns centered on authorship,
authenticity, equity, and assessment validity. Several studies report cases in which GenAI
completes exams or assignments with little evidence of authentic learning, undermining
academic integrity and, in some instances, leading to self-sabotaging behaviors when
students rely on inaccurate outputs [59–61]. Research on intrinsic plagiarism and AI-text
detection highlights both the scale of these challenges and the limitations of current
detection approaches [62–64]. Proposed responses—ranging from classifiers trained on
pre-ChatGPT corpora to outlier analyses of performance or repository activity—may
flag potential misuse at scale but raise concerns about fairness, transparency, and due
process [11,12,65]. Undeclared use, often driven by fear of sanctions, together with unequal
access to GenAI tools and skills, further exacerbates existing equity gaps [66,67]. Beyond
student practices, faculty and institutions stress concerns about integrity, reliability, and
the need for training and clear policies [68]. Ethical-risk analyses underline copyright,
legal compliance, privacy, and academic integrity as key criteria, shifting attention from
individual misconduct to institutional responsibility for regulating GenAI at the system
level [69].

Taken together, the systematized review showed benefits in perceived academic
efficiency/performance and self-regulation when GenAI was integrated with teacher
guidance, alongside risks related to cognitive dependence, reduced analytical engagement,
and ethical or institutional concerns in more passive uses. However, the predominance
of studies from Europe and North America suggests that these findings should be
interpreted as global reference points rather than as directly transferable to Latin American
technology-oriented settings, reinforcing the relevance of contrasting them with local
survey evidence.

4. Discussion

This study combines a systematized review with locally gathered survey evidence
to examine the role of GenAI in technology-oriented higher education. The findings
reveal efficiency-related benefits, cognitive challenges, and ethical considerations operating

https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15070466

Computers 2026, 15, 466

18 of 31

across pedagogical, institutional, and theoretical levels. Beyond confirming patterns
reported in prior studies, the present work contributes by examining GenAI use in
technology-oriented programs through five analytically integrated dimensions—perceived
academic efficiency/performance, usage patterns, autonomy, critical-thinking engagement,
and ethical/institutional concerns—within an underrepresented Ecuadorian/Latin
American context. It further articulates these findings through an efficiency–depth tension,
highlighting that educational benefits depend on pedagogical mediation and institutional
conditions rather than on tool availability alone.

4.1. Convergences with Global Literature

The results are consistent with international findings in three main areas: (i) gains
in efficiency, reflected in time management and support for writing, programming, and
tasks [1,2,37]; (ii) autonomy, particularly when GenAI is used with verification and teacher
guidance [51,52]; and (iii) perceived engagement and confidence, as students describe the
tool as facilitating participation and reducing uncertainty in academic tasks [3,38].

A similar pattern emerges in item-level ethical concerns: students associate GenAI
with issues of authorship, authenticity, and privacy rather than with direct effects on
perceived academic efficiency/performance, aligning with observations reported in [49,62].
The evidence suggests that GenAI accelerates academic processes and, when used with
teacher mediation, supports self-regulated learning. Although this study is situated in
Ecuador, these trends resemble those documented in other Global South contexts, where
digital divides, limited institutional guidance, and uneven governance conditions shape
adoption patterns [58,66].

While the patterns observed here are broadly consistent with findings from Europe,
North America, and Asia, their interpretation differs due to contextual conditions. In
the Ecuadorian setting, the absence of explicit institutional guidelines, limited formal
training on GenAI, and reliance on freely accessible tools intensify the tension between
efficiency gains and cognitive depth, suggesting that similar levels of GenAI adoption may
produce qualitatively different educational effects depending on regulatory maturity and
instructional support. In this sense, the present study contributes contextual refinement
by showing that the same broad benefits reported internationally may be accompanied by
stronger risks of dependency, weaker instructional mediation, and more fragile institutional
conditions related to guidance, privacy, and responsible use in underrepresented higher
education contexts.

4.2. Disruptive Findings

One notable finding is the tension between perceived academic efficiency/performance
and critical-thinking engagement, reflected in students’ self-reported practices and
item-level responses (i.e., reduced analytical effort), rather than in a negative correlation
between perceived academic efficiency/performance and critical-thinking engagement
scores. While GenAI supports efficiency, without assignments requiring explanation,
comparison, or justification, it may encourage rapid answers with limited conceptual
grounding. These associations should be interpreted as non-causal, exploratory, and
context-bounded, given the cross-sectional, self-reported, and single-institution nature of
the survey evidence.

This contrasts with studies reporting simultaneous improvements in performance
and analytical reasoning [9,17], indicating that the effect is not universal and depends
heavily on pedagogical design. In technology programs where complex problem solving
is essential, this tension becomes particularly relevant and reflects a broader educational
challenge [6,11,12]. Accordingly, the central issue is not whether GenAI improves or

https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15070466

Computers 2026, 15, 466

19 of 31

harms learning in general, but under which instructional conditions efficiency gains remain
compatible with sustained analytical engagement.

4.3. Pedagogical Implications

The findings indicate that the educational effects of GenAI depend less on the
technology itself and more on how it is pedagogically embedded within teaching and
learning activities. Rather than producing uniform outcomes, GenAI shapes learning
processes in relation to assessment design, instructional guidance, and opportunities
for reflection. From this perspective, three interrelated pedagogical priorities emerge.
These priorities provide a context-informed pedagogical translation of recurring concerns
about autonomy, superficiality, integrity, and responsible use into concrete strategies for
technology-oriented higher education.

Process-oriented assessment. Emphasizing iterative projects, partial submissions, and
oral defenses shifts evaluation toward reasoning, traceability, and transparency of student
work, reducing the centrality of final products that are vulnerable to unreflective GenAI
delegation [11,12,40,68].

Critical prompting literacy. Embedding explicit activities focused on formulating,
refining, and critically evaluating prompts—together with verification and citation
practices—supports more reflective interaction with GenAI tools and mitigates risks of
overreliance and superficial engagement [16,18,70].
Metacognition and self-regulated learning.

Integrating reflective checkpoints,
justification criteria, and verification requirements encourages students to use GenAI
as a cognitive support for deep learning rather than as a shortcut for task completion,
reinforcing self-regulated learning processes [38,44].

4.4. Implications for Teacher Training and Assessment Design

The triangulated evidence (review + student survey) underscores the need to translate
pedagogical practices into concrete competencies within teacher training and professional
development. As GenAI is already embedded in students’ academic routines, the challenge
is not its presence but how assessment design and teacher preparation can leverage
efficiency gains (RQ01) without compromising deep understanding and critical-thinking

https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15070466

Computers 2026, 15, 466

20 of 31

engagement (RQ04). This shifts the focus from viewing GenAI as an integrity threat
toward strengthening instructors’ capacity to guide its pedagogical use (RQ02–RQ03) and
address ethical concerns through clear, enforceable norms (RQ05). Accordingly, teacher
training programs should prioritize a focused set of competencies aligned with learning
outcomes in technology-oriented higher education. This contribution is especially relevant
because prior studies often identify teacher guidance as important, but tend to provide
less detail on the concrete instructional competencies needed to operationalize responsible
GenAI integration in computing and engineering education, particularly in contexts where
institutional support remains limited.

Assessment redesign under GenAI availability. Instructors should be trained to design
process-centered assessments that prioritize reasoning and evidence over final answers.
This includes requiring justification and decision logs, evaluating alternative solutions,
incorporating brief oral checks or live demonstrations (i.e., debugging), and using rubrics
that reward explanation quality, verification, and reflective critique of AI-supported outputs.
These competencies address the tension between efficiency gains (RQ01) and reduced
analytical depth (RQ04).

Scaffolding autonomy while preventing dependency. As autonomy is associated
with meaningful GenAI-supported learning (RQ03), teacher training should incorporate
scaffolding and gradual withdrawal strategies, using structured prompts and checklists
initially and reducing support over time to foster independent problem solving and
self-regulated learning (RQ03–RQ04).

Policy literacy and integrity-by-design. Teacher training should also develop
competencies in policy literacy and integrity-by-design, enabling instructors to interpret
institutional guidelines, communicate acceptable GenAI use, and embed ethical principles
into assessment design through authentic tasks and traceability requirements, rather than
relying on detection or blanket prohibitions. This also includes helping students understand
when GenAI outputs require verification, what kinds of academic or personal information
should not be shared with external GenAI systems, and how institutionally approved tools
differ from unrestricted public use (RQ05).

Overall, these implications frame instructors as cognitive mentors who align
GenAI-mediated practices with disciplinary rigor, assessment validity, and learning
objectives in programming, abstraction, and problem solving.

4.5. Institutional and Policy Implications

In contexts where institutional guidance on GenAI remains limited or uneven, such
as the setting examined in this study, institutions need to move beyond general debates
and articulate practical, enforceable guidelines for academic use. This includes clearly
defining acceptable practices—such as declared assistance in drafting or ideation—and
distinguishing them from uses that compromise academic integrity, including the full
delegation of graded tasks to a model.

At the same time, institutional responsibility extends to addressing digital equity:
reliance on free services and the absence of institutional licenses can widen existing
inequalities, particularly in Global South contexts. Ensuring minimum conditions such as
reliable connectivity, vetted tools, and secure access is therefore essential to maintain trust,
consistency in evaluation, and fairness across student populations.

A further institutional priority concerns privacy, cybersecurity, and governance
conditions surrounding GenAI use. At the service level, students may share academic,
personal, or project-related information with external systems whose data-processing
practices, retention conditions, or access controls are not always transparent. At the
platform level, the use of unvetted public tools, shared accounts, or non-standardized access

conditions may create inconsistencies in acceptable use, accountability, and data handling.
Although the present study did not conduct a technical audit of GenAI platforms, both
the reviewed literature and the students’ privacy-related concerns suggest that responsible
adoption requires clearer guidance on what information may be shared, which tools are
institutionally approved, and how data use should be communicated.

A further priority concerns faculty development. Beyond basic familiarity with GenAI
tools, instructors require competencies for designing AI-aware assessments, teaching
verification and justification strategies, and fostering metacognitive routines that translate
institutional policies into classroom practices. Without such support, policy frameworks
risk remaining abstract and disconnected from everyday teaching and assessment.

Taken together, these institutional and policy considerations link the Latin American
context with broader international debates on governance, equity, and academic integrity,
reinforcing the global relevance of the issues addressed in this study [15,63,69]. More
specifically, the study contributes contextual evidence showing that governance gaps are
not a peripheral issue, but a central condition shaping how students experience the balance
between efficiency, autonomy, integrity, and privacy concerns in GenAI-supported learning.

4.6. Theoretical Contributions

At the theoretical level, the findings contribute to refining self-regulated learning (SRL)
and Cognitive Load Theory (CLT). Autonomy emerges as a key associated factor that may
facilitate meaningful learning when paired with verification and explanatory work [13,14].
From a CLT perspective, GenAI can reduce extraneous load and streamline routine
steps; however, when it replaces the germane effort required for schema construction,
critical-thinking engagement may weaken [19]. Together, these insights support an
interpretive framework in which efficiency gains are conditional on task design and
teacher mediation. Thus, the study contributes contextual theoretical refinement by
positioning autonomy not as an automatic benefit of AI-assisted learning, but as a
conditional educational process shaped by verification practices, instructional scaffolding,
and assessment design. More broadly, the findings suggest that educational efficiency
should not be equated with educational value, as faster task completion may coexist with
weaker analytical engagement when reflective and interpretive work is displaced.

The proposed framework is interpretive, integrative, and context-bounded in nature,
combining evidence from the review, survey-based correlations, and qualitative student
perceptions and does not imply causal or predictive relationships among variables. Rather
than testing a structural model, it synthesizes empirical associations with established
theoretical perspectives to illustrate pedagogical tensions and context-specific interpretive
relationships. In this sense, it is also consistent with recent higher education proposals that
frame GenAI integration through graduated levels of autonomy, agency, and risk.

ethical/institutional concerns, while the tension between perceived academic efficiency/
performance gains and critical-thinking engagement emerges as a core pedagogical
challenge.

The framework indicates that the educational value of GenAI depends less on
efficiency gains than on the instructional and institutional conditions that shape their
use. The evidence reveals a persistent tension between perceived academic efficiency/
performance improvements and sustained critical-thinking engagement. Without
pedagogical mediation, efficiency-oriented uses of GenAI risk simplifying tasks at the
expense of the depth of reasoning required in technology-oriented programs.

4.7. Study Limitations and Future Directions

The interpretation of these findings should be considered in light of several limitations.
The survey component relied on a cross-sectional, self-reported, non-probabilistic sample
from a single institution in Ecuador; therefore, the results should be interpreted as
context-specific and exploratory rather than causal or statistically representative.
In
addition, this study focused on students’ perceptions rather than objective learning
outcomes and did not incorporate instructors’ perspectives, which limits the institutional
interpretation of classroom practices.

The review component should also be interpreted as a focused systematized evidence
synthesis based on selected sources rather than as an exhaustive review of all relevant
education databases. The search strategy prioritized Scopus as the main interdisciplinary
source, complemented by ACM Digital Library and IEEE Xplore to capture computing-
and engineering-related studies. SpringerLink was used as an additional publisher
platform to identify potentially relevant studies available through that source. As a
result, studies indexed only in education-specific databases (i.e., ERIC or EBSCO-based
resources) or regional databases may not have been captured. In addition, the restriction
to English-language studies may have underrepresented relevant scholarship published
in other languages, including bodies of literature that may be particularly important in
regional or national research contexts.

From a measurement standpoint, some constructs were assessed with short scales
and ethics-related items were analyzed primarily at the item level due to low internal
consistency. This study also did not include a technical evaluation of GenAI services or
platforms; consequently, privacy-, security- and governance-related issues were interpreted
from the literature and from students’ reported concerns rather than from direct technical
or platform-level evidence.

A further procedural limitation is that, although informed consent was obtained
within the questionnaire prior to participation, no separate written consent forms were
collected and no formal institutional ethics approval or exemption document was obtained
prior to data collection.

These limitations suggest several directions for future research. Longitudinal,
quasi-experimental, and design-based studies are needed to examine how GenAI-aware

5. Conclusions

By triangulating a systematized review with survey evidence from technology-oriented
programs in Ecuador, this study shows that GenAI in higher education is associated with
widespread adoption and perceived academic efficiency/performance gains, but also
with a persistent pedagogical tension between academic acceleration and analytical depth.
Considered jointly, these patterns are not contradictory; rather, they indicate the conditions
under which GenAI supports learning rather than merely speeding up task completion
within the context examined here.

The main contribution of this study is to provide context-bounded evidence showing
that GenAI’s educational value in technology-oriented programs depends less on the tool
itself than on the pedagogical and institutional conditions surrounding its use. Across
both the review component and the local survey evidence, meaningful use is more likely
when students are required to verify outputs, justify decisions, and engage in reflective
reasoning and when institutions provide clearer guidance, teacher preparation, and
equitable conditions of access.

Accordingly, responsible GenAI integration should be framed as a matter of
assessment design, instructional mediation, and governance rather than of mere tool
availability. In this sense, this study highlights that process-oriented assessment, critical
prompting, metacognitive routines, and explicit institutional guidance are central to
critical-thinking engagement, and academic integrity in
preserving autonomy,
technology-oriented higher education in this context.
