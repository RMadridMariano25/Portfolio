1. Introduction

This  study  investigates  the  effects  of  students’  ChatGPT  use  on  their  academic  self-efficacy  in
higher education. With the increasing adoption of generative AI (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT in
learning environments, concerns have emerged about their influence on learners’ academic self-
efficacy. Using a quantitative design, the study collected data through a structured online ques-
tionnaire completed by 296 higher education students. The findings indicate that perceived in-
dependent academic self-efficacy (without ChatGPT) is, in most cases, higher than their perceived
technology-mediated  academic  self-efficacy  (with  ChatGPT).  Nevertheless,  while  ChatGPT  in-
creases students perceived self-efficacy when used, it simultaneously decreases their confidence in
completing  tasks  without  its  assistance.  In  addition,  while  perceived  academic  self-efficacy
without  using  ChatGPT  is  significantly  correlated  with  students’  perceived  creativity  and  crit-
ical thinking, perceived academic self-efficacy with ChatGPT is not. These findings raise concerns
about potential overreliance on GenAI tools and their impact on the relationship between self-
efficacy  and  personal  cognitive  traits.  The  study  concludes  by  emphasizing  the  need  for
balanced  GenAI  integration  in  education  to  foster  independent  learning  skills,  creativity,  and
critical thinking, while preventing dependency and promoting responsible, reflective technology
use.

The rise of generative AI (GenAI) has sparked widespread debate about its influence on many aspects of human life, particularly
learning. In higher education, tools such as ChatGPT may foster personalized learning by adapting content to learners’  needs and
providing real-time interactive feedback (Nguyen et al., 2024; Wang, 2025). As students increasingly integrate ChatGPT into their
academic activities, it is important to understand not only how the usage of such tools relates to academic self-efficacy, but also
whether it alters the relationship between self-efficacy and core cognitive traits such as creativity and critical thinking.

Academic self-efficacy refers to students’ beliefs in their ability to successfully perform academic tasks.
However, in the context of GenAI use, such as ChatGPT, it is important to distinguish between independent academic self-efficacy,
students’  perceived ability to perform tasks independently (“without ChatGPT”), and technology-mediated academic self-efficacy,
their perceived ability to perform those tasks with AI assistance (“with ChatGPT”).

Moreover, creativity and critical thinking are considered the foundations upon which this efficacy is built, as they enable students
to generate original ideas and evaluate information thoughtfully (Orakci, 2023). The concern is that reliance on GenAI may undermine

Despite existing research on the use of GenAI, a research gap remains in comparing students’ perceived self-efficacy when using the

2. Literature review

2.1. GenAI in higher education: opportunities and challenges

Recent studies on integrating GenAI into higher education underscore its potential to enhance learning outcomes significantly
(Abbas et al., 2024; Essel et al., 2022; Labadze et al., 2023). GenAI applications are increasingly becoming part of educational systems,
fostering meaningful interactions between learners and technology (Chan & Hu, 2023; Okonkwo & Ade-Ibijola, 2021). These tech-
nologies are designed to adapt educational materials to suit individual learning preferences and speeds, offering interactive, real-time
feedback and customized assistance (Chan &  Hu, 2023). Commonly referred to as virtual teaching assistants, GenAI tools provide
support  by  helping  with  coursework,  answering  inquiries,  and  clarifying  difficult  concepts  (Ding  et  al.,  2023;  Fryer  et  al.,  2019;
Labadze et al., 2023).

Extensive research has also been conducted on students’ attitudes towards GenAI (Abbas et al., 2024; Bibi & Atta, 2024; Chan & Hu,
2023; Ding et al., 2023). The literature generally suggests that students view these tools positively, appreciating their use and func-
tionality However,  Another significant concern is the risk  of overreliance on GenAI  tools, as Ding et  al. (2023) found that many
students uncritically accept the responses they generate. This finding underscores the importance of developing critical thinking skills
and a deeper understanding of the sources that GenAI tools rely upon for their outputs.

Therefore, many researchers and educators highlight the need to train students in the responsible use of GenAI and in AI literacy,
which encompasses a wide range of abilities, including knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating
with AI, and AI ethics (Ng et al., 2021).

While these studies highlight both the promise and the risks of GenAI in higher education, they also point to the need to examine

how such tools relate to students’ perceptions of their own academic abilities.

2.2. Academic self-efficacy and learning

One framework that is particularly useful for understanding students’ perceptions of capability in this context is academic self-
efficacy. Self-efficacy, a concept developed by psychologist Albert Bandura (1977), is a critical determinant of how people think,
behave, and feel. Self-efficacy influences choices, effort, perseverance, resilience in the face of adversity, and overall performance.
There is a vast amount of literature on the connection between self-efficacy and learning. Many studies across different disciplines and
learning contexts indicate that high self-efficacy leads to greater confidence and a higher likelihood of success and satisfaction from
learning, whereas low self-efficacy often results in doubt and decreased motivation (for example, Aldhahi et al., 2022; Alemayehu &
Chen, 2023; Chen, 2020; Hassankhani et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2013).

It is important to distinguish between general self-efficacy and academic self-efficacy. General self-efficacy refers to a broad, stable
sense of confidence in one’s ability to handle various situations and challenges across various aspects of life. It reflects an individual’s
overall belief in their capacity to achieve goals, overcome obstacles, and succeed in diverse contexts (Bandura, 1977). On the other
hand, academic self-efficacy is a more specific construct pertaining to a student’s belief in their ability to perform and manage aca-
demic tasks successfully. This belief includes their confidence in understanding concepts, completing assignments, studying effec-
tively,  and  successfully  attaining  educational  goals  (Ferla  et  al.,  2009;  Honicke  &  Broadbent,  2016).  Academic  self-efficacy  is  a
powerful predictor of learning performance and motivation to learn (Wu et al., 2020; Yokoyama, 2019).

In the pre-digital era, individuals developed self-efficacy through direct experience, social learning, and gradually overcoming
challenges. However, digital developments pose new challenges and raise questions about the relationship between self-efficacy and
technology-assisted learning. Technological self-efficacy is defined as students’ perception of their ability to use technology-related
tools  and  sites to  engage  in  learning behaviors  to  achieve  an intended  learning outcome  (Pan,  2020). Technological self-efficacy
provides  a  sense  of  mastery  over  technology  and  thereby  positively  affects  the  attitude  toward  technology-based  learning  (Pan,
2020; Puzziferro, 2008; Wang et al., 2013). In GenAI-supported learning environments, however, this construct may take on a more
complex form, as students’  confidence may be shaped not only by their own perceived abilities but also by their interaction with
technological support.

2.3. The impact of GenAI integration on academic self-efficacy

Recent  studies  have  begun  to  explore  whether  and  how  GenAI  tools  influence  students’  academic  self-efficacy.  GenAI  tools
introduce a new layer of complexity in the relationship between technology use and academic self-efficacy. While some researchers
argue that the use of GenAI enhances students’ academic self-efficacy (Mun, 2025; Pellas, 2023; Yilmaz & Yilmaz, 2023), others report
contrasting findings. For example, Daha and Altelwany (2025) found that students with high academic self-efficacy, strong learning
goal orientation, and high performance-approach motivation were less likely to use ChatGPT. Their study also linked ChatGPT use to
increased procrastination and decreased academic performance, and found it did not significantly affect students’ sense of academic
self-efficacy. Similarly, Deng et al. (2025) reported that while ChatGPT use improved academic performance, affective-motivational
states, and higher-order thinking propensities, it also reduced mental effort and had no significant effect on academic self-efficacy.

Although  these  studies  suggest  a  complex  relationship  between  ChatGPT  use  and  academic  self-efficacy,  they  leave  open  the
question of how this relationship connects to students’  broader cognitive characteristics. Several studies point to a more complex
relationship between the use of ChatGPT and academic self-efficacy. Parsakia (2023) posits that GenAI chatbots like ChatGPT can
significantly boost students’ self-efficacy by offering timely, personalized feedback and scaffolding that help learners master tasks and
internalize a sense of academic competence. However, it warns that over-reliance on these tools can actually undermine self-efficacy:
when  students  depend  too  heavily  on  chatbots  for  validation  or  problem-solving,  they  may  weaken  real-world  peer  and  mentor
connections, narrow their creative strategy repertoire, and stunt the development of independent critical thinking, ultimately eroding
their belief in their own abilities rather than reinforcing it. Bouzar et al., (2024) examined the impact of ChatGPT usage on academic
writing self-efficacy and found a positive correlation between self-efficacy and prolonged engagement with ChatGPT. On the other
hand, they observed that the frequency of ChatGPT use predicted reliance on the tool, suggesting that students who regularly use
GenAI may develop a dependence on it. However, their study did not explore the impact of ChatGPT usage on students’ ability to
perform tasks without GenAI assistance.

2.4. The role of self-efficacy, creativity, and critical thinking

To better understand the nature of academic self-efficacy in GenAI-supported contexts, it is also necessary to consider the higher-
order cognitive traits that typically contribute to students’ sense of academic capability. A growing body of research highlights the role
of personal cognitive traits, particularly creativity and critical thinking, in the development of academic self-efficacy (e.g., Basereh &
Pishkar, 2016; Pretz & Nelson, 2017). Bandura (2002) social cognitive theory emphasizes experiences of mastery and personal agency
as key sources of self-efficacy beliefs, suggesting that learners’  confidence in their academic abilities is shaped by their perceived
competence in tackling complex tasks. In this context, creativity and critical thinking function as essential cognitive tools that enable
students to generate original ideas, evaluate information critically, and solve problems effectively (Orakci, 2023). Thus, creativity and
critical thinking can be seen not only  as cognitive competencies but also as foundations  for academic  self-efficacy and  academic
resilience.

George et al. (2024) argue that increased reliance on technology may undermine the development and maintenance of creativity
and critical thinking. As technology takes over more cognitive tasks, individuals engage less frequently in the mental effort required for
these higher-order skills, leading to their gradual decline. Research on ChatGPT’s impact on creativity and critical thinking reveals a
complex and often contradictory landscape: some empirical studies report that ChatGPT can enhance creative ideation and scaffold
higher-order reasoning (de la Puente et al., 2024; Suriano et al., 2025; Urban et al., 2024) while others warn of diminished originality,
reduced cognitive engagement, and no lasting improvements in critical faculties (Sudrajad et al., 2024; Toma et al., 2024).

The literature review revealed an inconclusive understanding of the relationship between academic self-efficacy, creativity, and
critical thinking in the context of ChatGPT use. This study aimed to investigate these complex relationships. To address these gaps and
clarify these relationships, it focuses on the following research questions:

3. Research questions

1.  What  is  the  difference  between  students’  perceived  academic  self-efficacy  in  completing  academic  tasks  with  and  without

ChatGPT?

2. What is the relationship between the extent of ChatGPT usage and students’ perceived academic self-efficacy in completing aca-

demic tasks with and without ChatGPT?

3.  What is the relationship between students’ perceptions of their creativity and critical thinking and their academic self-efficacy, with

and without the use of ChatGPT?

4. Methodology

To address these research questions, the study employs a quantitative research methodology. It employed a structured online
questionnaire distributed via Panel4All, one of Israel’s leading sampling companies, which adheres to ESOMAR standards for internet
panel management. Data collection was conducted from July 28, 2024, to August 4, 2024. To ensure a representative of the sample, the
survey provider implemented a stratified sampling procedure designed to approximate the composition of the Israeli higher-education
student population. Stratification was based on key student characteristics, including gender, field of study, degree level, and year of
study.

The questionnaire included items unrelated to the primary research goals to monitor the respondents’ engagement and alertness.
Participants  who  failed  to  answer  these  items  correctly  were  excluded  from  the  final  analysis.  Additionally,  because  the  online

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP (Version 0.18.3) and SPSS (Version 28).
Several assumptions were examined before conducting the parametric analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk tests were significant for several

severe departures from normality. Given the relatively large sample size, the paired-samples t tests were considered sufficiently robust
for the present analyses. A brief power analysis was also conducted to examine sample adequacy for the primary analyses. With 296
participants and a two-tailed α of 0.05, the study had high power to detect small effects in both the paired-samples comparisons (d =
0.20, power = 0.93) and the correlational analyses (r = 0.20, power = 0.94), indicating that the sample size was adequate for the main
analyses.

4.2. Research tool

Given the study’s focus on comparing perceptions across two conditions the participants were asked to report their perceived self-
efficacy under two clearly defined self-report conditions: completing academic tasks with ChatGPT assistance and completing the same
tasks independently without ChatGPT or similar GenAI tools (see Appendix A). In addition, the questionnaire was designed to capture a
range  of  variables  relevant  to  the  research  objectives,  including  demographic information,  the  modes  of  ChatGPT  use,  perceived
creativity, and critical thinking (Table 3). We utilized a task-specific approach to measure students' perceived academic self-efficacy
(Siefer et al., 2021). This approach offers superior predictive accuracy and practical utility compared to general self-efficacy scales
(Wang  &  Richarde,  1988). Bandura  (1977) emphasized  that  self-efficacy  beliefs  are  most  meaningful  when  aligned  with  specific
activities, as this close correspondence enhances the ability to forecast actual performance. Empirical studies in education, such as
Pajares (1997) and Street et al. (2017), have consistently shown that students’ confidence in executing precise tasks, such as solving
linear equations, correlates more strongly with real performance than broader, undifferentiated measures.

To  ensure  that  participants  clearly distinguished  between  tasks  performed  with  and  without  the  GenAI  tool,  we designed  the
questionnaire using a within-subjects design across two separate blocks. Furthermore, we used a parallel-item structure across both
conditions to ensure measurement consistency and reliability (Kim 2012). This within-subject approach allowed each participant to
serve as their own baseline, effectively controlling for subject variability and individual differences. Consequently, this design sub-
stantially reduced the error variance and increased the statistical power of the comparison (Charness et al., 2012).

In the first section, participants were explicitly instructed to rate their perceived self-efficacy in completing specific academic tasks
“with the assistance of ChatGPT.” Immediately following this, a second section presented the same set of tasks, but with a categorical
instruction to rate their capability to perform them “independently, without the use of ChatGPT or similar GenAI tools.” This sequential
separation, using contrasting bold instructions, was designed to facilitate a clear distinction between technology-mediated efficacy and
independent mastery, thereby reducing potential measurement bias.

The  items  assessing  the  types  of  academic  tasks  (e.g.,  writing  summaries,  writing  code,  and  translating  texts)  and  students’
associated perceived self-efficacy  in each were based  on Amzalag and  Kurtz (2025).  The statements examining perceived critical
thinking were based on Sarigoz (2012), while the statements assessing perceived creativity were based on Roy (2013). All statements
were adapted for the current study to suit the context of GenAI-integrated learning. The current study demonstrated high internal

As shown in Table 5, the extent of ChatGPT use was positively correlated with perceived academic self-efficacy with ChatGPT, both

To  answer  the  third  research  question,  a  Pearson  correlation  was  calculated  between  perceived  academic  self-efficacy  with

The  findings  show  that  while  perceived  academic  self-efficacy  without  ChatGPT  positively  correlated  with  perceived  critical

cases.

6. Discussion

The present study explored the relationship between ChatGPT usage and perceived academic self-efficacy among higher education
students. The findings provide valuable insights into how GenAI tools influence students’ perceptions of their academic self-efficacy,
both with and without ChatGPT assistance, and into the relationships between perceived academic self-efficacy, perceived creativity,
and perceived critical thinking in the GenAI era.

The study revealed that students use ChatGPT for various academic purposes, including searching for information and writing code.
However, at the time of the study, their overall use of ChatGPT was only moderate. Future studies should continue monitoring this
phenomenon.

Furthermore, this trend can be understood through students’ engagement and agency in shaping academic self-efficacy. According
to Bandura (1997), academic self-efficacy develops primarily through mastery experiences, i.e., when students complete tasks inde-
pendently and attribute success to their own efforts and abilities. In traditional learning situations, students who engage directly with
the material, apply critical thinking, and solve problems independently are more likely to feel capable and in control. In contrast, when
students use ChatGPT, they may feel that the tool is doing a substantial part of the cognitive work (George et al., 2024). This perception
can reduce their perceived ownership of the outcome, making them feel less competent, even if the outcome is good. One possible
interpretation is that, for some students, self efficacy in task performance may become more closely tied to the availability of the tool
than to their own perceived independent capability.

This distinction becomes even more meaningful when considered alongside the correlations between ChatGPT use and perceived
self-efficacy in the two conditions. The findings show that greater ChatGPT use was associated with lower perceived independent
academic self-efficacy in some tasks, particularly writing and text formulation. (Bouzar et al., 2024; Parsakia, 2023). However, these
findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the negative correlation between the extent of ChatGPT use and perceived independent
academic self-efficacy was relatively weak, and task-specific differences yielded small to medium effect sizes. This finding suggests that
while ChatGPT reduces independent confidence to some extent, a balanced pedagogical approach is needed to maintain students’
self-reliance.

Additionally, the study highlights a shift in educational dynamics driven by ChatGPT, similar to that caused by other digital-age
technologies  (George  et  al.,  2024).  While  perceived  creativity  and  critical  thinking  are  traditionally  predictors  of  academic
self-efficacy (Orakci, 2023), they were not significantly related to technology-mediated academic self-efficacy. These findings may
suggest that, in GenAI-supported contexts, perceived academic self-efficacy is less closely linked to students’ reported creativity and
critical thinking. One possible interpretation is that the perceived basis of efficacy differs when academic work is carried out with tool
assistance rather than independently.

Furthermore,  this  pattern  suggests  that  GenAI  tools  such  as  ChatGPT  may  alter  the  mechanisms  underlying  the  formation  of
perceived academic self-efficacy. Traditionally, academic self-efficacy develops through experiences of mastery, personal effort, and
the application of higher-order thinking skills such as creativity and critical thinking (Bandura, 1977). However, when students use
GenAI  tools,  this  association  is  disrupted.  The  absence  of  a  correlation  in  GenAI-use  contexts  implies  that  perceived  academic
self-efficacy may no longer be as tightly linked to one’s perceived internal cognitive abilities. At the same time, because the present
findings are based on cross-sectional associations, this possibility should be examined more directly in future studies using designs that
allow stronger causal inference, such as longitudinal or experimental research.

This shift raises important questions about the nature and quality of perceived academic self-efficacy in GenAI-supported learning
environments. If students’  confidence is shaped by what GenAI enables them to produce, this may represent a form of perceived
academic self-efficacy that is more context-dependent and potentially less transferable to situations in which such tools are unavai-
lable. These findings call for a re-examination of existing models of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997) in light of emerging technologies and
raise questions about how to design GenAI-integrated learning environments that continue to support learners’ sense of agency and
cognitive growth.

6.1. Study strengths and theoretical contributions

This study makes a theoretical and methodological contribution to the literature on GenAI-mediated academic self-efficacy. A key
strength of this research lies in its comparative evaluative design, which examines students’ perceived academic self-efficacy with and
without the use of ChatGPT. Grounded in Bandura’s (1997) social cognitive theory, this study uses task-specific self-efficacy measures
to provide a focused understanding of how GenAI integration affects distinct academic skills. Its examination of perceived creativity
and perceived critical thinking provides added value by illuminating the interplay between higher-order perceptual traits and reliance
on generative GenAI tools.

6.2. Limitations and future research

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the findings are based solely on self-reported data and a cross-
sectional  design,  which  implies  that  the  results  illustrate  correlational  rather  than  causal  relationships.  Additionally,  the  self-
reports  pertain  to  perceived  academic  self-efficacy  rather  than  actual  academic  performance;  future  research  should  incorporate
objective measures of academic achievement. Second, the research was conducted within a single national context (Israel), potentially
limiting the generalizability of the findings. Third, this study did not investigate specific modes of ChatGPT use (e.g., types of use,
goals, or interaction models), nor did it assess differences in students’ baseline AI literacy. As these dimensions may influence out-
comes, future research should investigate them more closely to develop a more nuanced understanding of GenAI’s role in shaping
learning experiences. Finally, as noted in the discussion, several observed relationships exhibited weak-to-moderate effect sizes; these
warrant careful evaluation. To further expand on these findings, future studies should employ longitudinal designs to monitor changes
in students’ reliance on GenAI and their perceived academic self-efficacy over time. Finally, qualitative approaches, such as in-depth
interviews or focus groups, are recommended to provide deeper insights into the underlying reasons for the observed gap between

7. Conclusion

This study contributes to a deeper understanding of how GenAI tools, such as ChatGPT, are reshaping students’ perceived academic
self-efficacy and the psychological foundations that support it. The findings suggest a need to revisit traditional models of self-efficacy,
such as Bandura’s (1997), in light of emerging technologies that mediate learners’ engagement with academic tasks.

A key contribution of this study lies in suggesting the emergence of a form of technology-medicated academic self-efficacy, which
may be less grounded in internal mastery and more dependent on external technological scaffolding. This finding raises important
concerns about the sustainability and transferability of students’ confidence in their abilities, especially in contexts where GenAI is
unavailable or discouraged. Moreover, the research highlights the potential erosion of perceived cognitive agency raises questions
about perceived cognitive agency when GenAI tools are overused or integrated uncritically into academic routines. The weakened link
between perceived higher-order thinking skills and self-efficacy in GenAI-supported contexts underscores the importance of designing
pedagogical approaches that maintain students’ ownership of the learning process.

In light of its empirical findings, particularly the negative correlation between extensive ChatGPT use and independent academic
self-efficacy, this study calls on educators to increase students’ awareness of their use of GenAI tools for learning purposes and to design
academic  tasks  that  leverage  GenAI  without  displacing  students’  cognitive  engagement.  Assignments  should  be  structured  to
encourage creativity, critical thinking, and other higher-order thinking skills, ensuring that GenAI becomes a tool for empowerment
rather than a replacement for it. A balanced pedagogical approach is essential to preserve learners’ autonomy and promote meaningful
academic development in GenAI-enhanced learning environments.
