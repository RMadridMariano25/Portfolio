Objective: The current study assessed pharmacy students’ perceptions about generative AI before
and after participation in a ChatGPT-based drug information activity.
Methods: In 2024, students at three colleges of pharmacy completed a baseline and post-activity
survey on their perceptions of ChatGPT including its reliability, usefulness, and impact on aca-
demic  performance  and  critical  thinking.  The  survey  was  modified  from  the  TAME-ChatGPT
assessment  and used a 5-point Likert  scale. After  the baseline survey,  students  answered clini-
cally relevant drug information questions on their own using primary or tertiary resources and
compared  their  answers  with  ChatGPT  responses.  Independent  t-test  samples  were  used  to
compare baseline and post-activity surveys.
Results: A total of 227 students completed the pre-survey and 203 students completed the post-
survey. Students’ concerns about the reliability of ChatGPT increased after completing the drug
information activity (pre-survey: 3.57  ± 0.96; post-survey: 3.88  ± 1.11; p = 0.002). Students’
concerns  about reliance  on ChatGPT and  prevention of  critical  thinking  increased  (pre-survey:
3.30 ± 1.34; post-survey: 3.57 ± 1.21; p = 0.031). The following areas decreased after the ac-
tivity: enthusiasm about ChatGPT as learning and research tool (pre-survey: 3.60 ± 1.02; post-
survey:  3.32  ± 1.18;  p  = 0.008),  viewing  ChatGPT  as  an  important  tool  for  academic  success
(pre-survey:  3.40  ± 1.13;  post-survey:  3.12  ± 1.23;  p  = 0.015),  and  concern  regarding  being
accused of plagiarism when using ChatGPT(pre-survey: 4.12 ± 0.96; post-survey: 3.91 ± 1.10; p
= 0.031). Open-ended responses revealed that students largely perceived ChatGPT as unreliable
for  drug  information,  citing  concerns  about  accuracy  and  outdated  content.  However,  some
students noted its potential usefulness for non-clinical tasks such as generating ideas, organizing
content, or providing general overviews.
Conclusion:  After  a  hands-on  ChatGPT-based  drug  information  activity,  pharmacy  students  re-
ported  increased  concerns  about  reliability  and  over-reliance  on  artificial  intelligence-based
technology.  The  results  of  this  study  may  encourage  pharmacy  educators  to  implement  class-
room activities for active exploration of the benefits and challenges of generative AI.
Contribution  to  literature:  Limited  published  data  describes  pharmacy  student  perceptions  of
artificial intelligence platforms as a drug information source. There is even less literature with
pre-  and  post-data  after  implementing  an  activity  in  which  students  gain  hands-on  experience
critiquing  an  artificial  intelligence  platform  response.  Therefore,  this  study  was  conducted  to

Introduction

Chat  Generative  Pretrained  Transformer  (ChatGPT)  is  an  artificial  intelligence  (AI)-powered  generative  language  model  that
publicly  launched in  November  2022.1 ChatGPT  generates  responses using a  combination of  deep  learning and  natural language
processing techniques. Generative AI applications, such as ChatGPT, can meaningfully enhance pharmacy education by providing
interactive,  personalized,  and  easily  accessible  learning  support.  It  can  assist  with  drug  information  retrieval  by  providing  quick
summaries of drug indications, contraindications, mechanisms of action, and side effects.2

There  is  growing  support  for  teaching  pharmacy  students  how  to  become  literate  about  generative  AI  and  ready  to  use  it
responsibly.3,4 Pharmacy students have reported a positive perception toward the inclusion of AI-related content in the curriculum.5
Furthermore, the American Association of Colleges of Pharmacy 2022 Curriculum Outcomes and Entrustable Professional Activities
and the Accreditation Council for Pharmacy Education Standards 2025 both highlight the need for pharmacy students to provide
“whole  person  care”  and  have  a  general  understanding  of  digital  health  literacy.6,7 Graduates  must  have  the  necessary  skills  to
effectively apply AI technologies in practice, with a clear understanding of its capabilities and limitations. Pharmacy educators must
face the ever-changing landscape of technology and prepare students to combine the seemingly disparate concepts of digital health and
patient-centered care so that optimal and efficient treatment strategies are delivered.

There is limited published information regarding the perceptions of United States-based pharmacy students in utilizing generative
AI tools such as ChatGPT for drug information queries. Data on the effect of AI-driven activities on pharmacy student knowledge,
misconceptions,  and  uncertainties  would  offer  essential  insights  in  how  to  design  and  incorporate  these  activities  in  pharmacy
curricula. Due to this, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the perceptions of pharmacy students about their professional and
personal use of ChatGPT before and after participation in a ChatGPT-based drug information activity.

Objective

The study aimed to evaluate the perceptions of pharmacy students about their use of ChatGPT before and after participation in a

ChatGPT-based drug information activity in a required didactic course.

Methods

Study design

This prospective cohort study was conducted across three institutions. Two of the institutions are a four-year program while one of
them is a three-year program. At each respective institution, second-year pharmacy student participants were requested to complete a
pre-survey, followed by a required ChatGPT-based drug information activity, then requested to complete a post-survey. The authors
developed the electronic survey using the previously validated TAME-ChatGPT questionnaire, modified slightly for the needs of the
activity. 8 The survey included 5-point Likert scale questions (5 = strongly agree, 4 = agree, 3 = neutral/no opinion, 2 = disagree, 1 =
strongly disagree) and a few free-response questions (see Supplementary Appendix). The survey was pilot tested internally by four
pharmacy faculty members. Qualtrics™  Survey Software (Qualtrics International) was utilized to build and distribute the survey.
Internal skip logic was used to direct respondents to different questions based on their responses. Additionally, security features in
Qualtrics™ were utilized to allow for anonymous data gathering. The study was deemed exempt by the UIC Institutional Review Board

The ChatGPT-based drug information activity asked students to answer a series of clinical questions. Representative questions that
were utilized are listed in Table 1. The students researched the answers to the drug information questions using primary and tertiary
literature; if specific journal articles were noted to use, the reference was given to students. The students then prompted ChatGPT (3.5)
to answer the same drug information questions; they were instructed to copy and paste the drug information question directly into
ChatGPT without altering the verbiage. The students were expected to then compare their answers to the information that ChatGPT
produced. The class session in which the activity occurred reflected the curricular format of each institution. Class sessions at one
institution were 2 h in length and led by a faculty investigator (two sessions, each with half of the students enrolled in the class and led
by  one  faculty  member)  in  a  workshop  format.  At  that  institution,  students  completed  the  activity  individually,  then  the  faculty
member led a discussion in which students shared their observations. Faculty investigators supplemented the comments shared by
students to ensure that the desired teaching points were covered.

Class session at another institution was 45 min in length and led by a faulty investigator in a workshop format. At that institution,
students completed the activity individually, then the faculty member led a discussion in which students shared their observations. The
faculty investigator supplemented the comments shared by students to ensure that the desired teaching points were covered. The class
session at the last institution was led by a faculty investigator in a similar workshop. The faculty provided instructions for the activity,
and then the students completed the activity individually. A classroom debrief was held to discuss students’ findings with artificial
intelligence.

Participant recruitment

Respondents were eligible to participate if they were students enrolled in one of the following Doctor of Pharmacy courses: PHAR
440 at University of Illinois Chicago (UIC) Retzky College of Pharmacy, PHAR 640 at Larkin University, and P239 at Temple University
School of Pharmacy. Prior to the start of the ChatGPT-based drug information activity, a verbal announcement detailing the pre-survey
consent language was made by the faculty members. Subsequently, a link to the pre-survey was provided to all students who were in
attendance at the class session. The survey invitation link described the purpose of the study and noted that by completing the survey,
the  responder  agrees  to  participate  in  the  study.  Participation  was  voluntary,  and  informed  consent  was  implied  through  survey
completion. Compensation was not provided to study participants. Upon completion of the ChatGPT-based drug information activity,
another verbal announcement was made and access to the post-survey was provided to the students. Pre- and post-survey responses
were not linked. The survey was open from February 2024 to October 2024.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.2; R Core Team 2024). Descriptive statistics included means and standard
deviations.  The  independent  sample  t-test  was  used  to  compare  the  means  between  pre-  and  post-survey  responses  (alpha  0.05).
Missing values were removed, and analysis included on complete survey response cases. Post-survey open-ended response analysis
followed a thematic analysis approach. First, all authors independently read and re-read the data to gain familiarity with the content.
Second, theoretical coding was applied to segments of text relevant to the research question, focusing on meaning-laden excerpts
rather than every individual word. Third, preliminary themes were generated by clustering related codes and identifying patterns
across responses. Fourth, these themes were reviewed and refined collaboratively, with subthemes developed where appropriate and
assessed for clarity, overlap, and support from the dataset. Finally, the findings were synthesized and written up with supporting
quotes. If a single response contained multiple distinct themes, the authors separated it into individual thematic units, which is re-
flected in the total “N” response count for that open-ended question.

Coding was performed in Microsoft Excel, with subsequent reviews and discussions conducted by all four authors during regular
team meetings. Discrepancies were resolved through group consensus, ensuring that the thematic structure accurately represented the
data and aligned with the study’s aims.

Results

A total of 227 students completed the pre-survey, while 203 students completed the post-survey. The analysis focused on changes in
students’ perceptions of ChatGPT, particularly regarding its reliability, usefulness, and impact on academic performance and critical
thinking. The survey response rates across the three study sites varied between the pre- and post-intervention periods. At UIC, the pre-
intervention response rate was 63.1 % (101 out of 160 participants), while the post-intervention response rate declined to 51.3 % (82
out of 160). Temple University demonstrated the highest response rates, with a pre-intervention rate of 93.4 % (100 out of 107) and a
slightly reduced post-intervention rate of 89.7 % (96 out of 107). At Larkin University, the pre-intervention response rate was 74.3 %
(26 out of 35), decreasing marginally to 71.4 % (25 out of 35) in the post-intervention period.

Responses to the first 13 survey questions were of greatest interest since all students answered these questions in the pre-survey
(regardless of prior generative AI use); Table 2 summarizes the data for the first 13 survey questions. In the overall study popula-
tion, students’ concerns about the reliability of ChatGPT increased after completing the drug information activity (pre-survey: 3.57 ±
0.96; post-survey: 3.88 ± 1.11;  p = 0.002). Similarly, students’  concern about being accused  of  plagiarism when using  ChatGPT
decreased  post-activity  (pre-survey:  4.12  ± 0.96;  post-survey:  3.91  ± 1.10;  p  = 0.031)  but  was  still  among  the  most  substantial
concerns. Additionally, concerns about relying too much on ChatGPT and not developing critical thinking skills increased following
the activity (pre-survey: 3.30 ± 1.34; post-survey: 3.57 ± 1.21; p = 0.031). Students’ enthusiasm for using ChatGPT in learning and
research  (pre-survey:  3.60  ± 1.02;  post-survey:  3.32  ± 1.18;  p  = 0.008),  and  their  perceptions  of  ChatGPT  as  an  academic  tool
decreased (pre-survey: 3.40 ± 1.13; post-survey: 3.12 ± 1.23; p = 0.015). Post-survey, the statements with the lowest agreement were
related to trusting the opinions of others about ChatGPT and ChatGPT being an important tool for academic success, but the mean
responses on the Likert scale reflected the position of neutral/no opinion. Statements with the highest agreement also had post-survey
means that reflected neutral/no opinion on the Likert scale.

Among participants who had used ChatGPT before the activity (n = 163), participants’ views on ChatGPT’s convenience and time-
saving capabilities showed a decline following the activity. Students were less likely to consider ChatGPT as a reliable information
source post-activity (pre-survey: 3.10 ± 1.06; post-survey: 2.58 ± 1.19; p < 0.001). The belief that ChatGPT aids in understanding
complex topics also decreased (pre-survey: 3.88 ± 0.99; post-survey: 3.34 ± 1.11; p < 0.001). Fewer students stated that they would
recommend ChatGPT to colleagues post-activity (pre-survey: 3.43 ± 1.02; post-survey: 2.89 ± 1.21; p < 0.001), and students became
less likely to view it as more useful compared to other sources (pre-survey: 3.31 ± 1.13; post-survey: 2.82 ± 1.23; p < 0.001). Students
generally acknowledged the importance of hands-on learning without relying on technology like ChatGPT (pre-survey: 4.01 ± 0.89;
post-survey: 3.77 ± 1.06; p = 0.020), with a slight decline in appreciation for ChatGPT’s efficiency in completing assignments (pre-
survey: 3.52 ± 1.08; post-survey: 3.02 ± 1.23; p < 0.001). The belief that ChatGPT disrupts critical thinking did not change (pre-
survey: 3.47 ± 1.16; post-survey: 3.46 ± 1.18; p = 0.903). However, the perception that ChatGPT is easy to use slightly decreased (pre-
survey: 4.28 ± 0.82; post-survey: 4.11 ± 0.90; p = 0.047).

Among the three institutions, response trends to the pre- and post- surveys varied. Students from UIC (pre-survey, n = 101; post-
survey, n = 82) exhibited increased skepticism regarding ChatGPT’s reliability (pre-survey: 3.60 ± 0.92; post-survey: 3.90 ± 1.05; p =
0.045). Students from Temple University (pre-survey, n = 100; post survey, n = 96) also expressed increased skepticism regarding
ChatGPT’s reliability (pre-survey: 3.50 ± 0.97; post-survey: 3.96 ± 1.11; p = 0.002) and the increased security concerns (pre-survey:
3.27 ± 1.05; post-survey: 3.625 ± 1.14; p = 0.025). Additionally, Temple University students’ perception of ChatGPT’s usefulness as a
tool for academic success and enthusiasm for ChatGPT for learning and research purpose declined after the activity (p = 0.011 and p =
0.007, respectively). Larkin University students (pre-survey n = 26; post-survey n = 25) demonstrated the least change in perceptions,
with most survey responses not changing between the pre- and post-survey.

Discussion

Our  study  findings  suggest  that  pharmacy  students  benefited  from  instructional  and  hands-on  exposure  to  generative  AI.  The
ChatGPT-based drug information activity led to increased skepticism regarding ChatGPT’s reliability and reduced students’ inclination
to  rely  on  it  for  academic  tasks.  While  enthusiasm  for  technology  remained  relatively  stable,  students  became  more  critical  of
ChatGPT’s accuracy and academic benefits after the activity. Institutional differences and results of the correlation analysis suggest
that pre-existing attitudes toward ChatGPT may have influenced how students perceived its utility post-activity. At the time that the
study was conducted we were not aware that AI was addressed in any other courses at the participating institutions, but this possibility
cannot be excluded.

Few prior studies have demonstrated the effect of didactic instruction on AI readiness among health professional students.9 One
author has defined AI readiness as preparedness in knowledge, skills, and attitudes to utilize AI in a healthcare setting along with
professional knowledge.10 Without focused educational efforts, surveys have reported low to moderate AI readiness levels among
health  professional  students.9,11–17 Most  of  these  surveys  suggest  that  a  large  proportion  of  health  professional  students  had  not
received instruction or experience with generative AI in their professional training. Although this is a rapidly changing field, these data
imply that there is much room for improvement. One study in nursing students reported an increase in AI readiness after a 28-h course
about AI compared to no instruction, which is much less feasible to replicate at other colleges than individual class sessions.9 In a
comprehensive review of current efforts to integrate AI into medical education, authors found that while interest in AI education is
growing,  formal  curriculum  frameworks  are  scarce,  with  only  two  papers  detailing  structured  frameworks  (one  general  and  one
focused  on  ophthalmology).23 Most  existing  AI  educational  programs  are  elective,  lack  theoretical  underpinnings  or  pedagogical
guidance, and are concentrated in undergraduate medical education or radiology. The authors emphasize the need for interdisci-
plinary collaboration to create structured AI curricula tailored to different stages and specialties of health professional training.

The  ubiquitous  reality  of  AI  in  the  field  of  pharmacy,  combined  with  a  professional  responsibility  to  help  students  become
responsible  and  effective  users  of  this  technology,  warrants  inclusion  of  instruction  on  generative  AI  in  pharmacy  school
curricula.4,14,18,19 Several authors have noted that pharmacy students desire didactic instruction in AI.5,20,21 Our findings may help
other pharmacy educators to develop activities that provide students with basic knowledge about the potential and limitations of AI,
but also situational experiences that show the potential and limitations of AI. Rather than fostering dependence on AI tools, effective
education will equip students to know when to use, and when not to use, technology in safe and effective ways.4,19 Guided and curated
experience will help students re-evaluate their reliance on generative AI in not just education, but also in practice. As artificial in-
telligence continues to evolve and become more integrated into routine digital environments, reliance on such tools may increase,

potentially shaping future norms of living and working. To note, OpenAI has reported significant improvements in both the perfor-
mance and affordability of its models.22 These developments reflect a broader trend toward the widespread deployment of AI across
applications  and  platforms.  While  this  trajectory  enhances  accessibility  and  functionality,  it  also  underscores  the  importance  of
maintaining critical awareness of AI’s capabilities and constraints as these tools become more embedded in daily workflows. For future
iterations of the activity, expanding the activity to include real-world scenarios such as patient counseling, documentation, or drug
interaction assessments may allow students to explore the practical implications of relying on AI in professional settings. Additionally,
integrating  interdisciplinary  perspectives  (e.g.,  ethics,  law,  and  informatics)  into  the  debrief  discussion  could  deepen  students’
appreciation of AI’s role, not just as a tool for efficiency, but also as a resource that must be used with critical oversight and professional
judgment.

Our study has several limitations. Interventions were not fully standardized among the participating schools of pharmacy, which
could have confounded the results. The possibility that students used generative AI applications to prepare their own answers to the
drug information questions cannot be fully excluded. The participating schools also have different curricula which could have included
other instruction on generative AI that was outside the investigator’s knowledge. Student participation was voluntary, which may have
introduced response bias, particularly if the participants were more engaged or positively inclined toward AI than students who did not
participate.  Not  every  student  responded  to  both  the  pre-  and  post-survey,  and  individual  student  responses  were  not  linked,  so
changes in perception experienced by individual students could not be assessed. Another limitation was that open-ended response
analysis in the post-survey was done via informal assessment. Exposure to generative AI outside the classroom may have affected
student perceptions and survey responses. Chat-GPT is now providing a different model for free users than what the authors tested in
the classroom; despite this the findings from the study are still relevant.

Conclusion

Increasing AI literacy and readiness in pharmacy students is a contemporary necessity. Pharmacy faculty can implement curricular
opportunities for students to explore benefits and challenges with generative AI so that students can learn how to use this technology
judiciously. Our experience with an educational activity designed to provide both basic and experiential knowledge about generative
AI to pharmacy students at three schools of pharmacy demonstrates the value hands-on learning about this topic. Pharmacy student
perceptions  of  generative  AI  changed  after  a  ChatGPT-based  drug  information  activity.  This  activity  involved  intentional  critical
evaluation, demonstrating that there was a potential for students to learn in the classroom even if they had previously used generative
AI.  Pharmacy  educators  must  continue  to  explore  effective  instructional  methods  to  provide  students  with  exposure  to  emerging
technologies that will affect pharmacy practice in the future.
