Artificial intelligence (AI) tools, such as ChatGPT, are becoming increasingly embedded in higher education for
assisting  students  in  tasks  ranging  from  information  retrieval  to  essay  writing  and  problem  solving  in  China.
Although such tools provide powerful cognitive support, the growing dependence on them raises concerns on the
types of cognitive tasks students choose to offload to AI tools and the conditions under which such offloading
occurs. Drawing on a value-based decision-making framework, this work investigated the factors influencing AI-
based cognitive offloading by university students. Two sequential studies were conducted. Study 1 developed and
validated a scale that distinguishes between AI-based lower- and higher-order cognitive offloading. Three-sample
analyses indicated that the scale exhibited robust psychometric properties. Study 2 tested the relationship be-
tween  the  perceived  value  of  AI,  AI-usage  capability,  AI  evaluation  capability,  and  both  types  of  AI-based
cognitive offloading by analyzing the behavior of 667 university students. All data were collected in mainland
China. Results indicate that the perceived value of AI is positively associated with both types of cognitive off-
loading through AI-usage capability. This indirect association was conditioned by the AI evaluation capability: it
became nonsignificant for lower-order offloading under a high evaluation capability but remained significant for
higher-order  offloading.  These  findings  highlight  the  conditional  nature  of  AI-based  cognitive  offloading  in
higher education. Moreover, they extend the cognitive offloading theory from tool-based to human–AI collab-
orative contexts, offering theoretical and practical insights for fostering reflective and value-driven human–AI
interaction in learning environments.

The rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) has profoundly
reshaped  higher  education.  Recent  surveys  have  indicated  that  more
than three-quarters of university students worldwide use AI tools such as
ChatGPT, Grammarly, or Copilot to support academic work (Al Zaidy,
2024; Lund et al., 2025). In China, policy initiatives such as Education
Modernization 2035 have further accelerated the integration of AI into
teaching, learning, and research (CPC Central Committee, State Council,
2019). Consequently, AI has become an integral component of students'
academic practices across disciplines. A growing body of evidence sug-
gests that AI may enhance learning performance by providing person-
alized  feedback,  supporting  self-regulated  learning,  and  fostering
motivation (Khan et al., 2025). However, whether AI contributes to the
development  of  students’  higher-order  cognitive  abilities  remains
debatable.  While  some  studies  indicate  that  AI  can  promote  critical
thinking and creativity when used as a scaffold (Benvenuti et al., 2023),
others warn that overreliance on AI may reduce cognitive engagement
and hinder the acquisition of domain knowledge (Pikhart & Al-Obaydi,
2025).  Neurocognitive  evidence  also  suggests  that  excessive  AI

assistance  may  weaken  neural  activation  during  learning  tasks
(Kosmyna et al., 2025a). These findings highlight the need to examine
the ways by which students use AI for cognitive offloading.

Against this backdrop, the Chinese higher-education context serves
primarily as the empirical setting, in which the present scale and model
were developed and tested, rather than as a defining component of the
construct. This setting is nevertheless meaningful because students’ AI
use in China is likely to be shaped, at least in part, by local policy ini-
tiatives  and  research-oriented  academic  practices  (CPC  Central  Com-
mittee, State Council, 2019). Accordingly, this study does not treat its
findings  as  universally  generalizable  across  all  higher-education  sys-
tems. Instead, it offers a context-specific scale development and initial
model test conducted in China, serving as a basis for future validation in
other national and institutional settings.

primary  objective  was  to  develop  and  initially  validate  the  AI-based
cognitive  offloading  (AIBCO)  scale  for  research-related  learning  and
academic activities in higher education. Grounded in Bloom's Revised

1. Literature review

1.1. AI-based cognitive offloading

Cognitive offloading is commonly defined as the use of external ac-
tions or tools (e.g., notes, diagrams, and digital devices) to reduce the
internal  cognitive  demands  of  encoding,  storing,  transforming,  and
retrieving information, or controlling behavior (Risko & Gilbert, 2016).
From this perspective, cognitive offloading is a meta-cognitively regu-
lated  cost–benefit  strategy,  whereby  individuals  shift  parts  of  their
cognitive  work  to  external  tools  to  save  effort,  time,  or  limited
working-memory resources (Prachi Pandey et al., 2020). A more recent
work has further emphasized that offloading decisions are value-based,
highlighting the intentional and (boundedly) rational nature of deciding
which cognitive skills to offload and which to retain internally (Gilbert,
2024),  rather  than  viewing  offloading  as  mere  passive  reliance  on
external resources. AI systems can likewise be understood as powerful
cognitive offloading tools (Grinschgl & Neubauer, 2022), because they
can support not only information retrieval and storage, but also expla-
nation,  organization,  evaluation,  and  content  generation  (Gerlich,
2025).  However,  although  prior  research  has  shown  that  AI  use  can
shape cognition and reduce cognitive load (Messeri & Crockett, 2024), it
has  not  clearly  conceptualized  AIBCO  as  a  distinct  construct.  Herein,
AIBCO refers specifically to the extent to which learners use AI during
task execution to take over cognitive tasks that they could, in principle,
perform themselves, thereby reducing their own cognitive effort.

Although AIBCO is related to other forms of AI-supported task per-
formance,  it  should  not  be  treated  as  interchangeable  with  them.  In
particular, AIBCO differs from delegation, AI-assisted scaffolding, and
AI-mediated collaboration. Delegation emphasizes the transfer of task
execution, judgment, or functional responsibility to AI systems, whereas
the offloading construct focuses specifically on reducing learners' own
cognitive effort during task execution (Fügener et al., 2022). AI-assisted
scaffolding  refers  to  the  types  of  support  intended  to  guide  learners'
thinking and foster capability development, rather than simply taking
over cognitive work on their behalf (Dhillon et al., 2024; Pea, 2004).
AI-mediated  collaboration,  in  turn,  emphasizes  sustained  interdepen-
dence, shared goals, and dynamic coordination between humans and AI
(McGrath  et  al.,  2025).  These  forms  of  human–AI  interaction  may
overlap with cognitive offloading in practice, but they are analytically
distinct. These distinctions help clarify the specific focus of this study on
learners’  use  of  AI  to  reduce  their  own  cognitive  effort  during  task
execution. Therefore, to fully understand this construct, examining the
extent  to  which  learners  offload  cognitive  processes  associated  with
different levels of cognitive demand to AI during learning is necessary.
To differentiate the aforementioned levels of AIBCO, this study uti-
lized  BRT  as  an  analytical  framework.  According  to  BRT,  cognitive
processes  can  be  divided  into  lower-order  levels,  including  remem-
bering, understanding, and applying, and higher-order levels, including
analyzing,  evaluating,  and  creating  (Anderson  &  Krathwohl,  2001).
Although BRT was not originally designed for self-report classification,
its hierarchical structure provides a useful framework for distinguishing
the levels of cognitive tasks that learners offload to AI during learning.
Singh et al. (2025) systematically examined the influence of AI across
the  six  levels  of  human  cognitive  processing,  providing  an  important
point of reference for this study. In addition, the cognitive load theory

provides  complementary  support  for  this  classification  by  suggesting
that higher-order cognitive processes typically require greater cognitive
effort and impose heavier cognitive demands than lower-order processes
(Sultanova,  2025).  Building  on  this  logic,  this  study  used  BRT  as  a
task-based framework for differentiating AIBCO levels according to the
levels  of  cognitive  tasks  that  learners  hand  over  to  AI  during  task
execution,  and  aimed  to  distinguish  between
lower-order  and
higher-order AIBCO.

Lower-order AIBCO refers to situations in which learners use AI to
take  over  cognitive  tasks  involving  remembering,  understanding,  and
applying (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). Higher-order AIBCO refers to
situations in which learners use AI to take over cognitive tasks involving
analyzing,  evaluating,  and  creating.  This  distinction  reflects  the  hier-
archical structure of BRT and the assumption, supported by the cogni-
tive  load  theory,  that  higher-order  forms  of  task  processing  typically
require greater cognitive effort and impose heavier cognitive demands
than lower-order forms  (Sultanova,  2025). Importantly,  in this  study,
the classification of lower-order versus higher-order AIBCO was deter-
mined by the cognitive level of the task handed over to AI during task
execution, rather than by the quality or depth of the learner's subsequent
engagement with the AI output.

Concretely, the six levels of AIBCO were operationalized as follows.
Remember-level offloading refers to using AI to retrieve, store, or recall
factual  information  instead  of  relying  on  one's  own  memory.
Understand-level  offloading  refers  to  using  AI  to  interpret,  clarify,  or
summarize information on the learner's behalf. Apply-level offloading
refers to using AI to perform practical or procedural tasks that involve
applying learned knowledge or rules to specific problems. Analyze-level
offloading  refers  to  using  AI  to  break  down,  categorize,  compare,  or
structure  information.  Evaluate-level  offloading  refers  to  using  AI  to
judge the quality, logic, or soundness of ideas, arguments, methods, or
written work. Create-level offloading refers to using AI to generate novel
content, ideas, frameworks, or academic products that a learner could
otherwise construct independently.

Although these six forms of AIBCO are analytically distinguishable,
they  are  interdependent.  In  real  learning  contexts,  many  tasks  are
composite  rather  than  discrete,  often  involving  both  lower-order  and
higher-order  cognitive  processes.  Therefore,  higher-order  offloading
may build upon, and in some cases subsume, lower-order forms of task
processing. In this study, tasks were classified according to the highest-
level  cognitive  operation  that  learners  hand  over  to  AI  during  task
execution.  Thus,  offloading  a  higher-order  task  may  also  entail  off-
loading the lower-order processes embedded within it. Accordingly, this
study treated the six levels as unique yet interconnected dimensions.

1.2. Relationships among perceived AI value, AI-usage capability, AI
evaluation capability, and AI-based cognitive offloading

Drawing  on  value-based  decision-making  and  cognitive  offloading
theories (Gilbert, 2024; Risko &  Gilbert, 2016), learners’  engagement
with AI as an external tool for cognitive offloading may be influenced by
their cost–benefit evaluation of using AI. These include the perceived
value  of  AI,  AI-usage  capability,  AI  evaluation  capability.  This  study
conceptualized  these  variables  as  interconnected  rather  than  causal,
focusing on their associative patterns within higher education.

The perceived AI value was conceptualized in this study as a broad
evaluative  appraisal  of  AI,  reflecting  the  extent  to  which  learners
perceive  AI  as  useful,  trustworthy,  explainable,  fair,  accountable,
transparent,  and  low  in  risk  (Yanit  et  al.,  2023).  Because  AI  systems
often  operate  as  “black  boxes”  for  non-expert  users,  such  value  judg-
ments are likely to be shaped by accessible heuristic cues rather than by
detailed  technical  understanding.  In  this  study,  these  cues  included
fairness,  accountability,  transparency,  explainability,  and  perceived
risk.  Shin  (2021) showed  that  users'  perceptions  of  fairness,  account-
ability,  transparency,  and  explainability  function  as  salient  heuristic
cues  in  shaping  trust  and  the  perceived  usefulness  of  AI  systems.

Similarly,  perceived  risk  can  function  as  an  intuitive  and  affective
heuristic  cue  that  shapes  individuals’  evaluations  of  the  value  and
credibility  of  AI,  thereby  influencing  acceptance  and  use  intentions
(Hong, 2025; Van Schaik et al., 2017). From a value-based decision--
making perspective, external tools that are perceived as more valuable
are  more  likely  to  be  used  for  cognitive  offloading  (Gilbert,  2024).
Accordingly,  a  higher  perceived  AI  value  may  be  associated  with  a
greater tendency to engage in AIBCO.

AI-usage  capability  is  another  important  factor  associated  with
AIBCO.  This  study  conceptualized  it  in  a  relatively  narrow  sense  as
learners' operational proficiency in applying and exploiting AI technol-
ogy  to  accomplish  academic  tasks,  rather  than  as  a  comprehensive
measure  of  AI  literacy.  Following  Wang  et  al.  (2023),  this  construct
refers to the ability to use AI tools effectively, adapt them to task re-
quirements, and integrate them into ongoing workflows. In this sense,
the AI-usage capability captures practical fluency in interacting with AI
systems  across  different  task  contexts,  without  necessarily  implying
deeper  critical  reflection  or  evaluative  judgment  (Held  &  Heubeck,
2025).  This  narrower  operationalization  is  intentional,  as  this  study
distinguished the AI-usage capability from the AI evaluation capability,
which refers to a learner's ability to critically assess, monitor, and judge
AI outputs(Wang et al., 2023).

From a value-based decision-making perspective, greater operational
fluency may lower the perceived effort and time costs of using AI as an
external cognitive resource (Gilbert, 2024). The AI-usage capability can
be considered a central factor that reduces the motor and temporal costs
associated  with  interacting  with  AI  systems.  As  users  become  more
proficient,  the  effort  required  to  invoke  AI  tools  decreases,  enabling
them  to  be  seamlessly  accessed  as  internal  cognitive  resources.  Ac-
cording to the extended mind thesis (Farkas, 2012), frequent and fluent
interactions enable external technologies to become stably embedded in
daily cognitive routines and assume functional roles within the broader
cognitive  system.  Accordingly,  learners  with  stronger  AI-usage  capa-
bility may be more likely to engage in AIBCO.

AI usage capability is positively related to the perceived value of AI.
Positive  heuristic  perceptions  of  AI,  such  as  fairness,  transparency,
explainability, and low risk, enhance the trust of users and acceptance of
AI  technologies  (Fahnenstich  et  al.,  2024).  A  higher  perceived  value
motivates users to explore, experiment with, and repeatedly engage with
AI tools, strengthening their usage capabilities (Held & Heubeck, 2025).
Therefore,  the  perceived  value  of  AI  may  be  related  to  the  AI  usage
capability, and an indirect association may exist between the perceived
value  and  AIBCO  through  the  AI  usage  capability.  While  the
cross-sectional  design  precludes  causal  inference,  this  pattern  is
consistent with the theoretical proposition that a higher perceived value
of AI positively influences usage capability development, which in turn
aligns with greater AIBCO.

While the AI usage capability may account for the pathway through
which the perceived value relates to AIBCO, this relationship is unlikely
to be uniform across individuals. In particular, learners' AI evaluation
capability may condition the strength of this association. The AI eval-
uation capability refers to the ability to analyze, select, and critically
assess  AI  systems  and  their  outputs  (Held  &  Heubeck,  2025).  Gilbert
(2024) further  reinforced  the  significance  of  evaluative  capability  in
determining  offloading  behavior.  Beyond  motor  and  temporal  costs,
Gilbert argued that individuals consider the epistemic cost of relying on

2. Study 1

Study  1  developed  and  validated  a  psychometrically  sound  scale

measuring AIBCO in research-oriented higher education.

2.1. Item generation

The  initial  item  pool  for  the  AIBCO  scale  was  developed  using  a
multistep  procedure  that  integrated  both  theory-  and  data-driven  ap-
proaches  following  established  guidelines  for  scale  development
(Boateng et al., 2018). First, a comprehensive review of existing ques-
tionnaires on cognitive offloading and related constructs was conducted,
along  with  an  analysis  of  the  instruments  used  in  studies  on
AI-supported learning and the delegation of academic tasks to AI. Based
on this, the target construct was defined as AIBCO in research-intensive
higher education contexts, rather than general AI use across all disci-
plinary  settings.  This  scope  restriction  was  intentional  rather  than
incidental. Because AIBCO is expressed through concrete task behaviors,
the  behavioral  manifestations  are  likely  to  vary  across  educational
contexts  and  disciplines.  In  the  initial  stage  of  scale  development,
attempting to cover all possible higher-education tasks within a single
item pool would have risked producing items that were overly generic,
weakly interpretable, or unevenly relevant across student populations.
Therefore, this study anchored the scale in research-intensive academic
contexts, wherein students routinely engaged in tasks such as reading
scholarly literature, learning research methods, working with data, and
producing  academic  writing.  These  activities  provide  a  coherent  and
theoretically meaningful context in which different levels of AIBCO can
be systematically observed and measured.

To complement this literature review with empirical evidence, open-
ended interviews were conducted with 20 students (10 undergraduates
and 10 postgraduates) enrolled in research-focused programs, such as
those involving research-methods courses, term papers, capstone  pro-
jects,  or  theses.  During  these  interviews,  participants  were  asked
whether they use AI in their learning and research, followed by ques-
tions about the specific AI tools they engage with. To ensure consistent
interpretation, AI was defined for participants as any AI-powered tool or
application that can generate text, retrieve information, analyze data, or
assist  with  academic  tasks.  For  descriptive  purposes,  the  reported  AI
tools were grouped into six categories: general models (e.g., ChatGPT),
literature search tools (e.g., Research Rabbit), literature reading tools (e.
g., UPDF), data processing tools (e.g., PyCharm), writing assistance tools
(e.g., Propal), process management tools. Among the surveyed AI tools,
general models (such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek) were reported as the
most widely used, with usage rates far surpassing other types of AI tools.
This  finding highlights the  central role of general-purpose AI tools in
learners' academic and research activities. Participants were specifically
instructed  to consider  AI tools that are  accessible in  mainland China.
This ensured that the responses were based on AI tools commonly used
and available within the local context. Participants were also asked to
describe the tasks they typically hand over to AI in their academic and
research activities, using the prompt: “Which tasks do you hand over to
AI in your daily study and research activities, and how frequently?” The
responses  were  analyzed  to  identify  recurring  themes  and  behaviors,
which were subsequently transformed into candidate items for the scale.
During this process, this study intentionally formulated the items at a
functional  level  rather  than  in  narrowly  discipline-bound  terms.  For

2.2. Questionnaire administration

The  AIBCO  scale  was  administered  using  a  five-point  Likert-type
frequency scale, with response options ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (al-
ways) (Boateng et al., 2018).

Following  established  guidelines  for  scale  development  (Boateng
et al., 2018), this study ensured adequate sample sizes for each phase
of  validation.  Specifically,  an  exploratory  factor  analysis  (EFA)  was
conducted  with  158  participants  (approximately  five  respondents  per
item), and the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with 239 participants,
exceeding the minimum recommended thresholds for robust factor so-
lutions.  The  third  subsample  (n  = 68)  was  used  only  for  preliminary
criterion-related validity (CRV) testing. While modest in size, it served
as an initial check of external correlations rather than a confirmatory
test. To assess the CRV, the Revised Study Process Questionnaire was
used  (Biggs  et  al.,  2001),  which  assessed  students’  approaches  to
learning through 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly
disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The surface learning approach (10 items;
e.g.,  “I  find  I  can  pass  most  assessments  by  memorizing  key  sections
rather than understanding them.“) reflects rote memorization and me-
chanical use of knowledge. The deep learning approach (10 items; e.g.,
“I find that studying gives me a feeling of deep personal satisfaction.“)
reflects intrinsic motivation and active knowledge construction. These
two  approaches  correspond  theoretically  to  lower-  and  higher-order
cognitive  processes  in  BRT.  Specifically,  surface  learning  aligns  with
remembering,  understanding,  and  applying,  whereas  deep  learning
aligns  with  analyzing,  evaluating,  and  creating.  Thus,  the  two  scales
serve as criterion measures to assess the external validity of the AIBCO
scale.

form. The survey link was distributed via university administrators and
course instructors who invited students to participate during their free
time outside of class. The study was conducted in accordance with the
Declaration  of  Helsinki,  and  ethical  approval  was  obtained  from  the

2.3. Results

First, EFA was conducted to examine the dimensional structure and
item quality of the scale. Because lower- and higher-order AIBCO are
conceptually  related,  principal  axis  factoring  with  Promax  oblique
rotation  was  used  as  the  primary  extraction  and  rotation  method.
Principal  component  analysis  with  varimax  rotation  was  performed
(Terluin  et  al.,  2023).  The  Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin  (KMO)  measure  of
sampling  adequacy  was  0.93,  indicating  that  the  data  were  highly
suitable  for  factor  analysis.  Bartlett's  test  of  sphericity  yielded  a
chi-square value of 4330.37, with a significance level of p < 0.05, sug-
gesting  that  the  correlation  matrix  significantly  deviated  from  the
identity  matrix  and  that  sufficient  correlations  existed  between  the
variables  to  meet  the  prerequisites  for  factor  analysis.  Items  were
removed if they showed (1) communalities <0.35, (2) factor loadings

rion. The square roots of the AVE values for lower-order cognitive off-
loading (0.79) and higher-order cognitive offloading (0.80) were both
greater  than  the  latent  correlation  between  the  two  dimensions  (r  =
0.73).  Although  the  correlation  was  substantial,  this  pattern  suggests
that  the  two  dimensions  are  empirically  distinguishable.  At  the  same
time, given their strong association and shared theoretical domain, we
interpret  lower-  and  higher-order  cognitive  offloading  as  related  but
distinguishable dimensions of AIBCO rather than as fully independent
constructs.

Criterion-related validity was further examined using Sample 3. The
results indicate that the surface learning approach is positively associ-
ated with both AI-based lower-order cognitive offloading (r = 0.26) and
AI-based higher-order cognitive offloading (r = 0.28), indicating small
to moderate effect sizes (both ps < 0.05). By contrast, the deep learning
approach is not significantly related to either offloading dimension (r =
0.17, 0.20; ps > 0.05).

2.4. Discussion

Study 1 presents initial psychometric evidence from three indepen-
dent  student  samples  that  these  two  dimensions  capture  meaningful
variations in, whereby learners redistribute different levels of cognitive
processes to AI tools.

The  CRV  evidence  from  Sample  3  further  clarified  how  AIBCO  is
situated within broader learning approaches of students. Using Biggs’
two-factor  Study  Process  Questionnaire  as  an  external  criterion,  deep
learning orientation was observed to be not significantly correlated with
either  lower-  or  higher-order  AI-based  offloading,  whereas  surface
learning  orientation  showed  moderately  positive  correlations  with
either  dimension.  This  pattern  is  theoretically  important  because  it
clarifies  what  higher-order  AIBCO  does  and  does  not  capture.  In  this
study, higher-order AIBCO refers to the extent to which learners hand
over  cognitively  complex  tasks,  such  as  analyzing,  evaluating,  and
creating,  to  AI.  It  does  not  imply  that  learners  themselves  engage  in
deeper, more reflective, or more elaborative learning. Deep learning is

3. Study 2

Building on Study 1, Study 2 examined the interrelationships among
the  perceived  AI  value,  AI-usage  capability,  AI  evaluation  capability,
and AIBCO, providing preliminary construct-validity evidence that the
AIBCO dimensions are meaningfully related to theoretically relevant AI-
related constructs.

3.1. Sample

Study  2  employed  convenience  sampling  at  a  single  university  in
mainland  China.  Accordingly,  the  sample  should  not  be  treated  as  a
representative of all higher-education students. University administra-
tors  distributed  survey  links  generated  through  Credamo  to  students.
After screening for inattentive responses using a validity check item, 667
valid  questionnaires  were  retained.  Among  the  participants,  78.41%
were women.

3.2. Measurement

AIBCO was measured using the scale developed in Study 1. In the
Study 2 sample, the two sub-dimensions demonstrated strong internal
consistency (α = 0.90 and 0.93).

The fairness, accountability, transparency, and explainability (FATE)
of the perceived value of AI were measured using items adapted from
Shin (2021), with three items for each dimension (12 items  in total).
Example items include the following: “The system has no favoritism and
does not discriminate against people” (fairness); “The system requires a
person  in charge who should be accountable for its potential adverse
effects”  (accountability);  “The  evaluation  criteria  of  the  algorithms
should be publicly released and understandable” (transparency); and “I
find  the  algorithms  easy  to  understand”  (explainability).  The  FATE
subscale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.96).

The risk dimension of the perceived value of AI was measured using
three items adapted from Wu et al. (2022), including “Information or
answers provided by AI may be incorrect.” The internal consistency of
the Risk Subscale was 0.88.

To obtain a composite indicator, all risk items were reverse-coded
and aggregated with the value-related items to compute the perceived
AI value in learning. Higher scores reflected a higher perceived value
and lower scores, perceived risk in AI-supported learning environments.
The internal consistency of the combined scale was 0.96.

The AI-usage capability was assessed using items adapted from Held
and Heubeck (2025). An example item is as follows: “I can skillfully use
AI  to  help  me  with  my  daily  work.”  The  initial  three-item  scale
demonstrated low internal consistency (α = 0.64). Item-level inspection
showed that the reverse-coded item (“It is usually hard for me to learn to
use a new GenAI application”) had weak correlations with the other two
items (r = 0.24 and 0.18). Conceptually, this item reflects the perceived
difficulty  in  learning  new  AI  tools  rather  than  the  capability  of  an

individual to effectively use AI for current tasks. Therefore, the item was
excluded from the analysis. The remaining two items showed good in-
ternal consistency (α = 0.84).

The  aforementioned  refinements  were  theoretically  justified  and
consistent with the psychometric recommendations for construct puri-
fication (DeVellis, 2017). The theoretical meaning of the constructs re-
mains unchanged.

The  AI  evaluation  capability  was  measured  using  three  items
adapted from Held and Heubeck (2025). An example item is as follows:
“I can choose a proper solution from various solutions provided by AI.”
The scale demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.90).

3.3. Results

3.3.1. Descriptive statistics and correlation

As listed in Table 3, the perceived value of AI positively correlates
with  the  AI-usage  capability.  The  perceived  value  of  AI  is  positively
correlated  with  both  AI-based  lower-  and  higher-order  cognitive  off-
loading.  Given  the  significant  correlations  among  the  main  variables,
multicollinearity  was  examined  by  inspecting  the  VIF  values.  The
maximum  VIF  was  2.11,  which  is  below  the  commonly  accepted
threshold of 3.00, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern in
this study (Hair, 2010). The diagonal elements listed in Table 3 repre-
sent the AVE values of each construct. All AVE values exceeded 0.60,
which is higher than the recommended cutoff of 0.50, suggesting good
convergent validity. The square root of each construct's AVE was greater
than  its  correlations  with  other  constructs,  supporting  discriminant
validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Harman's single-factor test showed that the first factor accounted for
46.52% of the variance, which was below the 50% threshold, suggesting
that a common method bias was unlikely to substantially influence the
findings. Furthermore, CFA was conducted to assess the potential impact
of common method variance. The one-factor model demonstrated poor
fit (χ2/df = 19.45, GFI = 0.36, RMSEA = 0.16, RMR = 0.16, CFI = 0.57,
and NFI = 0.56), whereas the five-factor model showed a substantially
better, though not perfect, fit (χ2/df = 7.53, GFI = 0.75, RMSEA = 0.09,
RMR = 0.11, CFI = 0.86, and NFI = 0.81). Overall, these results indicate
that  the  data  do  not  support  a  single-factor  structure  dominated  by
common method variance, and that the results do not suggest that CMV
is likely to account for the observed factor structure.

4. Discussion

4.1. Theoretical implications

The core contribution of this work is highlighted through Study 1,
wherein a scale that distinguishes between AI-based lower- and higher-
order cognitive offloading was developed and validated. Based on this
scale,  Study  2  was  conducted,  which  provided  theory-informed  sup-
porting evidence by revealing a patterned set of associations among the
perceived value of AI, AI usage capability, AI evaluation capability, and
both forms of AIBCO. Overall, these findings highlight that, in the age of
AI,  the  issue  of  cognitive  offloading  is  no  longer  only  about  whether
individuals  offload  cognition  or  how  frequently  they  do  so,  but  also
about how and why they choose to hand over different forms of cogni-
tive work to AI systems.

The findings of this work contribute to the literature in several ways.
First, the study advances the measurement of AIBCO by establishing a
scale  that  explicitly  distinguishes  between  lower-  and  higher-order
cognitive  offloading.  Existing  measures  often  rely  on  general  in-
dicators such as AI-usage frequency or technological dependence, which
do not fully capture the complexity of cognitive offloading in learning
contexts (Morales-García et al., 2024). Grounded in BRT, the developed
scale is better suited to capturing differences in the types of cognitive
tasks  that  learners  hand  over  to  AI,  especially  in  research-related
learning and academic activities in higher education in China (Fowler,
2023; Singh et al., 2025).

Second, this study refines the theoretical understanding of cognitive
offloading by showing that in AI-supported higher education, offloading
should  not  be  treated  as  a  unitary  or  undifferentiated  process.  Tradi-
tional  discussions  of  cognitive  offloading  often  focus  on  whether  in-
dividuals  rely  on  external  aids  to  reduce  cognitive  effort  (Risko  &
Gilbert,  2016). Herein, this  perspective  is  extended  by  demonstrating
that learners may hand over qualitatively different levels of cognitive
tasks to AI, ranging from lower-order tasks such as remembering, un-
derstanding,  and  applying  to  higher-order  tasks  such  as  analyzing,
evaluating, and creating. This suggests that AIBCO is better understood
as a differentiated process structured by the cognitive level of the work
transferred  to  AI,  rather  than  as  a  single  general  tendency  toward
external  support.  This  distinction  is  theoretically  important  because
offloading  a  lower-order  task  is  not  equivalent  to  offloading  a
higher-order task, as the two involve different forms of cognitive work
and different levels of cognitive demand (O'Brien et al., 2022).

Third,  this  study  provides  empirical  evidence  that  is  broadly
consistent with Gilbert's (2024) value-based decision model of cognitive
offloading in AI-supported contexts. By showing that learners' percep-
tions of AI value and capability are systematically associated with their
cognitive offloading, this study connects the cognitive offloading theory
with the study of AI perceptions and extends the current understanding
of when and how learners may make offloading decisions in AI-assisted
learning contexts.

3.4. Discussion

Study 2 showed that the perceived AI value was indirectly associated
with both types of cognitive offloading via the AI-usage capability. This
indirect association was conditioned by the AI evaluation capability: it
became nonsignificant for lower-order offloading under a high evalua-
tion capability but remained significant for higher-order offloading. This
pattern is broadly consistent with the framework established by Gilbert
(2024),  who  proposed  that  individuals  decide  whether  to  offload
cognitive  tasks  based  on  a  rational  cost–benefit  evaluation  of  the
cognitive effort. The findings of Study 2 also resonate with prior evi-
dence  indicating  that  the  positive  perceived  value  of  AI  is  associated
with greater willingness to rely on AI assistance (Zhai et al., 2024). This
interpretation  is  also  consistent  with  the  opportunity–cost  model  of
cognitive effort (Sharot et al., 2023), which suggests that people may
allocate mental resources in ways that minimize effort while preserving
the perceived value through external assistance.

Notably, under a high evaluation capability, the indirect association
between the perceived value of AI and lower-order cognitive offloading
via the AI-usage capability became insignificant. This finding is consis-
tent with prior evidence suggesting that stronger monitoring or more
cautious  evaluation  has  been  linked  to  lower  reliance  on  automation
(Siau  &  Wang,  2020).  However,  when  the  dependent  variable  was
higher-order cognitive offloading, the indirect effect remained signifi-
cant even under a high evaluation capability. At first glance, this pattern
may appear somewhat paradoxical, given that the AI evaluation capa-
bility  was  positively  correlated  with  both  lower-  and  higher-order
cognitive offloading, while also weakening the indirect association be-
tween the perceived AI value and offloading via the AI-usage capability.
One plausible interpretation is that students with a stronger evaluation
capability may still engage in AIBCO and may even do so relatively often
overall,  but their offloading  is less  dependent on  usage fluency alone
(Siemens  et  al.,  2022).  Instead,  their  decisions  may  be  shaped  more
strongly  by  critical  monitoring,  selective  judgment,  and  task-specific
assessment of when AI support is warranted (Kosmyna et al., 2025b).

Importantly, higher-order AIBCO herein refers to offloading cogni-
tively  demanding  tasks  to  AI,  rather  than  the  evidence  that  learners
themselves  engaged  in  higher-order  thinking  or  deeper  learning.  One
possible  interpretation  is  that  higher-order  cognitive  offloading  typi-
cally involves tasks with greater cognitive demands, which may sustain
reliance on AI even among individuals with relatively strong evaluative
capability. In such cases, learners may not rely on AI uncritically, but
may  instead  combine  AI  use  with  ongoing  monitoring  and  selective
judgment.  This  pattern  may  be  more  consistent  with  a  collaborative

(1)  Implications for educators and institutions

First, the results highlight the importance of shifting from a limited
focus  on  using  AI  to  systematically  cultivate  abilities  of  students  to
evaluate AI. In developing AI literacy initiatives and course curricula,
higher education institutions should explicitly incorporate learning ob-
jectives  such  as  identifying  the  limitations  and  risks  of  AI-generated
outputs,  understanding  the  sources  of  uncertainty,  and  making
evidence-based  judgments  about  AI  suggestions.  These  evaluative
competencies should be considered core learning outcomes rather than
supplementary skills attached to prompt engineering or tool operations.
Second, universities should strengthen their disciplinary foundations
and competencies. When integrating AI into professional curricula, ed-
ucators should avoid considering it as a shortcut that lowers disciplinary
rigor  (Zhong  et  al.,  2025).  Instead,  domain-specific  knowledge  and
critical reasoning training should serve as the cognitive foundation for
developing the AI evaluation capabilities of students.

Educators should adopt different instructional strategies for different
learning tasks. Because lower- and higher-order AIBCO involve different
types  of  task  demands,  they  may  require  different  instructional  re-
sponses. In some cases, heavy reliance on AI for lower-order tasks may
be  associated  with  more  superficial  engagement,  whereas  AI  use  in
higher-order  tasks  may  create  opportunities  for  guided  reflection,
critique, and structured support for thinking.

(2)  Implications for AI learners

The  study  findings  underscore  the  importance  of  metacognitive
regulation and reflective use of AI for learners. Students should recog-
nize that not all cognitive tasks are equally suitable for offloading to AI.
Different forms of offloading may present different pedagogical risks and
opportunities.  For  example,  reliance  on  AI  for  relatively  routine  task
components  may  sometimes  be  associated  with  more  passive engage-
ment,  whereas  AI  use  in  more  open-ended  tasks  may  invite  greater
monitoring,  comparison,  and  revision.  Learners  are  encouraged  to
actively monitor when to use, how to use, and when to disengage from
AI assistance, as well as reflect on the influence of AI interaction on their
own  understanding  and  skill  development.  Through  such  reflective
engagement, learners may be better positioned to use AI in ways that
preserve cognitive agency while still benefiting from AI-supported task
assistance.

(3)  Implications for AI system and platform designers

Finally, this study offers insights into the design of human-centered
AI  systems.  At  the  system  level,  AI  platforms  should  be  designed  to
enhance human agency by reserving space for user decision-making and
reflection. For instance, systems could require users to specify learning
goals before generating outputs, present multiple alternative solutions
rather than a single “best” answer, and explicitly indicate uncertainty or
risk  levels  to  support  informed  choice.  These  design  strategies  may
reduce  uncritical  forms  of  offloading  and  encourage  more  reflective
engagement.

AI interfaces can incorporate external scaffolds to support evaluative
reasoning.  Practical  implementations  include  displaying  information
sources alongside the generated content, providing built-in questioning
prompts  (e.g.,  “What  assumptions  underlie  this  answer?”  or  “What
opposing views might exist?“), requiring users to specify the reasons for
adopting or rejecting AI suggestions or embedding peer and instructor
review  mechanisms  for  AI-generated  outputs.  Such  scaffolds  may

First,  although  two  sequential  studies  were  conducted—one  for
establishing  the  AIBCO  scale  and  the  other  for  testing  the  proposed
model—both studies relied on self-reports and cross-sectional data. In
particular, Study 2 used a single-source design; therefore, the observed
relationships  among  the  perceived  AI  value,  AI-usage  capability,  AI
evaluation capability, and AIBCO should be interpreted as associative
rather  than  causal,  and  they  do  not  establish  temporal  ordering.  In
addition, common-method variance cannot be ruled out. The observed
associations may partly reflect a more general positive orientation to-
ward  AI  rather  than  the  fully  distinct  underlying  mechanisms.  Other
unmeasured  factors  such  as  prior  AI  experience,  frequency  of  AI  use,
degree level, disciplinary background, or institutional norms may have
contributed to the observed pattern. Moreover, Study 2 relied on con-
venience sampling from a single university in mainland China, raising
concerns  regarding  the  external  validity  and  limiting  the  generaliz-
ability of the findings to other institutional, disciplinary, and cultural
contexts.  Future  research  should  adopt  longitudinal,  experimental,
multi-site,  and  multi-source  designs  to  test  the  temporal  dynamics  of
AIBCO,  reduce  common-method  concerns,  and  examine  whether  the
study findings hold across broader higher-education populations.

Second,  almost  all  participants  were  university  students;  this  may
have limited the external validity of the findings. Previous studies have
shown that university teachers tend to adopt more cautious and reflec-
tive  attitudes  toward  AI  integration  compared  to  students.  Therefore,
future  research  could  explore  the  determinants  and  mechanisms  of
AIBCO among teachers, whose perceptions, responsibilities, and peda-
gogical contexts may lead to distinct offloading behaviors and decision
patterns (Saito et al., 2025).

Third, the AIBCO scale was developed in a research-intensive higher-
education  context,  and  its  behavioral  referents  reflect  that  scope.
Although the items were coded at a functional level to extend the scale
across  a  range  of  research-oriented  disciplines,  some  items  are  less
applicable  to  practice-based,  studio-based,  or  performance-oriented
programs  in  which  students’  academic  tasks  take  substantially
different  forms.  This  indicates  that  the  current  scale  should  be  inter-
preted  as  a  context-specific  measure  of  AIBCO  in  research-intensive
academic settings rather than as a  universally applicable measure for
all higher-education contexts.

Future research can extend the scale to other disciplinary environ-
ments  by  retaining  the  underlying  conceptual  distinction  among  the
levels of AIBCO, while revising the behavioral referents of the items to fit
the task demands and academic practices of those contexts. For example,
in non-research-intensive contexts, items could be reformulated around
discipline-specific activities such as design ideation, composition, studio
critique,  lesson  planning,  clinical  reasoning,  or  performance  prepara-
tion.  Subsequent  studies  should  then  re-evaluate  the  scale's  content
validity, factor structure, and measurement invariance in those contexts.
Fourth, while the psychometric properties of the AIBCO scale met
conventional thresholds, the authors acknowledge that the sample sizes
in  Study  1—particularly  for  the  CRV  analysis—were  relatively  small.
Accordingly, the findings should be viewed as preliminary evidence for
the scale's dimensional structure. Future research with larger and more
diverse samples is needed to replicate these results and further test the
scale's stability and generalizability across academic contexts.

Fifth,  although  the  CFA  results  were  generally  supportive  of  the
proposed two-factor structure, the RMSEA value was slightly above the
conventional thresholds for a close fit. This suggests that the measure-
ment  model  should  be  interpreted  with  caution  and  that  further
refinement of the scale structure may be warranted in future research. In
addition, the post hoc refinements of the measurement scales in Study 2
require  further  validation.  While  these  adjustments  can  improve  reli-
ability and conceptual coherence, they may reduce comparability with

5. Conclusion

Ethical approval

This study described the structure and associative patterns of AIBCO
in higher education through the development of a measurement scale
and an examination of their interrelationships. The AIBCO scale differ-
entiates between lower- and higher-order offloading, offering a practical
instrument  for  future  studies.  The  study  further  identified  how  the
perceived AI value, AI-usage capability, and AI evaluation capability are
associated with AIBCO. In the context of the growing integration of AI
into  higher  education,  this  integrative  framework  offers  a  nuanced
perspective  on  the  cognitive  dimensions  of  human–AI  collaboration.
Furthermore,  the  study  suggests  theoretical  directions  for  designing
educational  and  technological  practices  that  encourage  collaborative
and  reflective  engagement  with  AI,  rather  than  uncritical  cognitive
delegation.
