Students’ ability to manage their own learning is crucial for academic success, but many struggle
with self-regulation, often leading to disengagement. Past studies have explored tactics to boost
students’  self-regulated learning (SRL), such as writing self-reflective reports and incorporating
prompts in video lectures. These tactics, however, often lack timely, personalized feedback due to
the labor-intensive nature of such support. In this study, we report the development and imple-
mentation  of  an  innovative  large  language  model  (LLM)-based  recommender  system  named
SRLAdvisor  to  address  these  limitations.  The  system  combines  a web  interface  with  a  series  of
LLM-based agents, allowing students to receive immediate, personalized feedback. By processing
students’  natural language interactions in real-time, SRLAdvisor dynamically profiles their self-
regulation  and  delivers  personalized  recommendations.  This  study  involved  22  postgraduate
students and employed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach to evaluate students’
self-regulation and learning performance. We found near-perfect agreement between the system
detection  and  human  coding.  SRLAdvisor  exhibited  greater  recommendation  precision  in  self-
monitoring  and  self-evaluation  tasks.  Students  who  perceived  the  system  as  highly  useful
showed  significantly  greater  self-reported  SRL  gains  than  those  with  lower  perceptions.  Addi-
tionally,  a  k-means  cluster  analysis  indicated  that  students  who  engaged  more  frequently  with
SRLAdvisor achieved significantly greater improvements in learning performance. These findings
underscore the potential of leveraging LLMs to deliver personalized recommendations. However,
they also reveal potential concerns, such as over-reliance on AI and prompt monotony, that need
to be further examined. These issues are particularly relevant in the context of LLM-supported
SRL interventions and warrant further discussion.

2. Theoretical perspective and previous work

This study is based on Zimmerman’s (2002) three-phase cyclical model of SRL, which includes the forethought, performance, and
self-reflection phases. According to Panadero (2017), Zimmerman’s model is the most frequently cited in the field. In the forethought
phase, students use SRL strategies (i.e., techniques or actions that students perform) such as setting goals and planning appropriate
actions for the upcoming task. Specific goals lead to consistent performance (Latham & Locke, 1991), while pursuing an ambitious and
unrealistic goals may cause disappointment (Shih et al., 2010). Goal setting and planning are interlinked, as students define learning
goals and then plan actions to achieve them.

The performance phase involves adopting strategies like time management and help-seeking to stay engaged and make adjustments
as needed. In this phase, four types of metacognitive questions (comprehension, connection, strategic, reflection) can be used to bolster
students’ proficiency in using SRL strategies (Mevarech & Fridkin, 2006). Comprehension questions prompt students to identify the
main ideas in the task problem. Connection problems direct students to relate the present problem to previously solved problems.
Strategic questions can be regarded as strategies for addressing the problems. Reflection questions can guide students to review their
learning process from time to time. The self-evaluation phase involves assessing performance against standards and attributing out-
comes to specific factors, which influences future forethought processes and efforts to improve success.

Given the importance of SRL in online activities, significant efforts have been made to support it. One method involves pre-defined
reflective questions or prompts (Moos &  Bonde, 2016) to address different SRL phases (e.g., "Do you need to adjust how you are
learning?"). These questions are integrated into video lectures (Moos & Bonde, 2016; van Alten et al., 2020) or stored in a database for
students to access at their discretion (Shyr &  Chen, 2018). However, these pre-set questions lack personalization, potentially frus-
trating novices while failing to challenge advanced learners. They also do not suggest follow-up SRL actions, thus limiting their impact.
Another method uses visualization tools like bar charts and spider charts to show learners’ progress, performance, or interaction

formation without suggesting concrete follow-up actions, leaving students with weak self-regulation unsure of next steps.

To address this, some studies have included recommendations alongside visualization tools to suggest relevant SRL actions using
trace  data.  For  example,  Du  et  al.  (2024) used  a  spider  chart  to  display  information  on  six  SRL  strategies  (e.g.,  goal  setting  and
planning, monitoring, cognitive strategy, time management, self-evaluation, help-seeking), identified students’ weakest SRL strategy,
and offered relevant recommendations. By considering students’ deficiency in using SRL strategies, these recommendations provide
more  targeted  strategies  for  improvement.  However,  current  recommender  systems  provide  one-way  recommendations  without
allowing student interaction or questions, limiting their effectiveness.

In recent years, LLMs have gained significant attention due to their ability to offer timely and personalized feedback. While LLMs
have  shown  promise  in  supporting  student  SRL  such  as  by  helping  learners  set  goals  and  plans  (Hew  et  al.,  2025),  facilitating
help-seeking process (Pan et al., 2025), and stimulating the self-reflection activities (Kumar et al., 2024), our understanding of their
full potential remains limited. For instance, while Kumar et al. (2024) utilized LLMs to facilitate self-reflection, their approach lacked
support  for  recommending  other  SRL  actions,  such  as  goal-setting  or  self-monitoring.  Other  studies  reported  that  the  LLM  often
produced lengthy responses with strategies that did not address their specific knowledge needs (Ng et al., 2024).

3. Research purpose and questions

To address the limitations of previous research on supporting SRL in online activities, we introduce SRLAdvisor, a novel LLM-based
recommender system. This system offers two key advantages over previous methods. First, unlike static approaches such as pre-set
questions  embedded  in  videos  or  databases,  SRLAdvisor  employs  Conversational  Self-Regulated  Learning  Profiling  (CSRLP)  to
analyze students’ conversational data in real time and extract information related to students’ self-regulation. This enables the system
to build each student’s dynamic self-regulation profile and provide personalized, adaptive recommendations as they progress. This
reduces frustration and boredom by matching the difficulty and complexity of the support to the learner’s needs.

Second,  the  system  utilizes  the  capabilities  of  LLMs  to  provide  specific,  actionable  follow-up  guidance  for  SRL  strategies.  For
example, to enhance students’ goal-setting, SRLAdvisor can proactively introduce the SMART framework and guide them step-by-step
in  applying  it  effectively.  Unlike  current  recommender  systems  that  offer  one-way  suggestions,  SRLAdvisor  enables  two-way
communication. Students can ask questions, seek clarifications, and provide feedback on the recommendations. This interaction helps
ensure that students understand the suggestions and feel supported throughout their learning journey.

To support replicability, we ropvided a thick description of our development and implementation, enabling other researchers and
educators to reproduce our work and drive further innovation in this field. The following four research questions guided our study.

4. Design and implementation of SRLAdvisor

4.1. Overall structure

The  LLM-based  recommender  system  comprises  three  components:  (a)  Task  Agent,  (b)  Recommendation  Agent,  and  (c)  SRL
widgets (Fig. 1). The LLM model used was ChatGPT4o. SRLAdvisor was developed as a web-based application with a Vue.js front-end
providing interactive user interfaces for learners, and a Django (Python) back-end responsible for managing data, integrating the
OpenAI API for ChatGPT-4o, and performing structured information extraction (SIE). A MySQL database stored conversational logs,
learner profiles, and behavioural trace data for real-time recommendation generation.

The Task Agent helped students to complete their weekly tasks and stimulate their SRL interactions through embedded prompts
over time. It consists of three sub-agents (TB.1, TB.2, TB.3), each corresponding to different SRL phases: goal-setting and planning,
performance, and self-reflection. The Recommendation Agent was created to provide personalized recommendations tailored to each
student’s needs. At its core, the Conversational Self-Regulated Learning Profiling (CSRLP) method was used to extract each learner’s
self-regulation information. The SRL widgets engaged students through three tools: the Portfolio for goal-setting and planning, the
Progress Bar for self-monitoring, and the Quizbot for self-evaluation. Detailed descriptions of the Task Agent, Recommendation Agent,
and SRL widgets are given in the following sections.

4.2. Task Agent

To initiate the Forethought Phase, students were encouraged to set their goals and plans before starting their formal coursework
each week. During this phase, Task Agent TB.1 helped students identify their weekly learning goals and guided them in selecting
appropriate SRL strategies to achieve these goals (Fig. 2). For example, Task Agent TB.1 prompted students with the following question
“Let’s identify your personal learning goals for this week. Keep in mind tasks like completing the course design and redesign, reviewing
session 1 content and previewing session 2 content. What specific goals would you like to achieve by the end of this week”.

To facilitate the Performance phase, we embedded four metacognitive questions (comprehension, connection, strategic, reflection)
into Task Agent TB.2 (Fig. 3). This agent guided students through the initial course design in a structured manner. First, the agent
facilitated task analysis by asking comprehension questions (e.g., What are the main ideas you have regarding this challenge?) and
connection questions (e.g., How does this challenge connect with any similar challenges or experience you have encountered in the
past?). Next, it introduced strategic questions and suggests a series of recommended strategies (e.g., using principles of problem-based
learning). Students then selected the most appropriate strategies to do their course design task. Finally, the agent concluded with
reflection questions (e.g., “Are there any parts of the task where you faced challenges?), encouraging students to review their learning
process. During the initial course design, the system prompted students to indicate their confidence level on the scale of 1–5 at two
specific time points. This method allows students to gain an overview of their initial course design and reflect on their learning journey.

4.3. Recommendation agent

To provide adaptive and actionable support for SRL in conversational environments, we developed a Recommendation Agent to
generate personalized feedback and recommendations for each learner (see Fig. 5). The Recommendation Agent is grounded in the
Conversational  Self-Regulated  Learning  Profiling  (CSRLP)  method,  which  systematically  creates  and  updates  each  learner’s  self-
regulation profile through structured information extraction (SIE) from their chat conversations with the Task Agents.

(Table 1). This schema includes elements such as a user’s specific goals, detailed plans with associated timeframes and strategies,

To operationalize this process, we employed prompt engineering to guide the Recommendation Agent in extracting relevant in-

phase—forethought, performance, and self-reflection—the agent generates feedback supported by specific examples extracted from
the chat history between the student and the Task Agent. The recommendations are presented in a clear and accessible format within

The Recommendation Agent leverages In-context Learning (ICL) to dynamically adapt its feedback based on the specific context,
without requiring additional training or parameter updates (Dong et al., 2024). This establishes a closed-loop feedback system, where
user chat conversations update the learner self-regulation profile, which in turn guide the generation of personalized responses. Chat
logs are transmitted back to the database in real-time, ensuring the system learns from each interaction and continuously refines its
support strategies. By integrating structured information extraction, ICL, and a closed-loop feedback mechanism, the Recommendation
Agent offers a dynamic, and personalized approach to supporting self-regulated learning. Learners receive guidance that is tailored to
their current goals and strategies, adapts as they progress, resulting in more personalized learning experiences.

4.4. SRL widgets

In addition to chatlogs, we utilized the SRL widgets to record other relevant self-regulation log data. Recording other relevant self-

regulation log data is useful because it provides a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of students’ self-regulation.

we collected data on students’ goal-setting and planning behaviors through the Portfolio interface, which students accessed to manage

accessed the portfolio, (b) the number of times they revised their goals and plans, and (c) the number of times they clicked to update
the completion status of a goal or plan.

Additionally, students could monitor their task completion progress in real time by hovering over the Progress Bar (Fig. 7, bottom).
To capture their self-monitoring behaviors, we recorded the number of times they viewed the Progress Bar while completing tasks

learning process.

5. Method

This study used a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell et al., 2003), combining quantitative and qualitative data
collection to provide a comprehensive analysis of the SRLAdvisor system. The use of mixed methods enables us to address both the
breadth and depth of the research questions: quantitative data allow us to assess the effectiveness of SRLAdvisor on student outcomes,
while qualitative data offer deeper insights into how and why these outcomes occurred from the perspective of individual learners.
In the quantitative phase, we collected data from three primary sources: students’ self-reported surveys, trace data and learning
performance metrics. These data were used to evaluate the effectiveness of the SRLAdvisor system and its impact on students’ out-
comes. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interview and conversational data were collected to gain deeper insights into students’
experiences and perceptions of the SRLAdvisor system. The qualitative data were then analyzed in the context of the quantitative
findings, enabling a richer and more nuanced understanding of students’ experience. This integrated approach ensured a thorough
exploration of both the system’s performance and students’ engagement with it.

This study involved 22 postgraduate students (7 males and 15 females) from a university in Hong Kong. The participants were
enrolled in a course aimed at developing skills in designing courses for adult learners. During the course, students learned about key
assumptions  underlying  adult  learning  and  explored  effective  instructional  strategies  to  engage  adult  learners.  The  course  was
delivered in a blended format, consisting of weekly in-person sessions while all weekly course practice tasks were completed remotely
online. Students were required to submit the weekly online course practice tasks by set deadlines, but could complete them at any time

5.2.2. Performance of recommendations in SRLAdvisor

To measure the performance of the SRLAdvisor recommendation system, we used the precision and recall metrics adapted from Li
et al. (2024). Precision measures the proportion of recommended items that the user adopts. Recall represents the system’s ability to
identify and cover all useful items that are relevant to the user. Together, they provide a balanced view of both relevance and coverage
(Table 3). In this study, we define "True Positive" as the system recommended an SRL strategy, and the user adopted it, “True Negative”
as the system did not recommend an SRL strategy and the students did not adopt it, "False Positive" as the system recommended an SRL
strategy but user did not adopt it. "False Negative" as the system did not recommend an SRL strategy but the user independently
adopted it.

5.2.3. Perceived usefulness

Students’ perceived usefulness was assessed using a five-point Likert scale questionnaire adapted from Davis (1989), ranging from 1
("strongly disagree") to 5 ("strongly agree"). Perceived usefulness refers to “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular
system would enhance his or her job performance” (Davis, 1989, p. 320). The questionnaire included five items, such as “Overall, I
found the chatbot was useful in my learning”.

5.2.4. SRL gains, and student perceptions of system

We evaluated the differences in SRL gains between students with low- and high-perceived usefulness. Students were divided into
either a high-perceived usefulness group, or a low-perceived usefulness group based on the mean survey score. To assess SRL gains, we
administered the Online Self-regulated Learning Questionnaire (OSLQ) (Barnard et al., 2009) at two time points: before (session 1) and
after (session 7) using the SRLAdvisor system. The OSLQ consists of 24 items rated on a five-point Likert scale, with an example item
being, “I set standards for my assignments in online courses.” The reliability of the questionnaire was high in this study, with Cron-
bach’s α coefficients of 0.81 for the pre-test and 0.86 for the post-test. SRL gains were calculated as the difference between post-survey
and pre-survey scores.

To gain deeper insights into students’ perception of the SRLAdvisor system, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 17
students. Students were asked to describe how they found the system useful and suggest improvements. An example interview question
was,  “What  do  you  like  most  regarding  this  system?”  We  analyzed  students’  responses  inductively  using  the  grounded  approach
(Bryant & Charmaz, 2007), from which similar codes were sorted and combined until no more new codes could be generated.

The analysis began with initial coding, followed by focused coding and then axial coding, as outlined by Mirhosseini (2020, pp.
133–146). Throughout this process, we were guided by our research question to ensure that our coding remained relevant to the
study’s aims. During the initial coding stage, we carefully examined the interview transcripts and generated a total of 207 distinct
codes. For example, a typical code derived from a student’s comment was, “It was really helpful for my review.” In the focused coding
phase, we grouped similar and repetitive codes to identify overarching patterns and themes within the data. For instance, the codes “It
was really helpful for my review” and “It allows me to reflect on my learning progress” were both interpreted as expressing the theme
of “Facilitating students’  self-evaluation.”  Next, in the axial coding phase, we emphasized data reduction and organization. Minor
themes identified during focused coding were further analyzed and categorized into broader, more significant themes. A human coder
analyzed all the interview data and categorized them into relevant themes concerning the strengths and weaknesses of system. A
second human coder randomly selected and reviewed 20 % of the interview data. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion
between the two coders.

5.2.5. Behavioural engagement and learning performance

To investigate students’ behavioral engagement with SRLAdvisor and its impact on learning performance, we conducted a two-step

analysis involving clustering and performance evaluation.

We used clustering to distinguish high- and low-SRL behavioral profiles directly from learners’  trace data, consistent with SRL
analytics  practices  (e.g.,  Ye  &  Pennisi,  2022).  For  this  purpose,  unsupervised  clustering  is  suitable  because  it  detects  naturally
occurring patterns in trace data without imposing arbitrary cutoffs, consistent with SRL analytics practices (e.g., Ye & Pennisi, 2022).
Although N = 22 is modest, each student generated dense, fine-grained trace data across multiple activities. This increases the in-
formation content per participant and enabling meaningful differentiation of engagement behaviors aligned with SRL processes. We
relied on theory-based trace data and triangulated the clustering results with qualitative data to ensure robustness.

Specifically, we employed k-means clustering to categorize students based on their behavioral trace data (Table 2). Behavioral
engagement, defined as participation in learning activities (Fredricks et al., 2004), was central to this analysis. The clustering resulted
in two distinct groups: students with low behavioral engagement (Cluster 1) and those with high behavioral engagement (Cluster 2).
These profiles served as an analytic lens for subsequent learning performance comparisons.

To assess learning performance, we administered a pre-test and post-test. The pre-test, consisting of five items, evaluated students’
initial knowledge of course design before the intervention began. Sample pre-test items included: “Explain Jean Piaget’s learning
theory,”  and “Describe at least three principles of adult learning.”  Responses were independently scored by two trained coders to
ensure reliability. Four weeks after the intervention, students completed an individual delayed post-test. In this task, they were asked
to design a workshop for adult learners, such as higher education educators or corporate trainers, focused on understanding Generation
Z’s unique characteristics and learning needs in an increasingly AI-driven world. An independent coder evaluated their performance

Finally, we used statistical tests to analyze the differences in learning performance between the two clusters. A Mann-Whitney U
test was applied to examine pre-existing differences, while an independent samples t-test was used to assess post-intervention dif-
ferences. This approach allowed us to investigate how behavioral engagement with SRLAdvisor influenced learning outcomes.

5.3. Procedure

Fig. 9 shows the overall research procedure. In the first session, students completed a pre-survey to assess their baseline ability of
using SRL strategies and course design ability. Over five weeks, the Task Agent distributed weekly course design scenarios to help
students practice their skills, while the Quizbot assigned weekly quizzes to reinforce their knowledge comprehension. Based on their
evolving self-regulation profiles, the Recommendation Agent provided personalized SRL strategy recommendations to each student.
Two weeks after the intervention, students completed a post-survey to evaluate their proficiency in using SRL strategies and their
perceived usefulness of the SRLAdvisor system. Additionally, students were invited to participate in interviews to share their per-
ceptions and experiences with the SRLAdvisor system, providing deeper insights into its impact and effectiveness. Four weeks after the
intervention, a delayed post-test was administered to assess students’ course design proficiency.

6.3.3.1. Personalized  recommendations  to  enhance  students’  SRL. To  provide  more  direct  and  solid  evidence  that  the  AI-based
personalized system supported students’ SRL, we analyzed the student interview data with an explicit focus on SRL. Interviews can
provide reliable evidence concerning students’ self-regulation (Zimmerman & Pons, 1986). Interviews are useful because SRL com-
prises internal processes (planning, monitoring, reflection) that are not fully captured by logs or scores. Interviews can elicit more
detailed accounts of how the AI system helped students set goals, monitored progress, and reflected on their learning activities. In this
section, we presented qualitative evidence that suggested students set more specific goals, monitored progress more regularly, and
reflected more frequently. Together, these qualitative findings strengthen the claim that the AI system helped students’ SRL.

A majority of students indicated that the system could help with shaping their goals from broad, distal objectives to specific,
proximal targets. As student 7 reflected, “In the first session, I have no idea, so I set a very ambitious goal. But by the second session,
when I return to the task, I have a clearer understanding, and the system explicitly suggests revising it into a micro-lesson level goal”.
Another student 14 further noted, “I probably wouldn’t plan so meticulously. I might simply note what my assignment is and when to
finish it. But with the system, I’m prompted to extend my thinking regarding what else I could do, refine the details, and allocate time
appropriately.” (see Table 4 for an example) In this way, our system helps students to articulate their goals and plans become pro-
gressively more specific, measurable, and attainable.

Second,  the  system  reinforced  students’  awareness  of  setting  goals  and  plans. This  was  especially  evident  for  Student  8,  who
“normally did not set goals or plans for the course.” The training also helped students carry these skills beyond the current course. As
Student 1 explained, “When planning learning objectives for other courses, I recalled and applied the goal-setting approaches learned
through the system.” In short, students used these strategies in the course and generalized them to other courses.

Over time, students reported that SRLAdvisor produced more precise, actionable recommendations each week. As Student 16 noted,
“It helps us see the improvements we’ve made in our weekly assignments.” Student 10 added, “I can’t quite remember whether it was
Bloom’s taxonomy or something else, but I forgot it a second time, and the system pointed it out again. It reminded me that I didn’t
fully grasp it.” These insights helped students track both progress and recurring weaknesses, enabling them to better monitor, and
adjust their behaviors. In another example, a student had not been checking his learning activity progress. The system prompted:
“Increase engagement in self-monitoring by using the system’s tools to track learning progress and make notes to reinforce under-
standing  and  retention  of  key  concepts.”  Thereafter,  the  student  viewed  his  progress  more  frequently—five  times—indicating
heightened self-monitoring awareness.

Students also reported that the AI system supported self-reflection, but importantly, it also prompted self-initiated regulation. For
example, after a quiz exposed misunderstandings, Student 5 took the initiative to request more practice: “When I made many errors on
a 10-item quiz, I asked the AI system for additional practice on the concepts I missed.” This learner-driven request is important because
it shows that students move beyond recognizing gaps to independently seeking targeted remediation, turning awareness into meta-
cognitive control. The ensuing cycle (diagnose, plan, practice, and review) strengthened self-evaluation. Weekly quizzes made gaps
visible, as Student 10 noted, “I might indeed have problems with the learning content, but I wasn’t fully aware of them”. The option to
request customized quizzes enabled students to monitor progress and adjust strategies, thereby deepening their self-reflection over
time.

7. Discussion

This study introduced the SRLAdvisor, a system that integrates Task Agent, Recommendation Agent and SRL widgets. This system
aims to deliver personalized, actionable recommendations for students’ follow-up SRL actions. We first evaluated the performance of
SRLAdvisor based on the accuracy of the CSRLP method, the precision and recall of the recommendation agent, and students’ perceived
usefulness. Then, we tested the impact of the SRLAdvisor on students’ proficiency in using SRL strategies and learning performance. In
this section, we reflect on the main findings and propose implications for future practice.

7.1. Accuracy of the CSRLP method

Our findings indicate that SRLAdvisor’s CSRLP method achieved near-perfect agreement with human coding, demonstrating its
reliability in detecting students’ self-regulation information from the chat conversational data. This high level of accuracy is imper-
ative for offering personalized recommendations to each student.

7.2. Effectiveness of the recommendations

Fig. 10. Two clusters based on students’ log variables.

SRLAdvisor demonstrated a precision of 0.69 and a recall of 0.56 in its recommendations. Precision and recall are inversely related
(Herlocker et al., 2004). In the context of a SRL recommender system, achieving a higher precision score is often more critical than a
higher recall score. Precision measures the proportion of recommended strategies that are actually relevant to the student. A high
precision score indicates that the system excels at providing recommendations tailored to the student’s learning needs. In contrast,
recall measures the system’s ability to recommend as many strategies as possible that could potentially help the student. When more
strategies are recommended, the recall will increase but precision decreases because some of the strategies may be irrelevant.

In an SRL system, precision is particularly important because students rely on accurate and actionable strategies to improve their
learning habits. While the system may identify a wide range of potentially useful strategies (high recall), including too many irrelevant
recommendations (low precision) can overwhelm or frustrate students. Therefore, prioritizing precision ensures that students receive
relevant SRL recommendations, which in turn fosters student engagement with the system.

The results further revealed that self-monitoring achieved the highest precision score, followed by self-evaluation, and then goal-
setting. This is likely because self-monitoring and self-evaluation recommendations are immediate and actionable. They enable stu-
dents to assess their understanding of the course content in real time, identify knowledge gaps, and adjust their learning strategies
accordingly. Analysis of student interviews suggested that such recommendations were particularly beneficial, as they helped students
quickly pinpoint weaknesses in their understanding of the material, providing immediate value to their learning process.

In contrast, goal-setting and planning recommendations were perceived as less relevant especially in later sessions of the course.
Goal-setting and planning recommendations, which often focus on broader, future-oriented objectives, may not align with students’
immediate needs or perceived urgency. Additionally, the repetitive nature of goal-setting and planning recommendations may have

contributed to their perceived irrelevance. When the recommender system repeatedly prompts students to set goals and plans for the
coming week, students may feel bored or disengaged. Over time, this monotony can reduce the perceived value of such recommen-
dations, making them seem less meaningful.

Therefore, while self-monitoring and self-evaluation recommendations provide immediate, actionable insights that resonate with
students’  short-term learning goals, goal-setting and planning recommendations may need to be more personalized, and varied to
enhance their perceived relevance and effectiveness. Addressing potential monotony and demonstrating the value of these recom-
mendations in a compelling way could help increase student engagement and motivation.

7.3. Perceived usefulness and behavioral engagement

Students generally found SRLAdvisor to be highly useful. Those who perceived it as more useful showed greater improvement in
their self-reported SRL. It is possible that students who viewed SRLAdvisor as highly useful were more motivated to reflect on and
report their use of SRL strategies, leading to higher self-reported SRL gains. Students who had higher levels of engagement with the
system demonstrated more significant academic performance. The most significant feature of the SRLAdvisor is its ability to deliver
personalized recommendations, which became increasingly precise and relevant as SRLAdvisor accumulated more data over time.
Students noted that the system’s recommendations effectively linked their’ previous SRL strategy use to ongoing actions, helping to
identify their strengths and weaknesses. This, in turn, helped them monitor and adjust their SRL strategies when necessary.

Nevertheless, our analysis revealed two concerns associated with using LLM-based Task Agent: prompt monotony (i.e., becoming

repetitive over time), and potential over-reliance on AI. Each will be briefly discussed in turn.

In our present implementation, we intentionally stimulated students’ self-regulation through prompts every week. This approach
was taken to provide all students with repeated opportunities to practice SRL strategies for five weeks. As highlighted in the literature,
SRL strategies require ongoing practice to be effectively developed as it cannot be done overnight or in a week (Pintrich, 1995). For
example, in week 1, students were prompted to set their goals and plans using the SMART framework. Based on students’ responses,
SRLAdvisor  offered  specific  recommendations  to  improve  their  individual  goals  and  plans.  In  subsequent  weeks,  students  were
prompted if they wished to revise their previous goals and plans, or set new goals. The SRLAdvisor system would continue to offer
suggestions for improvement if necessary.

Nevertheless, in retrospect, we observed that as students became more skilled in self-regulation, some found the prompts related to
goal-setting and planning repetitive or unnecessary gradually. One notable finding is that, in the early stages of the course, the system
scaffolded goal setting and planning, enabling students to establish clear directions and regulate their learning more effectively. Over
time, however, many students became increasingly proficient with these strategies. As a result, they tended to approach the goal-
setting prompts with less intensity, often skimming or briefly confirming their plans rather than engaging deeply. This shift sug-
gests  that  the  reduced  uptake  of  recommendations  may  not  simply  indicate  disengagement,  but  rather  a  natural  progression  in
learners’ self-regulatory skills. Such a pattern highlights the potential value of implementing scaffolding fading mechanisms, where the
intensity and frequency of guidance gradually decrease as students develop greater autonomy. Moving forward, we plan to revise
SRLAdvisor so that it uses prompts such as goal-setting and planning repeatedly during the first two weeks, providing an initial scaffold
for all students. After this introductory period, the system will adapt by reducing the frequency of prompts based on each student’s
demonstrated SRL skills. By evolving in this way, SRLAdvisor will be better positioned to support learners at different stages of self-
regulated learning, making its contribution more widely applicable.

An emerging issue in the field of educational technology is the potential for over-reliance on AI systems (Darvishi et al., 2024; Hou
et al., 2025). This concern is not unique to our study on using AI to support SRL; rather, it is relevant to all practice involving AI in
education. As AI tools become more integrated into learning environments, there is a risk that students may become dependent on
these systems for guidance. Reducing AI over-reliance is therefore important to ensure that students actively engage with the learning
materials.

To address this, human teachers play an essential and complementary role in supporting student learning. For example, teachers
might review AI-generated feedback, pose probing questions, and intervene when students show signs of disengagement or over-
reliance on AI. Moreover, teachers can encourage students to explain their reasoning  when accepting or rejecting AI suggestions
(Chen et al., 2025). Such practices can help students become more active participants in their own learning.

Emotional support from teachers is also crucial, especially when students feel frustrated during their interactions with AI tools (Liu
et al., 2024). By providing encouragement and empathy, teachers help students persist through challenges and maintain motivation. It
is equally important for teachers to frame AI as a support tool rather than a substitute for human effort. Sharing evidence-based
insights—such as  findings  by  Deslauriers  et  al.  (2019) that increased  cognitive  struggle  is  a  marker  of  effective  learning—can  help
students understand that challenges and effort are integral to deep learning. Teachers can encourage students to embrace cognitive
challenges and develop self-reliance, rather than using AI as a crutch.

8. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that SRLAdvisor can accurately detect students’  self-regulation information and provide personalized,
actionable recommendations that enhance both SRL proficiency and academic performance. In the early stages of the course, students
reported that the system can effectively supported their goal setting, planning, self-monitoring, and self-evaluation. However, over
time, students appeared to benefit primarily from self-monitoring and self-evaluation—particularly when these activities were closely
tied to content or performance—while exhibiting fatigue with the detailed work of goal setting and planning. By embedding targeted
SRL  prompts  within  authentic  learning  tasks  and  analyzing  conversational  data,  the  system  builds  dynamic  learner  profiles  that
support  continuous  self-regulation.  The  results  highlight  the  importance  of  gradually  reducing  AI  assistance,  maintaining  active
teacher involvement and encouraging learners to engage with cognitive challenges. These strategies help maximize the benefits of AI-
driven guidance while preventing overreliance on automated support. Overall, SRLAdvisor offers a promising approach for empow-
ering students to become more autonomous and reflective learners in AI-enhanced educational environments.

This study has several limitations that warrant further research. First, while conversational data served as the primary driver for
analysis, the integration of multimodal and multichannel data, such as eye-tracking or video observation, should be further explored to
enhance personalized recommendations. Exploring these additional data sources would provide a more comprehensive understanding
of students’ SRL.

Second, the sample size was limited to 22 postgraduate students from a single course. In our study, we designed the LLM-based SRL
recommender system based on Zimmerman’s (2002) three-phase SRL model, which includes the forethought, monitoring, and eval-
uation phases. These phases represent core processes fundamental to all learning. Therefore, although our initial implementation
focused on a specific course, the underlying structure remains relevant and can be adapted for use in different educational settings. For
example, prompts can be adjusted to better support younger learners by using simpler language or more concrete examples, while still
maintaining alignment with the overarching SRL framework. We recommend that future research systematically investigate how the
LLM-based SRL recommender system can be adapted and evaluated across diverse disciplines and student populations. Such work will
be important for further validating and refining our approach.

The third limitation of this study is the absence of a control or comparison group, which restricts the ability to isolate the specific
impact of the system. To address this in future research, it would be valuable to implement experimental designs that include control or
comparison groups exposed to varying levels of AI scaffolding. For instance, future studies could compare the effects of SRLAdvisor
with different intensities of AI support, or with traditional instruction and no AI support, to better isolate the system’s unique impact.
