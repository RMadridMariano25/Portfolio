1. Introduction

Background:  Critical  thinking  (CT)  is  essential  in  orthopaedic  nursing  practice,  where  nurses  must  accurately
assess fractures, identify complications, plan mobility strategies, and support postoperative recovery. Concept
mapping (CMAP) can facilitate structured clinical reasoning, while artificial intelligence (AI) may further support
conceptual  organization  and  reflective  learning  processes.  However,  evidence  regarding  AI-assisted  CMAP  in
orthopaedic nursing education remains limited.
Aim:  To  evaluate  the  effectiveness  of  an  AI-assisted  CMAP  program  in  improving  CT  among  undergraduate
students enrolled in an orthopaedic nursing course.
Methods: A two-group quasi-experimental pretest–posttest design was employed. Participants were allocated to
either an experimental group receiving AI-assisted CMAP using ChatGPT (OpenAI) during a structured workshop
or a control group receiving standard instructional activities. CT was evaluated using a validated 12-item CMAP-
based assessment tool administered before and after the intervention. Data were analyzed using mixed-design
ANOVA and ANCOVA.
Results:  CT  scores  improved  significantly  over  time,  with  greater  improvement  observed  in  the  experimental
group. Mean CT scores in the experimental group increased from 2.40 ± 0.50 at pretest to 2.97 ± 0.72 at posttest,
whereas scores in the control group increased from 2.43 ± 0.68 to 2.57 ± 0.63. A significant interaction effect
between  time  and  group  was  identified  (F(1,58)  = 9.705,  p  = .003,  partial  η2  = 0.143).  Students  in  the
experimental group also reported high satisfaction with the AI-assisted CMAP program.
Conclusions:  AI-assisted  CMAP  may  support  CT  development  and  structured  clinical  reasoning  in  orthopaedic
nursing  education.  Integrating  AI-assisted  reflective  support  into  concept-mapping  activities  may  provide  an
effective pedagogical approach for helping nursing students organize and integrate complex clinical information.

Concept mapping (CMAP) is an established pedagogical method that
supports knowledge organization, fosters metacognitive reflection, and
helps  learners  visualize  relationships  among  clinical  concepts  (Novak
and Gowin, 1984). Despite its benefits, students often encounter chal-
lenges constructing concept maps that accurately integrate complex and
interconnected  information,  potentially  limiting  its  impact  on  deeper
analytical reasoning.

Recent technological developments have introduced opportunities to
incorporate  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  tools  into  nursing  education.
Emerging literature indicates that generative AI can assist students in
synthesizing  information,  expanding  conceptual  linkages,  and  orga-
nizing CMAPs more effectively through reflective prompting and scaf-
folded learning support (Han et al., 2025; Labrague and Al Sabei, 2024).

However,  concerns  have  been  raised  that  reliance  on  AI-generated
suggestions  may  impede  independent  reasoning,  contribute  to  over-
reliance on automated outputs, reduce learner autonomy, and result in
inconsistent  effects  on  CT  development  (Abujaber  et  al.,  2023;  Zhai
et  al.,  2024;  Hoffarth,  2026).  In  addition,  inaccurate  or  hallucinated
AI-generated content may influence students’ interpretation of clinical
information  if  AI-supported  learning  is  not  implemented  carefully
(Hoffarth, 2026). These mixed findings underscore the need for empir-
ical research examining how AI can be used to scaffold student learning
without diminishing analytical autonomy.

Although  previous  studies  have  explored  the  use  of  AI-supported
learning in nursing education, most have focused on general academic
support,  knowledge  acquisition,  or  student  satisfaction  rather  than
structured  clinical  reasoning  processes.  In  addition,  existing  studies
have  rarely  examined  the  integration  of  AI-assisted  concept  mapping
within orthopaedic nursing education, where students must synthesize
complex  and  rapidly  changing  clinical  information.  Many  prior  in-
terventions  also  lacked  explicit  mechanisms  to  preserve  independent
reasoning while using AI-supported guidance. Consequently, evidence
remains limited regarding how AI-assisted CMAP may influence critical
thinking within clinically complex nursing contexts.

Within orthopaedic nursing education, CT is particularly vital. Stu-
dents  must  integrate  pathophysiology,  fracture  classification,  neuro-
vascular assessment, postoperative monitoring, pain management, and
mobility  planning—tasks  that  require  systematic  reasoning  and  the
ability to synthesize multiple data points. Although CMAP can support
the  structuring  of  such  complex  information,  students  frequently
struggle  to  generate  comprehensive,  clinically  coherent  maps  (Binoy
and  Raddi,  2022).  Integrating  CMAP  with  AI-assisted  reflective  guid-
ance  may  therefore  offer  a  structured  mechanism  for  improving  stu-
dents’  analytical  processes  while  preserving  opportunities
for
independent judgement (Alavi and Okhovat, 2024).

This study investigated the use of an AI-assisted CMAP intervention
within an undergraduate orthopaedic nursing course. The purpose was
to  evaluate  whether  incorporating  AI-assisted  reflective  support  into
concept-mapping  activities  could  improve  students’  CT,  support  the
organization and integration of complex musculoskeletal clinical infor-
mation, and increase satisfaction with the learning experience. Findings
aim to inform the development of AI-enhanced pedagogical strategies
that  better  prepare  nursing  students  for  complex  clinical  decision-
making in orthopaedic and trauma care environments.

1.1. Design

A

two-group  quasi-experimental  pretest–posttest  design  was
employed  to  evaluate  the  effects  of  an  AI-assisted  concept  mapping
(CMAP) program on critical thinking (CT) in undergraduate orthopaedic
nursing  education.  This  design  was  selected  to  allow  comparison  be-
tween instructional approaches within an authentic educational setting
while  maintaining  feasibility  within  the  existing  academic  structure.
Participants were allocated to either the experimental or control group
according  to  the  study  implementation  schedule  within  the  course
setting. Both groups received identical foundational online instructional
materials;  however,  only  the  experimental  group  participated  in  an
additional structured AI-assisted workshop. Therefore, the intervention
should  be  interpreted  as  a  blended  AI-assisted  educational  approach
rather than the isolated effect of AI exposure alone.

To reduce scoring variability, all concept maps were independently
evaluated by two trained raters using standardized scoring criteria and
calibration procedures established prior to formal assessment. Scoring
discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

1.2. Participants

expected to apply musculoskeletal assessment knowledge and  clinical
reasoning  to  complex  patient  scenarios.  Sample  size  estimation  was
conducted using G*Power version 3.1. The calculation was based on a
significance  level  of  0.05,  statistical  power  of  0.90,  and  a  large,  esti-
mated effect size informed by previous educational intervention studies
examining concept mapping and critical thinking outcomes in nursing
education (e.g., Mohammadi et al., 2019). A minimum sample size of 50
participants  was  recommended;  therefore,  60  eligible  students  were
recruited to account for potential attrition and support stability of sta-
tistical analyses.

Eligibility criteria included prior exposure to basic concept mapping,
access to an internet-enabled device for AI-assisted activities, and will-
ingness to participate. Eligible students were informed about the study
during course orientation and classroom announcements. Students who
expressed  interest  received  detailed  study  information  and  provided
written  informed  consent  prior  to  participation.  Participation  was
voluntary,  and  students  were  informed  that  participation  or  non-
participation  would  not  affect  their  academic  evaluation  or  course
grades.  Students  were  allocated  to  an  experimental  group  (n  = 30)
receiving the AI-assisted CMAP intervention or a control group (n = 30)
receiving  standard  instruction.  Baseline  equivalence  between  groups
was established for demographic characteristics, academic performance,
and prior CMAP experience, ensuring comparability for evaluating the
effectiveness  of  the  intervention  within  the  orthopaedic  nursing  edu-
cation context.

1.3. The AI-assisted CMAP program

The  AI-assisted  concept  mapping  (CMAP)  program  was  developed
based on the conceptual framework of Novak and Gowin (1984) and was
designed  to  enhance  nursing  students’  critical  thinking  (CT)  through
structured concept map construction supported by AI-guided learning
activities.  The  program  integrated  principles  of  blended  learning,
reflective learning, and scaffolded cognitive support to facilitate clinical
reasoning in orthopaedic nursing education. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the
program consisted of three interconnected components: (1) knowledge
acquisition, (2) AI-assisted application, and (3) assessment with reflec-
tion. Program development was conducted through six sequential pha-
ses:  needs  assessment,  program  design,  AI  integration,  pilot  testing,
refinement, and implementation.

1.4. Knowledge acquisition phase

Students  in  both  the  experimental  and  control  groups  received
identical  online  instructional  materials  to  ensure  equivalent  founda-
tional learning exposure before concept map construction. The instruc-
tional  materials  included:  (1)  a  30-min  video  introducing  CMAP
principles  and  techniques,  and  (2)  a  60-min  video  covering  essential
orthopaedic nursing concepts, including fracture classification, patho-
physiology, neurovascular assessment, pain management, postoperative
monitoring,  mobility  planning,  and  prevention  of  common  complica-
tions.  The  materials  were  delivered  asynchronously  over  a  four-day
period, allowing flexible access while maintaining standardized prepa-
ration across participants.

1.5. AI-assisted application phase

The  AI-assisted  application  phase  was  implemented  only  in  the
experimental  group.  Students  participated  in  a  3-h  onsite  workshop
based  on  the  Blended  Learning  with  AI-Assisted  Concept  Mapping
(BLAI-CMAP)  model.  During  the  workshop,  students  constructed
concept  maps  individually  using  clinical  orthopaedic  case  scenarios
while interacting with ChatGPT (OpenAI) accessed through their per-
sonal digital devices.

enrolled in a required orthopaedic nursing course, where students were

reasoning processes.

To  reduce  variability  and  maintain  intervention  fidelity,  all  work-
shop  sessions  were  conducted  using  the  same  instructional  materials,
clinical  case  scenarios,  learning  objectives,  workshop  duration,  and
facilitator  guidance  procedures.  Faculty  facilitators  continuously
monitored  student  engagement  throughout  the  sessions  by  observing
concept map development, reviewing AI-assisted outputs, and prompt-
ing students to explain or justify conceptual relationships when neces-
sary.  Students  were  also  reminded  not  to  directly  copy  AI-generated
suggestions and were encouraged to critically evaluate all AI-supported
responses before incorporating them into their concept maps. Students
in the control group received only the online instructional materials and
completed concept map construction independently as part of routine
coursework  without  AI  assistance.  Both  groups  received  identical
foundational online instructional materials prior to concept map con-
struction. However, the experimental group additionally participated in
a  structured  AI-assisted  workshop  designed  to  provide  scaffolded
learning support and guided concept map development. Therefore, the
findings  should  be  interpreted  within  the  context  of  a  blended  AI-
assisted educational intervention rather than AI exposure alone.

1.6. Assessment and reflection phase

The  assessment  and  reflection  phase  was  implemented  for  both
groups. Critical thinking outcomes were evaluated using standardized
CMAP-based  assessments  administered  at  two  time  points:  pretest
(Week 1) and posttest (Week 3). Each assessment remained accessible
for 24 h and was completed individually without external assistance. In
addition,  students  in  the  experimental  group  completed  a  program
satisfaction questionnaire following completion of the intervention. An
overview of the study procedures and intervention flow is presented in
Fig. 2.

2. Data-collection instruments

2.1. CMAP-based critical thinking assessment tool

Critical thinking (CT) was assessed using a researcher-developed 12-
item  CMAP-based  evaluation  tool  grounded  in  Novak  and  Gowin's
framework  (1984).  The  instrument  consisted  of  two  dimensions:  (1)
Concept Map Structure (4 items assessing main concepts, sub-concepts,
logical flow, and symbolic representation) and (2) Case-Based Content
Accuracy  (8  items  evaluating  integration  of  orthopaedic  nursing
knowledge,  patient  data,  nursing  diagnoses,  interventions,  evidence-
based  practice,  and  use  of  appropriate  references).  Each  item  was
scored using a structured rubric ranging from 0 to 4, with higher scores
indicating greater completeness and accuracy. Total scores ranged from
0 to 68 and were interpreted as very low (0–13), low (14–27), moderate
(28–41),  high  (42–55),  and  very  high  (56–68)  CT  performance.  The

and access to digital devices.

3. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Descriptive statistics
were  used  to  summarize  demographic  characteristics  and  satisfaction
scores.  Baseline  equivalence  between  groups  was  assessed  using
independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests, as appropriate.

Prior to inferential analyses, assumptions for parametric testing were
evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests, Q–Q plots, boxplots, and Levene's
test.  Although  normality  tests  were  statistically  significant,  visual  in-
spection  indicated  no  severe  deviations  from  normality  or  extreme
outliers. Given the equal group sizes and the robustness of ANOVA to
moderate violations of normality, parametric analyses were considered
appropriate.  Missing  data  were  screened  prior  to  analysis,  and  no
missing values were identified.

To examine the effects of the intervention on critical thinking (CT)
scores,  a  two-way  mixed-design  ANOVA  was  conducted  with  time
(pretest-posttest) as the within-subject factor and group (experimental
vs. control) as the between-subject factor. Partial eta squared (η2p) was
reported as the measure of effect size.

Additionally,  an  analysis  of  covariance  (ANCOVA)  was  performed
using posttest CT scores as the dependent variable, group as the fixed

Ethical  approval  was  obtained  from  the  Human  Research  Ethics
Committee  of  Srinakharinwirot  University  (Protocol  code:  SWUEC-
681058).  Participation  was  voluntary,  and  written  informed  consent
was  obtained  from  all  participants.  Both  groups  received  identical
foundational online instructional materials prior to concept map con-
struction; however, the experimental group additionally participated in
a  structured  AI-assisted  workshop  designed  to  provide  scaffolded

responsible  AI  use,  students  were  instructed  not  to  enter  personally
identifiable information or real patient data into the AI platform. The AI

integrity  was  maintained  by  requiring  students  to  independently
construct  and  explain  their  concept  maps,  while  faculty  facilitators

5. Results

1)  Participant Characteristics

A total of 60 students participated in the study, with 30 assigned to
the  experimental  group  and  30  to  the  control  group.  Baseline  de-
mographic  and  academic  characteristics  including  age,  gender,  GPA,
and prior CMAP experience showed no significant differences between
groups  (p  > .05),  indicating  comparability  prior  to  the  intervention.
Details are shown in Table 1.

2)  Changes in Critical Thinking Scores

Critical  thinking  (CT)  scores  increased  in  both  groups  over  time;
however,  the  experimental  group  demonstrated  greater  improvement
following  participation  in  the  AI-assisted  concept  mapping  (CMAP)
program.  In  the  experimental  group,  mean  CT  scores  increased  from
2.40 ± 0.50 at pretest to 2.97 ± 0.72 at posttest. In the control group, CT
scores increased from 2.43 ± 0.68 to 2.57 ± 0.63.

A two-way mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant main effect
of time, F(1, 58) = 25.325, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.304, indicating that
CT  scores  improved  significantly  over  time  across  both  groups.  More
importantly, a significant interaction effect between time and group was
identified, F(1, 58) = 9.705, p = .003, partial η2 = 0.143, demonstrating
that  the  improvement  in  CT  scores  was  significantly  greater  in  the
experimental group than in the control group.

To further control for potential baseline differences, an ANCOVA was
conducted using pretest CT scores and prior concept mapping experi-
ence as covariates. The adjusted analysis showed that the experimental
group maintained significantly higher posttest CT scores compared with
the  control  group,  F(1,  56)  = 13.659,  p  < .001,  partial  η2  = 0.196.

Tables 2 and 3.

3)  Participant Satisfaction with the AI-Assisted CMAP Program

Participants  in  the  experimental  group  reported  high  satisfaction
with the AI-assisted CMAP program. The highest ratings were related to
understanding and concept mapping skills, including increased under-
standing of concept mapping (M = 4.58 ± 0.90) and improved concept
mapping skills (M = 4.56 ± 0.96). Participants also reported positive
perceptions regarding the use of AI tools and the application of knowl-
edge and skills in patient care activities. The overall satisfaction score
was high (M = 4.45 ± 1.00), indicating positive perceptions toward AI-
assisted learning and concept mapping activities. Detailed satisfaction
results are presented in Table 4.

6. Discussion

This  study  investigated  the  effectiveness  of  an  AI-assisted  concept
mapping (CMAP) program in promoting critical thinking (CT) among
undergraduate nursing students within an orthopaedic nursing course.
The  results  demonstrated  significant  CT  improvement  in  the  experi-
mental  group  and  higher  post-intervention  scores  compared  with  the
control  group,  suggesting  that  integrating  AI  scaffolding  with  CMAP
may support students' analytical reasoning and conceptual integration.
Students’ high satisfaction levels also indicate that the intervention was
well  accepted  and  perceived  as  beneficial  for  learning.  However,  the
observed  findings  should  be  interpreted  cautiously  because  the  inter-
vention incorporated not only AI-assisted support but also a structured
workshop  environment,  facilitator  guidance,  and  additional  opportu-
nities for reflective engagement. Therefore, the observed improvements
likely  reflect  the  combined  influence  of  a  blended  AI-assisted  educa-
tional approach rather than the isolated effect of AI exposure alone.

The observed improvement in CT can be interpreted through estab-
lished pedagogical theories. Concept mapping facilitates the hierarchi-
cal organization of knowledge and promotes meaningful integration of
new  information  with  prior  understanding,  consistent  with  Ausubel's
meaningful learning theory (1968). The AI-assisted reflective guidance
likely  strengthened  this  process  by  helping  students  refine  concept

9. Strengths and limitations

This study provides early empirical evidence on AI-assisted CMAP in
nursing  education,  grounded  in  established  learning  theories  and
implemented using a quasi-experimental two-group design. The use of a
validated  CT  rubric  and  calibrated  raters  strengthened  measurement
reliability, while satisfaction data offered insight into learners’ percep-
tions.  Limitations  include  a  relatively  small  sample  size,  single-
institution  setting,  and  short  intervention  duration,  which  restrict
generalizability and limit conclusions regarding long-term retention or
clinical applicability. Outcomes were also limited to cognitive gains and
self-reported satisfaction. In addition, because the study was conducted
within  a  single  academic  institution  and  course  context,  the  findings
may not be fully generalizable to other nursing programs, educational
environments,  or  student  populations  with  differing  levels  of  digital
literacy and prior learning experiences. Future work should incorporate
broader  samples,  extended  follow-up  periods,  and  performance-based
evaluations.

The findings of this study also complement emerging evidence on the
role  of  AI  in  supporting  metacognitive  processes,  adaptive  reasoning,
and structured learning (Han et al., 2025; Labrague and Al Sabei, 2024;
Srinivasan et al., 2024). These studies highlight the potential of AI to
function not as a substitute for human reasoning but as a scaffold that
reinforces  analytical  engagement—an  important  consideration  in  or-
thopaedic  nursing,  where  students  must  integrate  musculoskeletal
assessment data, pathophysiology, and clinical judgment. Despite these
potential  benefits,  integrating  AI  into  nursing  education  also  raises
important educational and ethical considerations. Potential risks include
overreliance  on  AI-generated  suggestions,  reduced  learner  autonomy,
inaccurate or hallucinated AI-generated content, and variability in stu-
dents' digital literacy and ability to critically evaluate AI-supported in-
formation.

7. Implications for practice

AI-assisted  concept  mapping  may  offer  educators  a  practical  and
scalable  approach  to  enhancing  CT  in  courses  requiring  complex
reasoning, including orthopaedic nursing. The AI component can help
students  clarify  conceptual  relationships,  structure  musculoskeletal
knowledge,  and  engage  more  deeply  with  analytical  tasks.  Educators
may  integrate  AI-supported  CMAP  activities  within  blended  learning
formats  while  maintaining  instructional  oversight  to  prevent  over-
reliance. This approach may support learning efficiency and provide a
structured pathway for developing higher-level reasoning skills.

8. Implications for research

Future research should involve larger and more diverse samples to
enhance  generalizability  and  adopt  longitudinal  designs  to  evaluate
retention  and  the  transferability  of  CT  skills  to  clinical  settings.
Performance-based  assessments—such  as  simulation  outcomes  or
structured  clinical  reasoning  evaluations—would  help  determine
whether  gains  in  CMAP-related  CT  translate  into  observable  practice

10. Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that AI-assisted concept mapping
(CMAP) may support the development of critical thinking and structured
clinical  reasoning  among  undergraduate  nursing  students  within  or-
thopaedic  nursing  education.  The  integration  of  AI-assisted  reflective
support  within  concept-mapping  activities  appeared  to  help  students
organize  and  integrate  complex  musculoskeletal  clinical  information
while engaging in analytical reasoning processes. Students also reported
positive  perceptions  toward  the  learning  experience.  However,  the
findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the intervention involved a
blended  educational  approach  that  included  structured  workshop  ac-
tivities,  facilitator  guidance,  and  reflective  learning  opportunities  in
addition to AI-assisted support. Furthermore, the study was conducted
within a single academic institution using a relatively short intervention
period,  which  may  limit  generalizability  and  conclusions  regarding
long-term  educational  impact.  Future  research  involving  larger  and
more diverse samples, longitudinal follow-up, and performance-based
clinical  outcomes  is  recommended  to  further  evaluate  the  role  of  AI-
assisted CMAP in nursing education.
