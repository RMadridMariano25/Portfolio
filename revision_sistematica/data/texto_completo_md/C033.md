Abstract
Despite growing interest in AI-enhanced instruction, the specific contribution of AI to the development of
critical thinking within flipped learning environments remains underexplored. This mixed-methods study
investigates the impact of an integrated AI-assisted flipped learning model on university students' critical
thinking skills, dispositions, and motivation. Over 10 weeks, 70 students were assigned to either an
experimental group using the Socrat.ai platform within a flipped-classroom framework or a control group
receiving traditional flipped learning without AI support. Quantitative data were collected using validated
scales for critical thinking skills, dispositions, and motivation; pre-test equivalence checks confirmed
comparability between groups, and post-test comparisons revealed greater improvements in the
experimental group across all three dimensions. Qualitative data from semi-structured interviews with nine
experimental-group students yielded four themes: AI-supported queSstioning as a catalyst for critical
thinking, enhanced motivation through personalized feedback, Simproved preparedness for in-class

INTRODUCTION A
Critical thinking is one of the most fundamental academic and professional competencies of the 21st
century, playing a crucial role in developing university students' innovative, analytical, and practical
problem-solving skills (Jintalan & Litao, 2024). It enables students to comprehend information and actively
engage in questioning, analyzing, interpreting, and inductive reasoning processes (Facione, 1990). Critical
thinking has been consistently identified as one of the key skills required for workforce readiness and
lifelong learning in major international assessments and world reports (OECD, 2019; Di Battista et al.,
2023). As emphasized in the 2023 Global Skills Report, critical thinking ranks among the top three most
essential skills for future careers (Coursera, 2023). However, employers consistently report significant gaps
in graduates' abilities to analyze complex problems and evaluate information critically, and global evidence
further confirms that university students often exhibit deficiencies in critical thinking skills (Al-Husban et
al., 2022; Din, 2020; Lodhi et al., 2023). For instance, Lodhi et al. (2023) reported that more than 50% of
university students demonstrated poor performance in inference and inductive reasoning skills. Similar
findings were reported in the International Assessment of Higher Education Learning Outcomes, indicating
that many university graduates lack the critical thinking skills required for success in knowledge-based
economies (OECD, 2023). Such deficiencies significantly impact students' academic achievement,
problem-solving abilities, and future employability (Li & Cui, 2025; Yurt, 2025). These findings underscore
the necessity of effective pedagogical interventions to enhance students' critical thinking competencies.

While traditional pedagogical approaches often fail to develop higher-order thinking skills, the flipped
learning model has emerged as a promising instructional strategy. In this model, students acquire
foundational content individually before class, while classroom time is dedicated to interactive activities
that promote deeper learning and critical thinking (Bergmann & Sams, 2015). Research indicates that
flipped learning can positively influence cognitive development and student engagement (Birgili et al.,
2021; Kong et al., 2024). However, implementation challenges such as instructor workload, student
motivation issues, and lack of real-time guidance have limited its effectiveness (Akçayır & Akçayır, 2018;
Ng & Lo, 2022).
Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) present unprecedented opportunities to address these
challenges while enhancing the pedagogical effectiveness of flipped learning. AI-powered systems can
provide automated content curation, personalized learning pathways, real-time student support through
intelligent chatbots, and adaptive feedback mechanisms (Huang et al., 2023a; Huesca et al., 2024; Şanlı,
2025). These technological capabilities address traditional implementation barriers while potentially
creating more sophisticated learning environments that support higher-order cognitive development. From
a theoretical perspective, integrating AI into flipped learning represents a synthesis of constructivist
pedagogical principles with adaptive learning technologies, creating what Siemens (2005) described as
"connectivist" learning networks that respond dynamically to individual student needs.

Critical Thinking: Conceptual Framework and Development
Critical thinking is the ability to systematically analyze, evaluate, synthesize, and reflect on information to
make decisions and solve problems (Facione, 1990). This definition emphasizes that critical thinking is a
set of skills focusing on how individuals process information. However, critical thinking is not merely a
cognitive process; it is also linked to individuals' willingness and habitual inclination to use these skills. In
this regard, Facione (1990) conceptualizes critical thinking as comprising skills and dispositions,
highlighting its multidimensional nature. While skills include cognitive processes such as analysis,
inference, and evaluation, dispositions encompass attitudinal factors such as open-mindedness, curiosity,
and systematic thinking. In line with this multidimensional view, the present study employed the Istanbul
Critical Thinking Scale (ICTS) to measure skills (Aykaç & Aslan, 2022) and the Critical Thinking
Disposition Scale (CTD) to assess dispositions (Sosu, 2013). The ICTS operationalizes critical thinking
skills through three interrelated cognitive dimensions, interpretation, evaluation, and inference, while the
CTD captures dispositional tendencies across Critical Openness and Reflective Skepticism.

Paul and Elder (2006) further contextualize critical thinking within a philosophical framework, defining it
as "thinking about thinking to improve thinking"—a process guided by intellectual standards (clarity,
accuracy, consistency) and intellectual virtues (humility, empathy, courage). This perspective emphasizes
that critical thinking encompasses technical cognitive abilities and ethical dimensions related to character
development. From an empirical standpoint, the ICTS reflects this cognitive-technical emphasis by
assessing how effectively students can interpret, evaluate, and infer from given information, whereas the
CTD addresses the attitudinal and ethical orientation toward engaging in critical inquiry.
Complementing these cognitive and dispositional elements, Valenzuela et al. (2023) examine critical
thinking motivation within the Expectancy-Value Theory framework, positing that individuals' engagement
in critical thinking activities depends on both their belief in their ability to perform these activities
successfully (expectancy) and the value they attribute to these activities. Accordingly, the present study
employed the Critical Thinking Motivation Scale (CTM), which evaluates expectancy beliefs alongside
value-related components such as attainment, utility, intrinsic interest, and perceived cost (Valenzuela et
al., 2011). This approach allows for a more comprehensive assessment of why students engage, or fail to
engage, in critical thinking processes, extending the framework beyond skills and dispositions.
Research demonstrates that critical thinking skills can be developed through specific educational
interventions. Meta-analyses by Abrami et al. (2015) and Huber and Kuncel (2016) show that explicit
S
instruction in critical thinking, combined with opportunities for application and feedback, yields significant
S
improvements in students' analytical abilities. The developmenEt of critical thinking requires educational
environments that actively engage students in questioning, Ranalyzing, and evaluating information rather
than passively receiving content (Jintalan & Litao, 2024).P By integrating skills (ICTS), dispositions (CTD),
and motivation (CTM), the present study adoptsN a multidimensional framework that reflects both the
theoretical complexity of critical thinking and itsI practical assessment through validated instruments.

It should be noted that skills, dispositioLns, and motivation are treated here as interconnected rather than
wholly independent constructs. CFrom an argumentation-theory perspective, critical thinking is
I
fundamentally a justificatory practice: individuals construct, evaluate, and revise claims through reasoned
T
support, counter-argumenRt, and qualification (Toulmin, 2003), and the strength of this argumentative
practice is shaped by leAarners' epistemic beliefs (Banihashem et al., 2024). In this framework, skills supply
the cognitive operations required for argumentation (interpreting evidence, generating and evaluating
counter-claims, drawing warranted inferences); dispositions determine the learner's willingness to engage
these operations rather than defaulting to unexamined acceptance; and motivation governs the persistence
with which engagement is sustained over time. The present study's three measures can therefore be read as
complementary facets of a single argumentative competence rather than as three unrelated outcomes. It is
also worth distinguishing the cognitive operations involved: Guilford's (1967) classic distinction between
divergent thinking (generating multiple candidate interpretations) and convergent thinking (narrowing these
to a single, defensible conclusion) maps loosely onto the ICTS's interpretation/evaluation and inference
dimensions, respectively, and AI-mediated Socratic questioning may differentially stimulate one process
over the other—a possibility we return to in the Discussion. Finally, what constitutes adequate critical
thinking is not discipline-neutral: evidentiary standards and argumentative norms differ across academic
fields (Moore, 2013), and because the present sample was drawn from an elective critical-thinking course
in a Faculty of Education, the findings should not be assumed to generalize to disciplines with different
epistemic cultures (e.g., the natural sciences).
Flipped Learning and AI Integration: Addressing Implementation Challenges

Flipped learning represents a pedagogical approach in which traditional content delivery is shifted to pre-
class activities, while classroom time is dedicated to interactive learning experiences promoting higher-
order thinking (Bergmann & Sams, 2012). Grounded in constructivist learning theory, this model aligns
lower-order cognitive tasks (remembering, understanding) with pre-class activities while emphasizing
higher-order tasks (analyzing, evaluating, creating) during in-class sessions (Anderson et al., 2001).
Research demonstrates that flipped learning can enhance student engagement and academic performance,
with 93% of studies reporting improvements in at least one dimension of student engagement (Bond, 2020).
However, traditional flipped learning implementation faces significant challenges that limit its
effectiveness. These include increased instructor workload in content preparation, student motivation issues
for pre-class activities, lack of real-time guidance during independent study, technological resource
limitations, and resistance to paradigm shifts (Akçayır & Akçayır, 2018; Ng & Lo, 2022). Research
indicates that these barriers can significantly impact the model's potential for fostering higher-order
thinking skills.
Implementing the flipped learning model presents multifaceted challenges that can significantly undermine
its effectiveness. One of the primary difficulties is the substantially increased time demand and workload
placed on instructors. Studies by Hao et al. (2016) and Zou et al. (2020) indicate that instructors often
struggle to find suitable online resources, and producing their materials is highly time-consuming. This
S
time burden is further compounded by the need for continuous content updates and mastering video
S
production tools. As a result, instructors may focus on preparingE only specific sections of the course rather
than the entire curriculum or seek assistance from teaching aRssistants (Buil-Fabregá et al., 2019; Sevillano-
Monje et al., 2022). P
N
Student motivation and engagement represent a further challenge: low participation in pre-class activities
I
and resistance to self-directed learning can und ermine the model’s effectiveness (Ng & Lo, 2022; Baig &
E
Yadegaridehkordi, 2023). The absence oLf real-time guidance outside the classroom compounds this issue,
as unanswered pre-class questions crCeate knowledge gaps that hinder in-class discussion (Fraga & Harmon,
I
2015; Li & Li, 2022). Content quality is also critical: excessively long or poorly produced videos reduce
T
engagement, with researchR recommending a maximum of 5–10 minutes per video (Murillo-Zamorano et
al., 2019; Birgili & DeAmir, 2022). Finally, technological resource limitations and resistance to paradigm
shifts remain barriers to effective implementation (Bachiller & Badía, 2020; Akçayır & Akçayır, 2018;
Bond, 2020). Addressing these challenges systematically is crucial to realising the full potential of flipped
learning. The following section considers how AI may provide targeted solutions to each of these barriers.
Recent technological advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have been proposed as potential solutions
to address these implementation challenges. AI-driven content curation systems can reduce instructor
workload through automated video editing and intelligent content recommendation algorithms (Huang et
al., 2023a), functioning as AI teaching assistants that analyze existing digital resources and assist instructors
in content curation. AI-supported gamification and personalized feedback systems may enhance student
motivation for pre-class activities by continuously monitoring performance and providing tailored reward
systems (Yıldız et al., 2022). Intelligent chatbots and virtual assistants can provide real-time guidance
during independent study phases, potentially addressing the critical gap in outside-classroom support
(Huesca et al., 2024; Alan & Yurt, 2024). Additionally, AI-based video analysis tools can optimize
instructional materials by assessing student engagement levels and adjusting video content structure to
prevent cognitive overload (Kazanidis et al., 2018; Baig & Yadegaridehkordi, 2023).
From a theoretical perspective, the potential integration of AI into flipped learning represents a fundamental
transformation in educational practice that transcends mere task automation. The integration embodies what

Kaplan and Haenlein (2019) describe as "cognitive partnerships" between humans and AI, where
technology serves as cognitive scaffolding that supports higher-order thinking development. This approach
aligns with Clark's (2005) concept of "extended mind" tools that expand students' intellectual capacities
through personalized cognitive support. Furthermore, the connection between AI-assisted flipped learning
and critical thinking development is theoretically grounded in Siemens' (2005) and Downes' (2012)
connectivism framework, which positions AI as an adaptive node in students' learning networks. In this
framework, AI provides personalized guidance that helps students navigate complex information
environments and develop evaluative skills essential for critical thinking (López-Villanueva et al., 2024).
These three theoretical lenses, constructivism, connectivism, and Expectancy-Value Theory, are not merely
juxtaposed in the present study but are intended to operate at different levels of a single explanatory model.
Constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978) explains the proximal mechanism: Socrat.ai's Socratic Dialogue and
Built-In Memory functions operate as a form of dynamic scaffolding, withdrawing or adjusting support as
a student's reasoning develops, consistent with research on AI-mediated scaffolding for self-regulated
learning (SRL) and, in the in-class collaborative phase, socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL),
whereby groups jointly monitor and adjust their reasoning strategies (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013).
Connectivism (Siemens, 2005) explains the distal, network-level mechanism: the AI functions as one node
among instructors, peers, and digital resources, and the longitudinal-memory feature extends this network
across time rather than only across actors. Expectancy-Value Theory (VSalenzuela et al., 2011) explains the
motivational mechanism linking the first two: scaffolded success (conSstructivism) raises expectancy beliefs,

Research Gap and Study Objectives
I
T
Despite the theoretical potRential of combining AI technologies with flipped learning (AAFL), research has
not yet established wheAther the integration of AI specifically enhances critical thinking beyond what flipped
learning methodology alone provides. Current studies have primarily focused on either flipped learning
effectiveness in general (Bergmann & Sams, 2015; Bond, 2020) or standalone AI-powered learning systems
(Huang et al., 2023a; Chiu et al., 2024; Huesca et al., 2024), with limited empirical investigation into the
distinct and combined contributions of these approaches to critical thinking development. This represents
a crucial distinction in research design, as attributing improvements to "AI-assisted flipped learning"
without a control group experiencing traditional flipped learning creates ambiguity regarding which
intervention component drives the observed effects.
The complex nature of critical thinking, encompassing cognitive skills, dispositional tendencies, and
motivational components (Ennis, 1996; Facione, 1990; Valenzuela et al., 2011), requires targeted
investigation using appropriately structured comparison groups to understand how specific technological
enhancements affect critical thinking development. While preliminary evidence suggests that both flipped
learning and AI integration can enhance student outcomes individually, the specific added value of
combining these approaches, and the particular mechanisms through which AI technologies contribute to
critical thinking enhancement, remain empirically underexplored.
This study addresses this gap by employing a rigorous randomized design with two comparison conditions:
(1) AI-assisted flipped learning and (2) traditional flipped learning. This methodological approach isolates

of the flipped learning model itself. By comparing these two treatment groups, the study can determine
whether improvements in critical thinking skills, dispositions, and motivation result specifically from AI-
enhanced features such as adaptive feedback, personalized content delivery, and intelligent support
mechanisms, or whether the flipped learning methodology alone accounts for observed gains.

premises, conclusions, and potential fallacies in peer-generated and published texts. Week 8 (Critical

critically evaluated each other's thinking processes and provided feedback on reasoning strategies.
Socrat.ai played a crucial role in individualized learning by adapting discussion prompts to students'
responses, encouraging them to ask more profound questions, and providing AI-driven guidance on
reasoning and argumentation. More specifically, the platform operationalized critical thinking support
through three identifiable cognitive mechanisms. First, Socratic Dialogue sessions involved the AI
generating open-ended, probing questions tailored to each student's prior response—for instance, when a
student offered a surface-level interpretation of a cognitive bias scenario, the system would counter with a

Istanbul Critical Thinking Scale (ICTS)
The ICTS, developed by Aykaç and Aslan (2022), is a 25-item skill test designed for university students to
measure critical thinking abilities through three cognitive dimensions: interpretation (7 items), evaluation
(11 items), and inference (7 items). The test yields scores ranging from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating
stronger critical thinking abilities. The scale consists of various paragraphs and statements, each offering
three response options where only one is correct. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the
three-dimensional structure of the scale, with standardized path coefficients ranging from 0.310 to 0.641,
S
all being statistically significant. The model fit indices indicated gooSd fit: χ²/df = 1.776, CFI = 0.966, NNFI
= 0.962, GFI = 0.966, RMSEA = 0.030. The original scale demEonstrated a stratified alpha coefficient of

0.676 (Aykaç & Aslan, 2022). The present study calculated Cronbach's alpha coefficient as 0.728 (n = 140),
P
which exceeds the conventional minimum threshold of .70 but remains modest for a 25-item cognitive test.

differential, sub-dimension-specific effects.
Critical Thinking Disposition (CTD)
The Critical Thinking Disposition Scale (CTD) was developed by Sosu (2013) for university students. The
scale consists of 11 items and comprises two dimensions: Critical Openness and Reflective Skepticism.
Critical Openness reflects individuals' tendency to actively engage with different perspectives and ideas.
At the same time, Reflective Skepticism represents the disposition to question evidence and evaluate claims
before accepting them. A sample item from the scale is "I often re-evaluate my experiences so that I can
learn from them." The scale employs a self-report format with a 5-point Likert-type response scale ranging
from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Total scores range from 11 to 55, with higher scores
indicating stronger critical thinking dispositions.

Regarding the psychometric properties of CTD, Sosu (2013) conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to

structure of the scale (CFI = .93, TLI = .91, RMSEA = .056, and SRMR = .055). Factor loadings ranged
from 0.44 to 0.66, all statistically significant at the p < .001 level. The original study reported a Cronbach's
alpha coefficient of .81, indicating good internal consistency. In the present study, the scale demonstrated
acceptable reliability with an alpha coefficient of .78 (n = 140).
Critical Thinking Motivation (CTM)
The Critical Thinking Motivation Scale (CTM) was developed by Valenzuela et al. (2011) for university
students. This 19-item scale is grounded in the expectancy value theory. It aims to measure motivation
toward critical thinking through two main components: expectancy and value. The expectancy component
assesses individuals' competency expectations regarding critical thinking. In contrast, the value component
encompasses various aspects, including attainment, utility, intrinsic value, and cost considerations.
Representative items include "Concerning reasoning correctly, I am better than most of my peers"
(Expectancy) and "Thinking critically will help me to be a good professional" (Utility). The scale employs
a 6-point response format ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 6 (totally agree), with total scores ranging
from 19 to 114. Higher scores indicate greater motivation toward criticaSl thinking.

DISCUSSION
This study aimed to examine the effects of the AI-Assisted Flipped Learning (AAFL) model on university
students' critical thinking skills, dispositions, and motivation. The research was conducted with volunteer
students enrolled in the “Critical and Analytical Thinking” course at a public university in the Marmara
region of Turkey. A randomized pretest-posttest control-group design was employed, where the
experimental group received instruction through a Socrat.ai-based AI-assisted flipped learning approach.
In contrast, the control group received instruction through a standard flipped learning approach without AI
support.
Data were collected using validated and reliable scales measuring critical thinking skills, dispositions, and
motivation. In the analysis, one-way MANOVA was conducted to confirm pre-test group equivalence,
followed by independent samples t-tests to compare post-test outcomes between the two groups.
Additionally, paired-sample t-tests were conducted within each group to examine pre-to-post changes.
Qualitative semi-structured interviews with nine experimental group students provided process-level
insights that contextualise and explain the quantitative outcomes. The mSain findings indicate that students
S
in the experimental group exhibited significantly improved cognitive processes, critical thinking
E

critical thinking development by facilitating persoNnalized learning, interactive engagement, and adaptive

A key methodological strength of this study is the use of a control group that received flipped learning

This study makes a significant theoretical contribution by providing one of the first controlled comparisons
of AI-assisted flipped learning against flipped learning alone, offering a more precise analysis of how AI
assistance, beyond the flipped structure itself, influences critical thinking development across three
interconnected dimensions.
A key aspect of this study is its exploration of critical thinking processes across three interconnected
dimensions. The cognitive dimension examines how students interpret, evaluate, and infer information,

perspectives and tendency to question claims critically, highlighting their willingness to engage in reflective

critically and their recognition of its importance in academic and professional contexts.
Our findings contribute to existing theoretical frameworks by demonstrating how the AI component of AI-

By integrating Vygotsky's (1978) social constructivism, Facione's (1990) conceptualization of critical

critical thinking motivation, we offer a more comprehensive understanding of how AI-specific
technological interventions can enhance critical thinking development within a flipped learning context.
This integrated theoretical perspective provides a framework for understanding not only what critical
thinking skills are developed but also why students might be more motivated to engage in critical thinking
processes and how their dispositions toward critical thinking can be cultivated.
By analyzing these specific components, this study offers a more precise understanding of how AI-driven
tools support critical thinking development. The personalized learning experiences and real-time feedback
mechanisms provided by Socrat.ai have enabled a detailed assessment of improvements in critical thinking,

Limitations
The redesigned study addresses the most fundamental limitation of the original design, the inability to
disentangle the effects of AI assistance from those of flipped learning, by employing a control group that
also received flipped learning without AI support. This design change substantially strengthens the causal
claims regarding AI's contribution to critical thinking outcomes. Nevertheless, several limitations remain.
Although several covariates, such as gender and cumulative GPA, were included in sensitivity analyses to
control for potential confounding variables, the influence of other unmeasured factors (e.g., students'
learning styles, technology acceptance, and attitudes toward AI in education) remains unknown. Future
research should incorporate these variables to understand better the mechanisms influencing critical
thinking development. Relatedly, individual differences in students' epistemic beliefs, their assumptions
about the nature and justification of knowledge, have been shown to shape how students engage in
argumentation and reasoning tasks, including gendered patterns of engagement (Banihashem et al., 2024).
Because epistemic beliefs were not assessed in the present study, we cannot determine whether students

ACCAAERRPTTTIICCEDLLEE M IIANNN PPURRSEECSSRSSIPT
with more sophisticated epistemic beliefs engaged more productively with Socrat.ai's Socratic prompts, or
whether the AI-assisted condition was differentially effective across students with different baseline
orientations toward knowledge and authority. This is a meaningful gap, given that 23 of 35 experimental-
group participants were female; future studies should assess epistemic beliefs and AI literacy as potential
moderators of gains in AI-assisted critical thinking. Additionally, some students encountered technical
difficulties and adaptation challenges during the initial weeks of using the Socrat.ai platform. These issues
may have influenced their engagement levels and learning outcomes, particularly during the early
intervention phase. Ensuring more comprehensive technical orientation and support in future
implementations may help mitigate such effects.
Another limitation concerns the potential novelty effect, whereby students' increased engagement may
partly reflect the appeal of using new and interactive technology rather than the pedagogical value of the
intervention itself. While the use of an active control group (flipped learning only) reduces, but does not
eliminate, this concern, future research could further address it through longer-term implementations or
staggered rollouts to differentiate between short-term enthusiasm and genuine pedagogical impact.
Two further design features constrain how specifically the observed gains can be attributed to AI per se.
First, both groups were taught by the same instructor within sections of the same course; while this held
S
instructional quality constant across conditions, the findings have not yet been replicated across instructors
S
or course sections, and instructor-specific effects cannot be fully ruled out. Second, time on task during pre-
E
class preparation was not measured or equated between gRroups: Socrat.ai's interactive, conversational
format may have led experimental-group students to spenPd more time engaging with pre-class material than
control-group students working with static digital Nmaterials. Because engagement time was not recorded,
we cannot fully disentangle the effect of AI-sIpecific features (e.g., adaptive questioning, longitudinal
memory) from the more general effect of iEncreased time on task. Future replications should log pre-class
L
engagement time across conditions Cand involve multiple instructors to address these limitations. A further
constraint on generalizability, disItinct from the sample-size limitation discussed below, is that the study
T
was conducted in a single elective course at one institution, with a self-selected population of students who
R
chose to enroll in a criAtical-thinking course; the findings may not generalize to compulsory courses, other
disciplines, or institutions with different student populations. Relatedly, although the AI-assisted condition
was designed to differ from the control condition primarily in the presence of AI-generated questioning,
feedback frequency, and the degree of accountability students felt for completing pre-class work, these
factors may also have differed between conditions. For example, Socrat.ai's interactive format may have
created a stronger sense of being "checked on" than the control group's static materials did. These factors
were not measured or equated between groups and cannot be fully disentangled from the AI-specific
features themselves.
A further limitation concerns the unexamined risk that AI-mediated dialogue could, under different
conditions, undermine rather than support critical thinking. Although the present qualitative findings
describe Socrat.ai as prompting deeper reasoning, a growing body of evidence cautions that generative AI
use can also produce "metacognitive laziness", a tendency to offload cognitive effort onto the AI system
rather than engage in independent reasoning, which has been associated with reduced learning gains when
AI support is not carefully structured (Fan et al., 2025). Similarly, Darvishi et al. (2024) found that AI
assistance can, in some implementations, diminish students' sense of agency over their learning. The present
design, in which Socrat.ai's role was tightly scoped to pre-class Socratic questioning rather than answer
generation, and in which in-class activities required students to apply their reasoning without AI support,

was intended to mitigate this risk. However, the study did not directly measure cognitive offloading,
overreliance, or agency. Future research should incorporate validated measures of these constructs to
determine the conditions under which AI-assisted flipped learning supports, rather than substitutes for,
independent critical thinking. Relatedly, students were not explicitly warned during orientation that
Socrat.ai's questions or feedback could themselves be inaccurate or misleading. This omission may help
explain the qualitative observation that students did not spontaneously question the substantive accuracy of
the AI's prompts (see Qualitative Findings). Future implementations should include explicit guidance on
the possibility of AI error as a safeguard against overreliance and unexamined trust in AI-generated content.
A related limitation concerns the relatively small sample size (n = 70, 35 per group). Although a priori
power analysis confirmed adequate statistical power for the primary analyses, the modest sample limits the
generalizability of findings to broader student populations and restricts the power available for subgroup
analyses. Future research should seek to replicate these findings with larger, more diverse samples and
across multiple institutions. Equally important, the ten-week intervention window and single post-test
measurement point mean that this study can speak only to short-term gains; whether these improvements
in critical thinking skills, dispositions, and motivation persist beyond the course or transfer to critical
thinking tasks in other courses or to authentic, non-classroom problem-solving contexts remains untested.
A delayed follow-up post-test and a transfer task administered outside tShe AAFL course would be needed
to establish that the observed gains reflect durable cognitive changeS rather than short-term, context-bound

performance. Additionally, although platform interaction data from Socrat.ai (including session duration

Conclusion
This study provides evidence that AI-assisted flipped learning is associated with greater improvements in
university students' critical thinking skills, dispositions, and motivation than flipped learning without AI
support. By employing an active control group that also experienced flipped learning, the design offers
more direct evidence than many prior studies that these gains are more plausibly attributable to the AI-
assisted component than to the flipped structure alone, while acknowledging, as discussed in the
Limitations, that factors such as differential time-on-task cannot be fully ruled out.
These findings suggest that educators who have already adopted flipped learning may find AI-assisted pre-
class scaffolding, such as Socratic questioning and personalized feedback, a worthwhile addition for
strengthening critical thinking outcomes. However, given the study's reliance on a single course, institution,
and ten-week window, these implications should be treated as provisional. Future research involving
multiple institutions, longer follow-up periods, and a fuller multi-group design (incorporating AI-only and
non-flipped conditions) is needed to establish the generalisability and durability of these effects.
