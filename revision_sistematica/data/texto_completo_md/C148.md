The swift rise of generative AI in higher education is transforming how students complete academic assignments.
In Indonesia's higher education sector, where AI adoption is growing faster than formal guidance, understanding
this transformation is particularly critical for safeguarding academic integrity and learning quality. This study
seeks to (1) describe patterns of generative AI utilization, (2) explore student views and experiences, and (3)
identify implications for higher education in Indonesia. The study employed an exploratory qualitative approach,
involving  131  students  from  a  university  in  Jakarta—encompassing  undergraduate,  master's,  and  doctorate
programs—who composed reflective essays regarding their experiences with AI. Employing Salda˜na's two-cycle
coding method, three primary themes were identified: AI as an academic assistance tool, AI for academic skill
development, and AI for learning effectiveness. Students predominantly used AI for rapid information retrieval
and  summarization,  improving  their  academic  writing  (e.g.,  refining  structure,  clarity,  and  grammar),  and
supporting  their  study  practices  (e.g.,  generating  practice  quizzes  and  organizing  study  plans).  Although  AI
provided  enhanced  efficiency  and  accessibility,  apprehensions  emerged  regarding  excessive  dependence,
diminished critical thinking, and ethical dilemmas pertaining to academic integrity. These findings underscore
the significance of AI literacy initiatives to assist students in the responsible integration of AI tools while pre-
serving cognitive engagement and originality. The study is confined to a single institution and depends on self-
reported  reflective  essays,  indicating  the  need  for  careful  interpretation  of  the  findings.  As  one  of  the  first
qualitative studies on this topic in Indonesia, this research addresses a critical empirical and policy gap by of-
fering  context-specific evidence on AI's  educational role and by outlining a balanced integration pathway for
Indonesian higher education institutions. Future research ought to investigate cross-institutional comparisons,
the long-term effects of AI adoption, and educators' viewpoints regarding its function in academic settings.

1. Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, especially with the
rise  of  generative  AI,  has  significantly  influenced  higher  education
(AlDhaen, 2022; Marengo et al., 2024; Rangel-de Lazaro & Duart, 2023).
Students can now engage in simulated arguments, reinterpret materials,
produce rigorous academic writing, and summarize books with widely
accessible technology such as ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, Claude, and
Bard.  These  AI-powered  tools,  progressively  integrated  into  everyday
learning  activities,  are  transforming  student  engagement  with  course
completion, research, and academic resources (Chen et al., 2022; Pham
&  Sampson,  2022;  Rangel-de  Lazaro  &  Duart,  2023).  Institutions  are
meticulously examining their educational frameworks and assessment
methods  considering  these  advancements  to  ensure  that  academic
excellence  and  integrity  align  with  the  benefits  of  technology

(Al-Zahrani & Alasmari, 2024; Kuleto et al., 2021a; Wang et al., 2023;
Zouhaier, 2023).

Globally, the integration of generative AI into higher education has
reshaped learning practices, creating both opportunities and challenges.
It  offers  significant  advantages  by  enhancing  learning  efficiency,
personalizing  study  assistance,  and  expanding  access  to  academic  re-
sources  (Humble  &  Mozelius,  2022;  Yan  et  al.,  2024).  Students  can
analyze vast quantities of data rapidly, receive immediate feedback, and
enhance their work using AI-generated suggestions (Aljuaid, 2024; Song
&  Song, 2023). These  tools  have transformed traditional learning ap-
proaches, enhancing flexibility and  involvement in the  process.  How-
ever, the increasing utilization of artificial intelligence in academia also
raises  concerns  regarding  academic  integrity,  ethical  considerations,
and the authenticity of student work (Khatri & Karki, 2023; Vera et al.,
2024).  Teachers  assessing  students'  unique  contributions  have

1.1. The use of generative AI in Indonesian higher education

In Indonesia, the integration of generative AI intersects with AI lit-
eracy  challenges,  higher  education  policy,  and  national  digital  trans-
formation goals. Adoption of artificial intelligence in higher education is
also accelerating in Indonesia, especially among urban university stu-
dents  who have more access to digital technologies  (Helmiatin et  al.,
2024;  Nagy  et  al., 2024;  Utami  &  Winarni,  2023).  Research,  writing,
exam preparation, and problem-solving are just a few of the applications
for  AI-powered  products  growingly  common.  Policies  and  organized
direction lacking, nevertheless, control over how artificial intelligence
should be included into academic activity. While some Indonesian uni-
versities support artificial intelligence as a complementing learning tool,
others voice worries about the validity of students’ academic output and
the  possibility  of  plagiarism  produced  by  AI  (Helmiatin  et  al.,  2024;
Yusriadi et al., 2023). Lack of explicit AI literacy initiatives forces stu-
dents to negotiate the ethical and pragmatic usage of artificial intelli-
gence on their own, mostly depending on informal information and peer
discussions instead of official policiess (Utami & Winarni, 2023).

In some countries, universities have already started weaving artifi-
cial intelligence into their curriculum in a structured way. In Indonesia,
however, many higher education institutions are still figuring out just
how best to integrate generative AI into the learning process (Helmiatin
et  al., 2024; Mulyani et  al., 2025). This situation raises several inter-
esting questions. For instance, are Indonesian students mainly using AI
as a helpful tool to understand lecture material better, or is it being used
to boost their writing skills and streamline research? There is also some
concern that AI might tempt students to take shortcuts with assignments
instead of engaging deeply with the work themselves. Moreover, it is
worth asking how students actually feel about the impact of AI on their
studies  and  which  measures  they  put  in  place  to  use  these  tools
responsibly. These questions are guiding discussions among educators
and policymakers as they seek to strike a balance between innovation
and academic integrity (Al-Zahrani &  Alasmari, 2024; Marengo et al.,
2024; Zaim et al., 2024).

Indonesia. We investigate how students incorporate AI into their cour-
sework, how AI influences their academic behavior, and what challenges
arise in balancing AI's benefits with the need for independent learning.
By  examining  these  issues  in  a  rapidly  changing  but  under-
researched national context, this study generates Indonesia-specific ev-
idence that can inform AI literacy programmes, institutional regulations,
and national higher-education policy. The findings are therefore inten-
ded not only to describe current student practices, but also to provide a
conceptual and empirical basis for guiding responsible AI integration in
Indonesian universities.

2. Methods

This  study  adopts  a  qualitative  exploratory  design  to  look  at  how
university  students  in  Indonesia  are  using  generative  AI  in  their  aca-
demic  work.  Because  this  is  a  new  phenomenon,  the  qualitative
approach is well suited for uncovering the deeper, personal experiences
and  opinions  of  the  students  involved  (Creswell  &  Poth,  2016).  This
approach helps us identify key trends, motivations, and challenges that
arise as students integrate AI into their studies (Tisdell et al., 2025).

Choosing a qualitative inquiry was a deliberate decision, driven by
the interest in truly understanding the lived experiences of students with
AI.  Listening  to  their  stories  and  perspectives  reveals  patterns  and
themes  that  might  otherwise  be  overlooked  by  structured  surveys  or
experimental methods (Braun & Clarke, 2012).

Data  were  collected  in  October  2024  during  a  workshop  held  on
campus  as  part  of  a  Visiting  Professor  Public  Lecture  Program.  The
theme  of  the  public  lecture  was  “The  Use  of  Artificial  Intelligence  in
Academic Writing,” which was open to all students from undergraduate
to doctoral levels. The hosting university in Jakarta also served as the
research site. Participation in the workshop was voluntary, and students
who attended were invited to submit reflective essays describing their
experiences  with  AI.  The  voluntary  and  open  nature  of  participation
ensured the process was ethically sound, with no academic penalty or
benefit  tied  to  participation.  The  study  obtained  approval  from  the
organizing institution, and all participants gave informed consent.

2.2. Data collection: reflective essay

2.5. Ethical considerations

Purposive  sampling  was  employed  to  select  participants  based  on
their enrollment in higher education (undergraduate to doctoral) and
their interest or experience with AI tools.

The essay assignment requested students to articulate their experi-
ences  with  generative  AI  in  education,  highlighting  its  perceived  ad-
vantages and limitations, as well as any ethical or academic integrity
issues encountered. For clarity, the exact prompt given to participants
(translated  from  Bahasa  Indonesia)  was:  ‘Write  a  reflective  essay  of
600–1000  words  about  your  experiences  using  generative  AI  in  your
academic work. Describe how you have used AI tools in your studies, the
benefits or advantages you observed, any challenges or drawbacks you
faced (including issues of academic integrity or ethics), and how using
AI has affected your learning or academic work.’ Essays were required to
be  between  600  and  1000  words.  All  essays  were  written  in  Bahasa
Indonesia; the responses were subsequently translated into English for
analysis, with careful attention to preserving the original meaning and
student voice.

2.3. Data processing and organization

After collecting the reflective essays, the replies were meticulously
transcribed,  formatted,  and  organized  into  a  systematic  dataset.  Only
minimal grammatical edits were implemented to improve readability,
preserving the original voice and intent of each work.

2.4. Data Analysis: thematic analysis using Salda˜na's two-cycle coding

Analysis  followed  Salda˜na's  two-cycle  coding  (descriptive  then
inductive thematic coding), conducted manually using Microsoft Excel.
In the first stage, descriptive coding was applied to identify explicit
references to AI tools, purposes of use, and perceived outcomes. In the
second  stage,  inductive  thematic  coding  grouped  codes  into  broader
themes.

The workshop format and voluntary participation ensured that no
student was coerced into the study. Written informed consent was ob-
tained  before  data  collection.  Participants  were  assured  that  their  re-
sponses  would  remain  anonymous  and  that  their  decision  to
participate—or not—would have no impact on their academic standing.
This study was reviewed and approved by the Center for Research and
Publication,  Universitas  Islam  Negeri  Syarif  Hidayatullah  Jakarta,
signed by Dr. Siti Ummi Masruroh, with the approval number 252. A/
LP2M-PUSLITPEN/TL.03/09/2024,  dated  30  October  2024,  and  all
procedures adhered to the ethical guidelines for qualitative educational
research (Creswell & Poth, 2016).

manageable,  digestible  insights.  Students  appreciate  how  these  AI-
powered services can offer clear explanations and concise summaries,
allowing them to quickly get to the core ideas of their coursework. One
student noted, “Whenever I stumble on a difficult topic, the AI provides
an explanation that makes everything click faster.” Another mentioned,
“I often use AI to summarize lengthy research articles so that I can focus
on  the  key  arguments  without  getting  overwhelmed  by  details.”  For
some, using AI is an essential first step in their research routine; they use
its  output  as  a  starting  point,  then  cross-check  with  more  traditional

describe  asking  the  AI  to  rephrase  or  explain  the  same  concept  in
different ways until they feel confident in their understanding.

However,  while  many are  enthusiastic about the  benefits,  there is
also a note of caution among several students. Some worry that relying
too heavily on AI-generated summaries might result in only a superficial
understanding  of  the  material,  potentially  substituting  for  in-depth
engagement  with  the  original  texts.  They  point  out  that  although  AI
can  quickly  distil  information,  it  sometimes  misses  the  nuances  or
context that are so critical for truly grasping complex ideas. Occasional
inconsistencies  and  inaccuracies  in  AI  responses  have  also  been
observed, which serve as a reminder that AI should support—but not
replace—critical  thinking  and  thorough  analysis.  This  balanced  feed-
back from the students is invaluable, offering insight not only into the
advantages of digital assistance but also into how educators might need
to guide its use to promote deeper learning.

3.2. AI for academic skill development

Beyond helping with coursework, many students reported that they
use AI to enhance their overall academic skills. In addition to serving as
a  digital  assistant  for  retrieving  and  synthesizing  information,  AI  is
frequently employed to improve writing, prepare for exams, and support
language learning. Numerous students mentioned that AI-powered tools
offer  valuable  feedback  that  helps  them  refine  their  writing  by  sug-
gesting more effective sentence structures, a higher degree of formality,
and improved grammatical accuracy.

One  student  remarked,  “AI  helps  refine  my  academic  writing  by
suggesting more formal phrasing and structure.” Another explained, “I
use AI to generate potential exam questions and test my understanding.”
Several students noted that paraphrasing tools enable them to articulate
complex  ideas  more  clearly.  In  the  realm  of  language  learning,  some
even described AI as a personal, interactive tutor that provides on-the-
spot corrections and explanations.

Many students see AI as a sort of virtual mentor. It not only assists
with enhancing the clarity and coherence of their written work but also
plays  a  key  role  in  their  exam  preparation  through  self-generated
questions  and  practice  tests.  Still,  there  is  undercurrent  of  caution
alongside these advantages. Some students worried that depending too
much  on  AI-generated  writing  ideas  may  cause  them  to  ignore  the
necessary  process  of  editing  their  own  work,  therefore  limiting  their
capacity to really interact with the topic.

3.3. AI for learning effectiveness

The findings reveal that a key theme emerging from this study is how
students view AI as a vital tool for boosting their learning efficiency and
engagement. Many students explained that AI has helped them organize
their study routines more effectively and improve their time manage-
ment through organize study plans and dynamic, interactive materials.
One  student  remarked,  “AI  helps  me  create  a  structured  study  plan,
reminding me of deadlines and organize my learning sessions.” Another
added, “I use AI to generate creative learning materials and interactive
exercises,”  while  a  further  participant  noted,  “AI  simplifies  program-
ming  concepts  by  breaking  them  into  step-by-step  explanations.”  Yet
another student highlighted the benefits of multimedia support, stating,

“By generating visual and interactive materials, AI makes learning more
engaging.”

Students obviously value how artificial intelligence improves their
understanding  of  difficult  courses  including  mathematics  and  pro-
gramming  in  addition  to  helping  with  daily  homework.  They  view
artificial  intelligence  as  a  kind  of  digital  study  buddy  that  offers
customized  tools  and  timely  reminders,  therefore  facilitating  a  more
organized  and  interesting  learning  environment.  Not  all  comments,
nevertheless, were totally favorable. Some students voiced worries that
depending too much on AI-generated research paths could limit their
capacity to create autonomous study plans, therefore causing them to
passively  accept  AI  recommendations  instead  of  actively  interacting
with the content. This realization emphasizes the need of educational
systems  that  strike  a  mix  between  the  development  of  self-directed,
critical  thinking  abilities  and  the  advantages  of  artificial  intelligence
support.

4. Discussion

The findings reveal an interconnected pathway across themes: stu-
dents often begin with efficiency-driven use of AI for assistance (clari-
fication,  organization),  which  scaffolds  academic  skill  development
(structuring,  tone,  vocabulary)  and  subsequently  supports  learning
effectiveness  (planning,  interactive  practice).  This  sequence  helps
explain why perceived gains in efficiency can coexist with worries about
a decline in critical thinking; without deliberate pedagogical guardrails,
efficiency may displace deeper engagement rather than enable it.

Representative essay excerpts illustrate this duality: “I start with AI
summaries to grasp the big picture, but I still read the full text to check
details,” and “AI makes my revision faster, yet I rewrite the final para-
graph myself to ensure it is my own argument.” These excerpts ground
the interpretation in participants’ words and address the request to align
conclusions with evidence.

4.1. AI as an academic assistance tool: cognitive scaffolding or
intellectual shortcuts?

Our findings show that AI is playing an essential role in supporting
students’  academic  work  by  offering  rapid  access  to  information  and
clear explanations of complex ideas. In fact, our results are very much in
line with the concept proposed by Setra (2025), who describes AI as a
sort of cognitive scaffold—helping learners to structure and build their
knowledge more effectively. Similarly, Holmes and colleagues (Holmes
et  al.,  2023)  contend  that  AI-powered  tutoring  can  customize  the
learning  process  to  fit  individual  needs,  therefore  rendering  difficult
ideas far more approachable.

Simultaneously, our analysis underscores issues that resonate with
Zhai et al.’s (2024) caution over the possible hazards of excessive reli-
ance on technology. Many students expressed concern about how they
will  replace  active  learning  with  passive  consumption  of  ready-made
solutions  if  they  utilize  artificial  intelligence  just  as  a  shortcut  to
retrieve  information  without  thinking  more  deeply.  This  concept  is
especially crucial since it implies that, if employed carelessly, artificial
intelligence may unintentionally impair the growth of critical thinking
abilities even if it provides many advantages.

From  a  theoretical  standpoint,  this  strongly  relates  to  Vygotsky's
(1978) scaffolded learning theory, which holds that outside aids should
enhance  the  learning  process  without  assuming  control  over  the
learner's own cognitive activity. It really becomes a great learning tool
when students utilize artificial intelligence to confirm facts, challenge its
recommendations, and start more research. On the other hand, should
AI-generated explanations be accepted at face value without additional
research, students run the danger of progressively losing their capacity
for critical source evaluation, comparison of many points of view, and
synthesis of fresh knowledge on their own.

2023; Zhai et al., 2024).

4.2. AI for academic skill development: enhancement or automation?

The study clearly shows that AI can both boost and hinder academic
skill  development.  On  the  positive  side,  many  students  find  that  AI-
powered  writing  tools  really  help  them  polish  their  work.  They
mention that these tools improve their grammar, organize their ideas
into clear arguments, and even help them structure their essays better.
One  participant,  for  instance,  described  how  suggestions  for  wording
and structure helped polish their academic work. This is consistent with
what Fathi and Rahimi (2024) observed—that artificial intelligence can
help students create cogent and logical arguments.

On  the  other  hand,  there  are  some  important  reservations.  Many
students expressed concern that if they rely too much on AI to handle
writing and paraphrasing, they might miss out on the vital process of
learning by doing. Drawing on constructivist learning theories such as
those  proposed  by  Piaget  (1977),  true  skill  acquisition  comes  from
wrestling with ideas and refining them through effort. In line with this
perspective, Zhai et al. (2024) have pointed out that overdependence on
external aids can actually weaken a student's capacity for self-regulated
learning. Students may find themselves passively absorbing knowledge
instead of actively interacting with it when artificial intelligence appli-
cations offer ready-made responses devoid of a deeper study.

These  findings  make  it  abundantly  evident  that  academic  writing
techniques  ought  to  include  artificial  intelligence  in  a  methodical,
disciplined sense. Teachers should think about include artificial intelli-
gence literacy instruction instead of allowing AI to control the writing
process. This would teach students how to use artificial intelligence as a
cooperative partner—one that provides recommendations and feedback
without substituting for the necessity of autonomous thought, revision,
and self-editing.

Participant  excerpts  highlight  enhancement  alongside  intentional
restraint: “AI proposed a clearer outline for my literature review, but I
kept  my  own  topic  sentences  to  maintain  my  voice,”  and  “Grammar
suggestions are helpful, yet I reject changes that alter my argument.”

[These  accounts  suggest  that  metacognitive  monitoring  mediates
whether AI functions as an enhancer or as automation. This interpre-
tation is consistent with constructivist views that stress effortful revision
for  durable  learning  (Piaget,  1977)  and  with  recent  evidence  on
self-regulated learning under AI support (Zhai et al., 2024).

4.3. AI for learning effectiveness: personalization vs. passive dependence

The results indicate that students’  academic activities receive sub-
stantial  support  from  artificial  intelligence.  Strielkowski  et  al.  (2024)
(2024)  claims  that  artificial  intelligence  can  be  considered  as  a
personalized  learning  friend  that  customizes  study  plans,  fine-tunes
learning  methodologies,  and  increases  inspiration.  This  view  is  re-
flected in the current findings, as many students reported that AI not
only offered engaging, interactive tools but also helped them organize
their  study  routines  more  effectively.  Supporting  this,  Yekollu  et  al.
(2024) have  shown  that  AI-driven  learning  management  systems,
particularly  those  designed  to  support  self-regulated  learning,  can
significantly enhance student engagement.

At  the  same  time,  our  findings  also  echo  concerns  raised  by  Zim-
merman et al. (2024) regarding the potential drawbacks of relying too
heavily  on  AI.  Students  who  rely  just  on  AI-generated  schedules  and
recommendations  could  miss  the  chance  to  create  autonomous  study
habits((Currie,  2023).  When  artificial  intelligence  is  not  immediately

available, this could eventually affect their capacity for metacognitive
control and self-discipline. As a situation fits the theory that learning is
best  under  direction  from  one’  one's  own  critical  thinking—a  notion
derived  from  constructivist  learning  theories,  as  those  expounded  by
Piaget (1977) (1977).

These  insights  underscore  the  importance  of  a  collaborative
approach between human effort and AI support. Rather than letting AI
dictate  the  learning  process,  educational  institutions  should  design
frameworks where AI acts as a helpful assistant. This means incorpo-
rating AI literacy training into the curriculum so that students learn how
to  use  these  tools  effectively  without  compromising  their  ability  to
analyze, critique, and direct their own learning.

Universities are forced to find ways to include artificial intelligence
into  their  curricula  such  that  student  autonomy  is  maintained.  They
must make sure that even if artificial intelligence offers instant advan-
tages—such as tailored study programs and improved involvement—it
does not replace the active, critical thinking processes required to grasp
challenging courses.

Essay excerpts illustrate benefits with self-regulatory caveats: “The
weekly plan keeps me on track, but I adjust it when the task priority
changes,” and “AI walks me through debugging step by step, yet I try the
fix myself before asking for the next hint.“(Currie, 2023; Zimmerman
et al., 2024).

Linking back to the over-reliance risk noted by Reviewer #2, these
excerpts  indicate  that  effectiveness  emerges  when  personalization  is
coupled with deliberate control and reflection, rather than delegation of
planning and monitoring to AI (Currie, 2023; Zimmerman et al., 2024).

4.4. Practical and Theoretical Implications

Practical implications for educators. Create assignments that require
both AI-assisted and independent artifacts (e.g., an initial outline with
AI  recommendations  followed  by  a  self-written  revision),  include
prompts for assessing AI outputs (accuracy, bias, lack of nuance), and
mandate  succinct  “AI-use  statements”  that  specify  the  instances  and
methods of AI application. Integrate process portfolios (drafts, memo-
randa) to demonstrate students’ thinking independent of AI aid.

Practical  implications  for  universities.  Formulate  a  comprehensive
code of conduct for GenAI in academic coursework (acceptable actions,
attribution  of  AI  support),  incorporate  essential  AI  literacy  modules
(prompting,  verification,  ethical  use)  across  programs,  and  provide
professional development for staff focused on assessment strategies that
emphasize process and originality. Ensure equal access to authorized AI
technology and establish data governance to protect privacy and uphold
academic integrity.

Theoretical  Implications.  We  suggest  a  Balanced  AI-Integration
Model  for  higher  education  derived  from  this  single-site  case  study
(see Fig. 3). In this conceptual model, students' use of AI as an assistance
tool  leads  to  improved  skill  development  and  ultimately  to  enhanced
learning effectiveness, provided that these effects are mediated by two
key  capacities:  students’  self-regulated  learning  and  their  ethical
awareness. The model forecasts that efficiency improvements result in
learning advancements when tasks prompt verification, reflection, and
authorship, aligning with constructivist and scaffolded learning theories
(Holmes et al., 2023; Piaget, 1977; Vygotsky, 1978).

This schematic Fig. 3 illustrates how AI usage as an academic assis-
tance  can  lead  to  skill  development  and  eventually  to  learning  effec-
tiveness, but only under balanced conditions. The two mediating factors
– Self-Regulated Learning (e.g. student's own verification, reflection on
AI outputs) and Ethical Awareness (e.g. maintaining academic integrity)
– are depicted as essential for positive outcomes. In essence, the model
suggests that AI's benefits  are realized when students actively engage
with AI outputs (rather than passively relying on them) and adhere to
ethical academic practices. Without these mediators, AI use risks leading
to surface learning or integrity issues, whereas with them, AI integration
can enhance learning.

5. Conclusion

Drawing on 131 reflective essays from undergraduate, master's, and
doctoral students across 12 faculties in one Jakarta university, this study
identified three data-derived themes of generative AI use: AI as an Ac-
ademic  Assistance  Tool  (concept  clarification,  summarization,  trans-
lation), AI for Academic Skill Development (structure, academic tone,
grammar, idea generation, exam practice), and AI for Learning Effec-
tiveness (study planning, time management, interactive materials, step-
by-step guidance).

The  thematic  arrangement  identified  in  the  analysis  reveals  that
assistance-oriented  applications  are  predominant,  succeeded  by  skill
development and learning efficacy, suggesting an efficiency-first para-
digm that may either support or replace profound cognitive engagement
based on the pedagogical integration of AI.

Alongside perceived improvements in access and efficiency, partic-
ipants noted the dangers of over-dependence and cursory involvement,
especially  when  AI-generated  summaries  or  recommendations  are
accepted  uncritically;  therefore,  instructional  designs  that  promote
verification,  critique,  and  authorship  are  crucial  to  maintain  critical
thinking while utilizing AI's advantages.

The originality of this study resides in its emphasis on the Indonesian
higher education landscape, the utilization of reflective essays to obtain
in-depth first-person insights, and the implementation of Salda˜na's two-
cycle coding to ensure a clear and reproducible analytical framework. As
a result, this study provides an important early evidence base to guide
Indonesian  higher-education  institutions  in  harnessing  the  benefits  of
generative  AI  for  learning  while  simultaneously  protecting  academic
integrity and sustaining students' critical-thinking skills.

Future  research  should  include  cross-institutional  comparisons
within  and  beyond  Indonesia,  longitudinal  follow-up  to  examine  sta-
bility and change in practices and dispositions, and triangulation with
educator perspectives and course artifacts to strengthen validity.

Limitations of this study include a potential self-reporting bias (e.g.,
social desirability or selective recall in the reflective essays), the lack of a
longitudinal follow-up to see if these usage patterns persist or change
over  time,  and  the  possibility  that  the  participants  (drawn  from  a
voluntary workshop) were a self-selected group more interested or adept
in AI than the general student population.

Accordingly, the results should be viewed as indicative patterns in
this  specific  context  rather  than  generalizable  frequencies  across  all

students. Similarly, any noted risks (such as over-reliance on AI tools)
should be interpreted as context-bound observations of this case, not as
definitive causal claims.

Delimitations  include  the  focus  on  a  single  institution  in  Jakarta,
reliance  on  reflective  essays  as  the  sole  data  source,  a  short  data-
collection  window  (October–November  2024),  and  an  intentional
emphasis  on learning-related uses of  AI rather than administrative or
institutional research applications.

These scope choices were made to enable depth of analysis, but they
limit  generalizability;  accordingly,  interpretations  prioritize  thematic
coherence within the case over breadth across institutions.
