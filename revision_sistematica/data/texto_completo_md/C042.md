Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence (Gen-AI) has come into existence and is rapidly transforming
the pedagogical world of Higher Education, with large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT,
Google Gemini and Microsoft Copilot at the forefront. Although there has been increasing uptake
across under- and post-graduate students, there are current mixed and limited empirical findings
on the exact  nature, direction and magnitude of the impact  of Gen-AI on measurable learning
outcomes. This study fills this gap by exploring the correlation between the pattern of using Gen-
AI  and  academic  learning  outcomes  of  students  in  HEIs  in  Punjab  Province,  Pakistan,  by
employing a quantitative method. A cross-sectional survey design was used to collect data from
521 undergraduate and graduate students from 5 major public universities with a validated 52
item self-report questionnaire that applied a latent factor analysis to the five constructs assessed:
Gen-AI  Utilization  Frequency,  Perceived  Learning  Enhancement,  Critical  Thinking
Development, Academic Engagement, and Learning Outcome Achievement. Data were analyzed
using  descriptive  statistics,  Pearson's  correlation  analysis  and  multiple  linear  regression  using
SPSS-26. The result indicated that there is a statistically significant positive relationship between
Gen-AI utilization and students' perceived learning outcomes (r = .71, p < .001), where Gen-AI
Utilization Frequency (β = .38, p < .001) and Academic Engagement (β = .29, p < .001) were the
most  significant  variables.  The  data  also  revealed  that  passive,  unmonitored  Gen-AI  use  was
strongly negatively correlated with critical thinking scores (r = −.34, p < .001), indicating that
excessive use of generative products may negatively affect higher-order thinking. The findings
have implications for curriculum design, educational policymaking, and institutional technology
policy,  highlighting  the  need  for  pedagogically-informed  integration  of  Gen-AI.  This  study
employs a quantitative research approach. The research method used in this study is quantitative.

Introduction
AI  has  gone  from  computer  science's  speculative  frontier  to  a  commonplace  part  of  everyday
academic life. One of the most groundbreaking advances in the last five years has been the advent
of generative artificial intelligence (Gen-AI), a subset of artificial intelligence that can generate
human-like text, code, images, and multimedia based on natural-language inquiries (Baidoo-Anu

Gemini,  Anthropic's  Claude,  and  Microsoft's  Copilot  have  seen  unparalleled  levels  of  user
adoption, with ChatGPT reaching more than 100 million active users in just two months since its
public release in late 2022 (Alshater, 2022). The universities and colleges are in a crossroads –
on one hand, the tools of Gen-AI are powerful learning aids, while on the other, they are potential
academic  dishonesty  vectors,  leaving  urgent  questions  about  what  should  be  done  in  the
institutions.
This tech change has implications for education that are significant. The supporters claim that
Gen-AI can provide personalised, on-demand tutoring, enhancements for language learning and
students  with  learning  disabilities,  literature  reviews  and  information  synthesis,  and  supports
writing  development  (Kasneci  et  al.,  2023;  Zhai  et  al.,  2024).  However,  critics  have  raised
concerns about the potential negative impacts of unregulated Gen-AI applications, such as the
loss  of  critical  thinking skills,  reduction  in  information  literacy,  academic  dishonesty,  and  the
standardizing of intellectual work (Cotton et al., 2023; Lund & Wang, 2023).
Nevertheless, there are a number of gaps in the existing literature. First, most of the published
research has been based on contexts in North America, Europe and East Asia, and, comparatively
less, on contexts of the Global South, and, specifically, of South Asian higher education contexts
(Rasul et al., 2023). Second, there are numerous studies remaining which are based on very small
convenience  samples  (N  <  100)  which  limits  the  ability  of  their  findings  to  be  generalizable.
Third, published studies have a variable understanding of the concept 'learning outcomes'. Fourth,
while  such  a  paradox  may  be  theoretically  sound,  there  have  been  few  empirical  tests  of  this
theoretical tension in a single analytical framework.

Primary Research Question
How accurately does the use of Gen-AI predict students' learning outcomes in higher education
institutions  of  Punjab  Province,  Pakistan?  The  study  also  investigates  the  moderation  of  the
relationship between Gen-AI use and critical thinking by usage patterns (active use versus passive
use) and the mediation of the relationship between Gen-AI Utilization Frequency and Learning
Outcome Achievement by Academic Engagement.

Theoretical Framework & Review of Related Literature
Theoretical Underpinnings
This study is based on three complementary theoretical frameworks that together can account for
students' use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools and how these tools affect student
learning outcomes.
The first is the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) by Davis (1989) that suggests that the two
main factors influencing the adoption of technology are perceived usefulness and perceived ease
of  use.  Perceived  usefulness  is  defined  as  the  extent  to  which  one  believes  that  a  particular
technology increases his or her performance, and perceived ease of use is defined as the degree
to which a technology is thought of as easy to use. In the context of GenAI, students' beliefs that
these tools help them complete tasks and are user-friendly are more likely to encourage them to
use them in their learning. Hence, the link between GenAI usage with students' learning outcomes
might be mediated by technology acceptance, since greater acceptance of technology fosters more
meaningful and prolonged interactions with it (Tlili et al., 2023).
The  second  theoretical  base  is  the  Zone  of  Proximal  Development  (ZPD)  as  developed  by
Vygotsky and also cognitive scaffolding. The key to the learners understanding is to offer the
appropriate support within their zone of proximal development, according to Vygotsky. Building
on this, the capabilities of GenAI can be transformed into an intelligent scaffolding tool, providing
customized explanations, examples, problem-solving guidance, and real-time feedback. This type
of assistance helps students to close the achievement gap between where they are and where they

The  third  is  the  Cognitive  Load  Theory  (CLT)  proposed  by  Sweller  (1988)  that  categorizes
cognitive load into intrinsic, extraneous, and germane. The intrinsic cognitive load comes from
the difficulty of the content being learned, the extraneous cognitive load comes from the difficulty
of  the  learning  environment's  design  or  unnecessary  cognitive  demands,  and  the  germane
cognitive  load  is  related  to  the  cognitive  investment  for  meaningful  learning  and  schema
construction.  By  handling  tasks  like  information  retrieval,  formatting,  grammar  and  language
correction, and refinement, GenAI can help students minimize their cognitive load while learning
and studying. This decrease frees up mental space for learners to engage in germane processing,
enhancing conceptual comprehension and knowledge building. But relying too heavily on GenAI
for higher-order cognitive processes like critical analysis, interpretation, or synthesis, could lead
to  a  decrease  in  the  engagement  of  students  in  germane  cognitive  processes.  Consequently,
although students might seem more “productive”, learning and conceptual understanding might
be adversely affected by reduced cognitive engagement.

Generative AI and Academic Performance
Research on the direct link between the use of Gen-AI and academic outcomes has yielded mixed,
but generally positive results. In an investigation of 312 university students in Cambodia, Sok and
Heng  (2023)  determined  that  perceived  usefulness  of  ChatGPT  was  a  significant  positive
predictor of academic performance (β = .42, p < .01). Likewise, Shoufan (2023) conducted an
experiment  where  engineering  students  who  used  ChatGPT  to  aid  problem  solving  were
significantly higher on follow-up assessments compared to a control group (d = 0.61). In a recent
study, Zhai et al. (2024) performed a meta-analysis of 34 peer-reviewed studies, finding a pooled
effect size of g = 0.47 (95% CI [0.31, 0.63]) for the impact of AI-assisted learning on academic
outcomes, indicating a moderate positive effect.
In  Pakistan's  higher  education  sphere,  research  on  the  use  of  educational  technology  has
highlighted how the use of digital tools by students is becoming more prevalent as a method of
supporting learning, but noted that the choice of digital tool is constrained by infrastructure and
digital literacy issues (Rasul et al., 2023). However, the context of Punjab Province, where the
number of students in the most densely populated and technologically advanced universities in
Pakistan  is  relatively  higher  than  in  other  provinces,  is  an  important  one  to  examine  when
exploring the subject of Gen-AI adoption.

Generative AI and Critical Thinking
One of the aspects of the effect of Gen-AI on higher order thinking skills is possibly the most
debated  in  the  theoretical  literature.  Concern  has  been  raised  about  the  effects  of  Gen-AI  on
students' motivation and practice of critical thinking skills that are important for academic success
and future career development, such as source evaluation, logical reasoning, argumentation, and
synthesis (Cotton et al., 2023; Mollick & Mollick, 2023). There are empirical signs of this worry.
In a study of 985 students in several countries (Gerlich 2023), it was concluded that the higher
the reported intensity of Gen-AI usage, the lower the reports of critical thinking (r = −.29, p <
.001), especially if the students reported using Gen-AI more to generate content than to ideate
and  provide  feedback.  The  results  are  in  line  with  the  cognitive  offloading  hypothesis,  which
suggests that frequent use of external instruments to offload cognitive resources will lead to a
decrease in internal cognitive structures for independent thinking (Risko & Gilbert, 2016).

student involvement in  learning) is  one of the strongest  predictors of learning outcomes at  all

Research Hypotheses
H1 There is a statistically significant positive relationship between Gen-AI Utilization Frequency
and Students' Learning Outcome Achievement.
H2  There  is  a  statistically  significant  positive  relationship  between  Perceived  Learning
Enhancement through Gen-AI and Learning Outcome Achievement.
H3  There  is  a  statistically  significant  negative  relationship  between  passive  Gen-AI  use  and
Critical Thinking Development.
H4 The relationship between Gen-AI Utilization Frequency and Learning Outcome Achievement
is  partially  mediated  by  Academic  Engagement.  The  Learning  Outcome  Achievement  is
significantly predicted by
H5:  Gen-AI  Utilization  Frequency,  Perceived  Learning  Enhancement,  Critical  Thinking
Development, and Academic Engagement jointly.

Research Methodology
Research Design
The study design used was quantitative and cross-sectional survey design. The research questions
require the measurement of variables, statistical testing of hypothesized relationships and findings
that are generalizable beyond the immediate sample; for these reasons, quantitative methodology
was chosen. The cross-section design was suitable for the study because it sought to capture the
current situation of student populations in relation to the use of Gen-AI and its correlates all in
one  moment  in  time.  The  study  is  done  in  accordance  with  the  STROBE  (Strengthening  the
Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist for social sciences survey studies.

3.2 Population and Sampling
The  target  population  was  all  the  students  of  undergraduate  and  postgraduate  at  the  public
universities  of  Punjab  province,  Pakistan,  for  the  academic  year  2025-2026.  To  cover  key
geographic  sub-regions  of  Punjab  and  a  disciplinary  faculty,  5  major  universities  were
purposively selected:

•  University of the Punjab, Lahore
•  Quaid-i-Azam University (QAU) — Lahore Campus
•  Government College University (GCU), Faisalabad
•  Bahauddin Zakariya University (BZU), Multan
•  University of Gujrat (UOG), Gujrat

Stratified random sampling method was used. Each university was considered as a stratum and
for each stratum, the departments  were randomly  chosen from among the faculties of Natural
Sciences,  Social  Sciences,  Engineering  and  Humanities.  In  some  departments  students  were
recruited  by  systematic  random  sampling  (one  in  every  nth  from  the  rosters  of  students).  The
minimum required N for multiple regression analysis with five predictors, a = .05 with power =
.95 and a medium effect size (f2 = .15) was calculated to be 294 with the help of G*Power 3.1.9.7.

Participants
Of the 347 participants, 198 (57.1%) were male and 149 (42.9%) were female. Most of them were
undergraduate students (n = 241, 69.5%) while the others were postgraduates (n = 106, 30.5%).
The mean age of participants was 21.4 years (SD = 2.31, range: 18-31). As far as discipline is
concerned, 38.9% were Natural Sciences, 26.5% were Social Sciences, 20.5% were Engineering,
and 14.1% were Humanities. Nineteen students (5.3%) indicated that they didn't use a Gen-AI
tool for academic work, representing low penetration (5.3%) among the sampled population.

Instrumentation
The  researcher-developed  and  validated  questionnaire,  Generative  AI  and  Learning  Outcomes
Scale  (GALOS)  was  used  to  access  the  data.  There  are  52  items  across  five  subscales  on  the
instrument:

  Subscale A — Gen-AI Utilization Frequency (GAIF): 10 items scale that measures the

frequency and modality of using Gen-AI tools for academic purposes.

  Subscale  B  —  Perceived  Learning  Enhancement  (PLE):  12  items  related  to  students'
perceived improvement in their understanding, efficiency, and knowledge gained through
the use of Gen-AI tools.

  Subscale C — Critical Thinking Development (CTD): 10 items assessing students' self-
reported  critical  thinking  skills  (analysis,  evaluation,  synthesis,  and  independent
reasoning) with regard to academic content.

  Academic  Engagement  (AE):  10  items  assessing  cognitive,  behavioral,  and  emotional

engagement with academic tasks; subscale D.

  Subscale  E  —  Learning  Outcome  Achievement  (LOA):  10  items  that  assess  overall
academic  performance,  conceptual  understanding,  assessment  performance  and  skill
acquisition.

Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly
Agree). Scale validity was determined by means of a 3-stage process. The Content Validity Index

Data Collection Procedure
This  research  received  ethical  approval  from  Research  Ethics  Committee  at  University  of  the
Punjab, Lahore (Ref: UP-REC-2025-114) before data collection. The administrative authorities
of  all  the  participating  universities  granted  institutional  permission.  Questionnaires  were
administered  in  class  by  research  assistants  who  were  trained  in  the  process  of  survey
administration  and  who  obtained  written  informed  consent  from  participants.  The  study  was
voluntary, and respondents were assured of complete anonymity. Confidentiality and privacy of all
participants were protected by not collecting personally identifiable information. The data collection
was carried out for three months, starting in February and ending in April 2026.
Data  were  entered,  cleaned,  screened  and  analyzed  using  IBM  SPSS  Statistics  Version  26.  First,
descriptive  statistics,  such  as  frequencies,  percentages,  means,  and  standard  deviations  were
calculated  for  the  demographic  data  and  various  study  variables.  Assumptions  of  normality  were
checked by reviewing the Q–Q plot and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test before conducting inferential
analyses.  The  Pearson's  product-moment  correlation  analysis  was  used  to  explore  the  relationship
between  the  study  variables  first  to  examine  the  degree  of  relationship  or  association  between the
variables  constructs.  Next,  multiple  linear  regression  analysis  was  carried  out  to  investigate  the
predictive  relationships  between  the  independent  and  dependent  variables.  All  the  underlying
assumptions (linearity, normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, independence of errors and lack of
multicollinearity) were tested to validate the regression models before the analyses were carried out.
In order to find unique contribution of each predictor to students' learning outcomes, the researcher
used  the  method  of  hierarchical  multiple  regression  analysis  by  entering  variables  in  theoretically
informed blocks. This procedure allowed for an analysis of the incremental contribution of each set
of  predictors,  controlling  for  all  prior  predictors.  Finally,  the  intervening  role  of  Academic
Engagement  on  the  relationship  between  the  independent  variables  and  the  learning  outcome  was
tested by using the PROCESS macro developed by Hayes (2018) (Model 4). The mediation analysis
was  repeated  with  5000  bootstrap  resamples  and  bias-corrected  95%  confidence  intervals  were
calculated to assess the significance of indirect effects. The indirect effect was deemed statistically
significant if the lower and upper bounds of the confidence interval were not within zero.

Key Finding: Model Fit
The  full  regression  model  was  statistically  significant  (F(4,  342)  =  135.4,  p  <  .001)  and
explained 61.3% of the variance in Learning Outcome Achievement (R2 = .613, Adjusted
R2 = .608). Gen-AI Utilization Frequency was the strongest predictor (β = .38), followed by
Academic Engagement (β = .29), Perceived Learning Enhancement (β = .22), and Critical
Thinking Development (β = .19). All VIF values were below 5.0, confirming the absence of
problematic multicollinearity.

Mediation Analysis (H4)
Academic Engagement (AE) was tested for mediation between the relationship between **Utilization
Frequency of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAIF)** and the relationship between the relation
and Learning Outcome Achievement (LOA) using **Model 4 of Hayes' PROCESS macro (Hayes,
2018)** with 5,000 bootstrap resamples. The results showed that the indirect effect of GAIF on LOA
was statistically significant via AE (B = 0.148, SE = 0.031, 95% Bootstrap CI [0.091, 0.213]). As the
bootstrap 95% confidence interval for the indirect effect did not contain zero, the indirect effect was
deemed statistically significant and suggesting that there was a significant mediation effect between
GAIF  and  LOA.  Also,  the  direct  path  from  GAIF  to  LOA  was  statistically  significant  even  when
controlling for Academic Engagement (B = 0.231, SE = 0.054, *p* < .001). This result suggests that
there is a partial mediation, meaning that GAIF directly affects Learning Outcome Achievement as
well as indirectly, via Academic Engagement. That is, the greater the use of such GenAI tools, the
better  students'  learning  outcomes,  both  directly  through  the  contribution  of  GenAI  tools  and
indirectly  through  increased  academic  engagement,  which  leads  to  better  academic  performance.
Hence, the Hypothesis H4 was accepted.

Group Differences
Independent  samples  t-tests  were  used  to  determine  if  there  were  any  differences  in  both  the
pattern of use of Gen-AI and scores on the learning outcomes between gender and academic level.
Male students reported significantly higher GAIF scores than female students (M = 3.89 vs. M =
3.53, t(345) = 4.22, p < .001, d = 0.45), though LOA scores did not differ significantly by gender
(t(345) = 1.17, p = .244). The scores for the postgraduate students were significantly higher than
the scores for the undergraduate students (M = 3.94 vs. M = 3.60; t(345) = 4.71, p < .001, d =
0.49) as would be expected for more advanced students.

Discussion
Gen-AI Utilization and Learning Outcomes
 This  investigation  yielded  the  most  significant  result  of  positive  correlation  between  Gen-AI
Utilization Frequency and Learning Outcome Achievement (r = .71, β = .38). This discovery is
consistent with the increasing research that has shown how Gen-AI tools, when integrated into
students'  academic  workflows,  can  be  used  as  effective  cognitive  scaffolds  that  facilitate
comprehension,  helps  reduce  barriers  to  information  access,  and  allows  learners  to  allocate
cognitive  resources  to  more  complex  tasks  (Kasneci  et  al.,  2023;  Zhai  et  al.,  2024).  This
association is particularly strong (r = .71), compared to the pooled effect sizes reported in recent
meta-analysis (g = 0.47; Zhai et al., 2024), which could be due to the high prevalence and intensity
of Gen-AI adoption in the technologically connected university population in Punjab.

Important Paradox Identified
Gen-AI use is positively associated with overall learning outcomes (r = .71) but negatively
associated with critical thinking capacity (r = -.34), which itself predicts good outcomes (β
= .19). This suggests Gen-AI use may enable short-term performance gains while potentially
eroding  the  foundational  cognitive  competencies  that  sustain  long-term  academic  and
professional achievement.

The potential impact of this paradox is a call to action: to welcome Gen-AI with caution until
there are pedagogical  interventions to  ensure and enhance critical  thinking.  It  is  important  for
educators to design (AI) activities that ask students to critique, extend, challenge, or evaluate AI-
generated  content  rather  than  producing  it  and  submitting  it  as  their  work.  This  practice  is
sometimes referred to as ‘AI-mediated critical inquiry' (Mollick & Mollick 2023).

Academic Engagement as a Mediator
The  mediation  analysis  showed  that  Academic  Engagement  partially  mediated  the  Academic
Engagement – Learning Outcome Achievement relationship, with Gen-AI Utilization Frequency
as the predictor.The mediation analysis indicated that the relationship between Gen-AI Utilization
Frequency  and  Learning  Outcome  Achievement  was  partially  mediated  by  Academic
Engagement. This finding builds on previous theoretical research (Baidoo-Anu & Owusu Ansah,
2023;  Fredricks  et  al.,  2004),  which  suggests  Gen-AI  tools  can  positively  impact  learning
outcomes  by  increasing  engagement  with  academic  content  in  depth  and  breadth,  and  has
provided empirical evidence in a well-powered sample that supports this claim.

Gender and Academic Level Differences
Higher GAIF scores by males could be due to gender-specific differences in technology use that
have been observed in the contexts of developing countries, where boys are more likely to have
access to and feel comfortable with digital tools. It is noteworthy that there was no difference
between the learning outcomes of gender groups, as female students reported using Gen-AI less,
suggesting  that  their  use  was  more  strategic  and  purposeful.  They  found  that  post-graduate
students had higher scores in LOA than the undergraduate students, which is in line with previous
studies  demonstrating  that  academic  maturity/self-regulation/metacognitive  sophistication  is
correlated to high academic performance.

Limitations
There are some limitations of this study which are acknowledged. First, cross-sectional design
does not allow for causal inference—experimental or longitudinal design is needed to establish
the temporal precedence and directionality. Second, the study used self-reported learning outcome
and critical  thinking, which are prone to  social desirability bias. Third, the study  was done in
Punjab Province, Pakistan and findings are not directly generalizable to other populations. Fourth,
the GALOS instrument has proven good psychometric characteristics, but it is not validated in
international contexts yet.

Conclusions and Implications
The  current  study  aimed  to  examine  the  impact  of  using  generative  artificial  intelligence  on
students' learning outcomes in higher education institutions in Punjab Province in Pakistan, with
347 undergraduate and postgraduate students completing a 52-item quantitative instrument with
content validity. The major findings are reported below:

•  The results indicate that Gen-AI Utilization Frequency is a strong and significant positive
predictor of Learning Outcome Achievement (β = .38, p < .001) which supports H1.

Implications for Policy and Practice
The findings provide a number of practical implications to the important stakeholders:

  For  Educational  Policymakers:  The  data  highlight  the  importance  of  coherent  and
institutionally designed governance frameworks for the use of Gen-AI that do not ban its
use (otherwise depriving it of the learning benefits it is known to provide) nor allow it to
be used without any restrictions (otherwise risking the loss of critical thinking skills). It is
suggested that a balanced pedagogically mediated integration model is used.

  For Curriculum Designers and Educators: Gen-AI integrated assessments should address
higher-order  cognition  and  not  just information  retrieval.  Content  that  asks  students  to
critique  AI  outputs  or  to  find  errors  in  AI  generated  content  has  a  higher  chance  of
maintaining the development of students' critical thinking skills.

  For  Students:  The  ability  to  use  Gen-AI  is  important  but  there  must  be  an  active  and
reflective component — a thinking partner instead of an answer machine. Students need
to be made aware of the cognitive offloading risk, and should be encouraged to use Gen-
AI to start their inquiry but not to end it.

  Future  Researchers:  This  study  presents  a  validated  GALOS  instrument,  which  is
deployable in comparative research across institutional, national and cultural settings, and
which  can  be  used  to  systematically  build  up  comparable  evidence  of  the  educational
effects of generative AI.
