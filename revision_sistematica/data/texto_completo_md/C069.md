Abstract
The rapid rise of Generative AI in education has brought transformative potential.
However, there is limited empirical insight into the factors influencing students’ use
of these tools and their impact on academic performance. Specifically, research has
not thoroughly examined how task-technology fit and behavioral factors shape Gen-
erative AI usage. This study addresses these gaps by integrating the Task-Technol-
ogy Fit (TTF) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to develop a theoretical
research model. Data were collected from university students through a structured
survey, and the model was validated using a hybrid Structural Equation Modeling-
Artificial Neural Network (SEM-ANN) approach. The results demonstrate that both
task  and  technology  characteristics  significantly  impact  task-technology  fit,  posi-
tively  influencing  the  use  of  Generative  AI  tools.  Additionally,  behavioral  factors
such as attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control were found to
strongly encourage Generative AI usage. Notably, the study confirms that these AI
tools  positively  contribute  to  students’  academic  performance.  At  the  same  time,
the study recognizes the ethical dilemmas tied to Generative AI, highlighting issues
such as academic integrity, excessive dependence, and its potential effects on critical
thinking. The findings offer valuable insights for various stakeholders and provide
practical guidance for strategically integrating AI tools to enhance student outcomes.

1  Introduction

Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a transformative branch of AI focused on
creating new content by learning patterns from vast datasets (Dwivedi et al., 2023).
In contrast to traditional AI models, which are mainly designed for tasks like clas-
sification  and  prediction,  Generative  AI  models  can  create  new  data,  including
text,  images,  music,  and  other  complex  outputs  (Lim  et  al.,  2023).  The  revolution
of Generative AI is gaining significant momentum in the Asia/Pacific region, with
IDC  projecting  an  impressive  CAGR  of  95.4%  by  2027  (Carreon  &  Cruz,  2024).
Some of the most prominent models driving the field include “Generative Adversar-
ial  Networks  (GANs)”,  “Variational  Autoencoders  (VAEs)”,  and  language  models
like “GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer)”. The applications of Generative AI
span a wide array of industries. For instance, it has revolutionized sectors in busi-
ness where Generative AI contributes to productivity and innovation, particularly in
the realms of automation and personalized content creation (Chui et al., 2023). This
capability streamlines operations and enhances decision-making quality by provid-
ing data-driven insights that support human judgment (Ashritha & Reddy, 2023).

As a versatile tool, Generative AI is being applied to enhance education through
the creation of personalized content, simulations, and virtual learning environments
(Al-Emran,  2024).  AI-driven  models  can  generate  personalized  quizzes,  assign-
ments, and study plans based on a student’s learning history and performance, pro-
viding  a  tailored  approach  to  education  that  adapts  in  real-time  to  their  progress
(Zawacki-Richter  et  al.,  2019).  This  personalization  helps  students  engage  with
material  that  addresses  their  unique  strengths  and  weaknesses,  thereby  improving
comprehension  and  retention.  Powered  by  large  language  models  (LLMs),  Chat-
GPT  enables  the  creation  of  personalized  educational  content,  enhancing  student
engagement and optimizing the overall efficiency of education delivery (K. B. Ooi
et al., 2023). In academic research, tools like ChatGPT assist with problem formula-
tion,  research  design,  data  collection,  analysis,  and  the  evaluation  of  written  work
(Susarla et al., 2023). Additionally, AI tools offer valuable support to self-directed
learners by providing personalized guidance, feedback, and learning resources, mak-
ing  it  an  effective  tool  for  independent  education  (Qin  &  Zhong,  2024).  Through
natural language processing (NLP), AI can generate summaries or adapt content to
different  reading  levels,  ensuring  that  students  receive  information  suited  to  their
learning needs.

The emergence of Generative AI has sparked considerable discussion regarding
its  place  in  academic  settings,  particularly  among  students  (Zhang  &  Tur,  2023),
emphasizing  the  need  for  a  deeper  understanding  of  the  factors  that  facilitate  its
adoption (K. B. Ooi et al., 2023). Furthermore, there is limited empirical research
on how Generative AI impacts students’ academic outcomes (Al-Qaysi et al., 2024).
While many studies have underscored the potential benefits of AI tools, there needs
to be more empirical research on how well these tools align with the specific tasks
students are required to complete, a concept referred to as task-technology fit. Task-
technology  fit  describes  the  extent  to  which  technology  meets  the  needs  of  a  task
(Faqih & Jaradat, 2021). It has been identified as a critical determinant of successful

2   Research model and hypotheses development

The theoretical foundation of this study is grounded in two complementary frame-
works: the “Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)” (Ajzen, 1991) and “Task-Technol-
ogy Fit (TTF)” (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). The TPB is widely used to explain
individual  behaviors  in  various  contexts,  particularly  technology  adoption  and  use
(Wong et al., 2024). The theory postulates that three main constructs—attitude, sub-
jective norms, and perceived behavioral control—jointly influence behavioral inten-
tions and, consequently, actual behavior (Gansser & Reich, 2023). In the context of
this research, these factors are examined in relation to students’ use of Generative AI
tools for academic purposes.

The TTF model posits that the performance impact of technology is contingent
upon the degree to which the technology matches the tasks it is intended to support
(Foroughi et al., 2024a, 2024b). This study integrates TTF into the TPB framework
by  considering  how  task  and  technology  characteristics  affect  the  fit  between  the
Generative AI tools and students’ academic tasks. The “Task-Technology Fit” con-
struct is hypothesized to directly influence students’ Generative AI use, which aligns
with the belief that when a technology closely fits the needs of the task, users are
more likely to adopt and effectively use it (C. Wang et al., 2023). In this integrated
model, the TPB constructs (“attitude”, “subjective norms”, and “perceived behavio-
ral control”) serve as psychological and social drivers of Generative AI use, while
the TTF framework addresses the technical alignment between task and technology.
Specifically, task characteristics and technology characteristics are posited to influ-
ence the degree of task-technology fit, which in turn impacts the behavioral use of
Generative AI. The ultimate outcome of interest, academic performance, is expected
to be influenced by the extent to which students employ Generative AI in their aca-
demic activities. Hence, by capturing the psychological determinants from TPB and
the technical task alignment from TTF, this research aims to provide a comprehen-
sive understanding of the factors that influence students’ Generative AI use and its
impact on their academic performance (see Fig. 1). The following subsections pro-
vide a more detailed explanation of the suggested relationships.

2.1   Task characteristics

Task characteristics refer to the specific attributes and requirements of a task that
need to be addressed for successful completion (Zhao et al., 2023). These charac-
teristics can include task complexity, uncertainty, interdependence, and the infor-
mation-processing demands that the task imposes (Y. C. Liu et al., 2023a, 2023b).
In  an  academic  context,  tasks  might  range  from  simple  information  retrieval  to
more complex assignments requiring critical thinking, research, and data analysis
(Sanabria-Z  et  al.,  2024).  In  the  context  of  the  TTF  model,  task  characteristics
are  critical  because  the  extent  to  which  the  technology  aligns  with  these  char-
acteristics will determine the perceived suitability of the technology for the task
(Almusawi  &  Durugbo,  2024).  For  instance,  if  a  task  demands  substantial  cog-
nitive  effort,  such  as  conducting  in-depth  data  analysis  or  synthesizing  multiple
sources  of  information,  a  technology  that  provides  advanced  content  generation
or  analytical  tools  (e.g.,  Generative  AI)  will  be  perceived  as  more  suitable  and
effective for the user (Wood & Moss, 2024). Conversely, if the task requires only
basic  operations,  advanced  technological  capabilities  may  not  significantly  con-
tribute to a better fit (Akimov et al., 2023).

Several  studies  in  educational  and  technological  contexts  have  supported  the
link between task characteristics and task-technology fit. For example, David and
Weinstein  (2024)  found  that  when  the  complexity  of  academic  tasks  increased,
students  preferred  technology  that  offered  enhanced  features  for  research  and
writing, which leads to a better fit for their needs. Similarly, Sharma et al. (2024)
demonstrated  that  task  interdependence  in  collaborative  learning  environments
was  positively  related  to  the  perceived  fit  of  digital  learning  tools.  These  find-
ings underline the importance of aligning the capabilities of the technology with
the specific demands of the tasks for optimal user satisfaction and performance.
Thus, it is hypothesized that:

H1: Task characteristics have a positive effect on task-technology fit.

2.2   Technology characteristics

Technology characteristics refer to the attributes and functionalities of a technology
that influence how well it performs in specific task environments (Fu et al., 2020).
These  characteristics  may  include  ease  of  use,  flexibility,  reliability,  functionality,
and the ability to process complex tasks (Shao & Li, 2022). In the context of educa-
tional technologies, tools like Generative AI can offer various features such as natu-
ral language processing, real-time feedback, and large-scale data analysis, which are
particularly useful for students engaged in academic tasks (Alqahtani et al., 2023).
The TTF model emphasizes that the closer the match between the technology’s fea-
tures and the requirements of the task, the greater the perceived fit (El-Masri et al.,
2022). When students perceive that the technology aligns with their academic tasks,
they  are  more  likely  to  adopt  and  effectively  use  it  (Sutherland  et  al.,  2024).  For
example, if a student needs to analyze large datasets or generate meaningful insights
from  unstructured  information,  a  technology  that  offers  advanced  data  processing
and  content  generation  capabilities  will  provide  a  better  fit  (S.  Chen  et  al.,  2024a,
2024b).

Previous  research  in  the  domain  of  educational  technology  has  consistently
demonstrated this relationship. Li et al. (2024) found that technologies with adapt-
able  features  significantly  enhanced  students’  perceptions  of  fit  when  performing
research-oriented  tasks.  Similarly,  Dahri  et  al.  (2024)  reported  that  the  flexibility
and customization options provided by AI-based tools positively impacted students’
perceived fit in performing academic tasks. These findings support the notion that
the richer the technology’s characteristics, the more likely it is to align with complex
educational tasks. According to these insights, this study suggests the following:

H2: Technology characteristics positively influence task-technology fit.

2.3   Task‑technology fit

The TTF refers to the degree to which a technology’s capabilities align with the spe-
cific needs of a task (Faqih & Jaradat, 2021). When users feel that a technology aligns
well with their tasks, they are more likely to use it because it enhances their efficiency
and  effectiveness  in  completing  those  tasks  (Grewal  et  al.,  2023).  In  academic  set-
tings,  Generative  AI  offers  a  wide  range  of  functionalities  that  can  assist  students  in
various tasks, such as content creation, problem-solving, and data analysis (Foroughi
et  al.,  2024a,  2024b).  However,  the  extent  to  which  students  adopt  and  use  Genera-
tive AI depends heavily on how well they perceive the technology aligns with the spe-
cific academic tasks they need to perform (Gupta et al., 2024). The TTF model asserts
that  when  there  is  a  strong  alignment  between  task  requirements  and  the  technolo-
gy’s capabilities, users are more inclined to use the technology because it helps them
achieve  their  objectives  more  efficiently  (Khashan  et  al.,  2023).  For  example,  if  stu-
dents perceive that Generative AI tools provide the necessary support for completing
complex academic tasks, such as synthesizing information or generating insights from

large datasets, they are more likely to integrate these tools into their learning routines
(Kohnke et al., 2023).

Empirical  evidence  supports  the  link  between  task-technology  fit  and  technology
use. For instance, Lousã and Lousã (2023) found that students were more inclined to
use  digital  tools  when  they  perceived  a  strong  fit  between  the  technology’s  capabili-

fit positively influenced the usage of AI-based educational tools in higher education.
These studies suggest that the better the task-technology fit, the more likely users will
adopt and rely on the technology. As such, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: Task-technology fit positively affects the use of Generative AI.

2.4   Attitude

Attitude refers to an individual’s positive or negative assessment of engaging in a spe-
cific  behavior  (Iranmanesh  et  al.,  2024),  such  as  using  Generative  AI.  According  to
the TPB, attitude is one of the central determinants of behavioral intentions and actual
behavior (Gansser & Reich, 2023). When students show a positive attitude toward Gen-
erative AI, seeing it as useful, efficient, or enjoyable, they are more likely to incorporate
it into their academic routines (Chiu, 2024). On the other hand, a negative attitude may
discourage them from using the technology, regardless of its capabilities (Mogavi et al.,
2024).  In  the  context  of  technology  use,  particularly  in  educational  settings,  attitude
plays a crucial role in determining adoption (Songkram et al.,  2023). When students
believe that Generative AI can enhance their learning experience, save time, or improve
the  quality  of  their  academic  work,  they  are  more  inclined  to  use  it  (K.  Chen  et  al.,
2024a, 2024b). Positive experiences with the technology—such as ease of use, helpful-
ness,  or  perceived  enjoyment—can  further  reinforce  favorable  attitudes  and,  in  turn,
increase the likelihood of use (Henstrom et al., 2024).

Several studies in the educational technology domain support the positive relation-
ship between attitude and technology use. For instance, Kim and Lee (2024) demon-
strated that students with positive attitudes toward AI-based learning tools were more
likely  to  adopt  them  for  study  purposes.  Similarly,  Latorre-Cosculluela  et  al.  (2024)
found that favorable attitudes toward educational technologies were significantly asso-
ciated with increased technology usage in academic contexts. These findings underline
the critical role of attitude in shaping students’ decisions to use Generative AI tools.
Based  on  the  supporting  evidence  from  prior  research,  the  following  hypothesis  is
suggested:

H4: Attitude positively influences the use of Generative AI.

2.5   Subjective norms

Subjective  norms  refer  to  the  perceived  social  pressure  to  perform  or  not  perform
a  specific  behavior  (Ajzen,  2020).  In  the  context  of  Generative  AI  use,  subjective
norms  encompass  the  influence  of  peers,  instructors,  and  the  broader  academic

community on students’ decisions to adopt AI tools (C. Wang et al., 2024a, 2024b).
When students perceive that important individuals or groups—such as classmates,
teachers, or academic leaders—expect them to use Generative AI, they may be more
likely to conform to these expectations, leading to increased technology use (Ivanov
et  al.,  2024).  The  influence  of  subjective  norms  on  technology  adoption  has  been
well-documented  across  various  educational  contexts  (Sprenger  &  Schwaninger,
2023). Social influence can shape attitudes toward technology by fostering a sense
of social desirability or academic pressure (Doo, 2023). For example, students who
observe their peers using Generative AI tools for assignments or hear positive feed-
back from instructors may feel encouraged to adopt similar technologies (Johnston
et  al.,  2024).  In  educational  environments  where  technology  use  is  prevalent,  the
effect of subjective norms becomes even more pronounced (Al-Emran et al., 2023).

Research  has  repeatedly  confirmed  the  positive  effect  of  subjective  norms  on
adopting  educational  technologies.  Chiu  et  al.  (2023)  reported  that  students  who
felt social pressure from their peers and instructors were more likely to engage with
AI-based  learning  tools.  Likewise,  Bai  and  Jiang  (2024)  observed  that  subjective
norms had a strong influence on the acceptance and sustained use of digital learning
platforms in academic settings. These findings suggest that social expectations are
pivotal in shaping students’ technology use. Taking these insights into account, this
study hypothesizes that:

H5: Subjective norms have a positive impact on the use of Generative AI.

2.6   Perceived behavioral control

Perceived behavioral control (PBC) refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to
execute  a  particular  behavior  based  on  their  perception  of  the  ease  or  difficulty  of
performing it (Ajzen, 2020). In the context of Generative AI use, PBC reflects stu-
dents’ confidence in their ability to effectively use AI tools to accomplish academic
tasks (Ivanov et al., 2024). When students feel they have sufficient skills, access to
necessary resources, and minimal obstacles to using Generative AI, they are more
likely  to  engage  with  the  technology  (J.  Kim  et  al.,  2024).  PBC  has  been  shown
to significantly influence technology adoption, as individuals with higher levels of
perceived  control  are  more  likely  to  adopt  new  tools  and  integrate  them  into  their
routine activities (Ahmed et al., 2024). For instance, if students believe that using
Generative AI requires minimal technical expertise and that they have the necessary
support and resources to do so, they are encouraged to leverage these tools in their
academic  work  (Wood  &  Moss,  2024).  On  the  contrary,  a  lack  of  perceived  con-
trol—due  to  insufficient  skills  or  technological  barriers—can  discourage  adoption,
regardless of the tool’s potential benefits (Moorhouse et al., 2023).

Several  studies  have  demonstrated  the  positive  link  between  PBC  and  technol-
ogy use. Research conducted by Chiu et al. (2023) found that students with stronger
control over AI-based learning tools were more likely to incorporate them into their
studies. Moreover, AL-Nuaimi et al. (2024) concluded that the ease of accessing and
using  educational  technologies  had  a  substantial  impact  on  student  adoption  rates,

2.7   Generative AI use

The use of Generative AI tools in academic settings has the potential to enhance stu-
dents’ academic performance by improving their ability to complete complex tasks,
conduct research, and generate high-quality content more efficiently (Jaboob et al.,
2024).  Generative  AI  can  support  students  by  enhancing  information  synthesis,
problem-solving, and content creation (M. Liu et al., 2023a, 2023b). This assistance
enables them to produce work that is often more insightful, detailed, or creative than
what they might accomplish independently (Pahi et al., 2024). As a result, students
can dedicate more time to higher-order thinking, which may lead to improved learn-
ing outcomes and greater academic success (Lee et al., 2024).

The  relationship  between  technology  use  and  academic  performance  has  been
widely explored in educational research (e.g., (Foroughi et al., 2022; F. Wang et al.,
2024a,  2024b)).  Tools  that  are  aligned  with  students’  learning  objectives  and  sup-
port task completion have been found to positively influence performance outcomes
(Cammies  et  al.,  2024).  For  instance,  the  integration  of  AI  in  learning  environ-
ments  has  been  shown  to  enhance  students’  critical  thinking  and  problem-solving
skills (Yilmaz & Yilmaz, 2023). When students regularly use Generative AI tools,
they can streamline their academic efforts, which can lead to improved performance
(Singh et al., 2024). Prior empirical research lends strong support to this association.
For example, Malik et al. (2023) demonstrated that frequent use of AI-based tools
for academic tasks significantly boosted students’ performance in research and writ-
ing  assignments.  Similarly,  Shoaib  et  al.  (2024)  found  that  using  intelligent  tutor-
ing systems and AI-driven educational platforms led to measurable improvements in
exam scores and overall academic achievement. Therefore, the following hypothesis
is proposed:

H7: The use of Generative AI positively impacts students’ academic performance.

3   Research methodology

The present study seeks to understand students’ use of Generative AI for educational
purposes by developing a theoretical model. Employing a quantitative methodology,
the research aims to test the proposed model and fulfill the study’s objectives. The
study targets university students studying at various higher education institutions in
Malaysia. Malaysia was selected as the context for this research due to several dis-
tinctive factors emphasizing its unique educational and cultural environment. First,
Malaysia’s proactive approach to integrating AI into education is well-documented,
with recent research underscoring the importance of AI literacy among students. For

instance, studies on Malaysian medical students advocate for including AI training
within curricula to better prepare graduates for the evolving demands of the work-
force (Tung & Dong, 2023). This reflects global educational trends where AI tools
are  increasingly  acknowledged  for  their  ability  to  enhance  learning  outcomes  and
foster  student  engagement  (Kadaruddin,  2023).  Consequently,  Malaysia’s  educa-
tion system stands at a critical juncture where the adoption of Generative AI could
play a transformative role in teaching practices and students’ preparedness for future
challenges.  In  addition,  Malaysia’s  cultural  framework,  marked  by  a  collectivist
orientation  and  a  high-context  communication  style,  offers  a  distinct  backdrop  for
analyzing student interaction with AI technologies. Previous research indicates that
Malaysian  students  frequently  exhibit  passive  learning  behaviors,  which  may  be
altered  by  the  interactive  and  adaptive  features  of  Generative  AI  (Alshuaibi  et  al.,
2018).  The  capacity  of  AI  to  promote  active  learning  is  further  supported  by  evi-
dence suggesting increased student participation and motivation when AI tools are
integrated into educational contexts (Ezeoguine & Eteng-Uket, 2024). Moreover, the
rising  acceptance  and  utilization  of  AI  tools  among  Malaysian  students  highlights
the timeliness of this research. Studies demonstrating positive correlations between
AI  use  and  academic  engagement  suggest  that  students  become  more  confident  in
their  learning  when  supported  by  AI  technologies,  leading  to  enhanced  academic
performance (Palmer et al., 2023; Reed & Dodson, 2024).

A  cross-sectional  online  survey  was  conducted  using  Google  Forms  to  collect
data. The survey link was distributed via university mailing lists and social media
platforms,  and  participants  were  required  to  provide  informed  consent.  Participa-
tion  was  voluntary,  with  no  financial  incentives  offered.  The  survey  was  divided
into four sections. The first section outlined the purpose of the study and included
statements on ethics and confidentiality. The second section collected demographic
data, such as gender, age, and educational attainment. The third section focused on
participants’ familiarity with Generative AI, examining their experience, frequency
of use, reasons for utilizing Generative AI, and the most commonly used tools. The
final section assessed the variables central to this research. This structure facilitated
effective  hypothesis  testing  and  enabled  the  investigation  of  relationships  among
variables (Creswell, 2014). A non-probabilistic sampling method was employed due
to the absence of an appropriate sampling framework for the target population and
the  lack  of  an  equitable  selection  process  (Sekaran  &  Bougie,  2016).  Specifically,
purposive  sampling  was  used,  meaning  only  individuals  who  had  used  Generative
AI were eligible to participate (Tongco, 2007), as confirmed by a screening question
at the beginning of the survey. Reliable and valid measurement scales were created
for each construct to examine the correlations among factors in the proposed model,
based on established sources. The constructs, items, and their sources are provided
in  the  Appendix.  Each  item  was  evaluated  using  a  five-point  Likert  scale,  with  1
indicating “strongly disagree” and 5 indicating “strongly agree”.

After the data collection process, the “Partial Least Squares-Structural Equation
Modeling (PLS-SEM)” and “Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)” were used for test-
ing  the  conceptual  model.  This  combined  approach  offers  a  robust  framework  for
understanding complex data relationships, particularly in technology adoption stud-
ies (L. Y. Leong et al., 2024). In the initial phase, PLS-SEM is used to determine

the  significant  relationships  in  the  model.  PLS-SEM  was  utilized  for  its  ability  to
handle small sample sizes and its flexibility regarding data distribution assumptions
(Hair  et  al.,  2011).  Additionally,  PLS-SEM  excels  at  estimating  complex  models
with multiple constructs, which is essential for understanding the intricate nature of
technology adoption among students (Sohaib et al., 2019). This method allows for
the simultaneous testing of measurement and structural models, providing insights
into  the  relationships  between  latent  variables  and  their  indicators  (Ibrahim  et  al.,

well-suited  for  modeling  non-linear  relationships  and  interactions  that  traditional
statistical methods may not capture. This is highly relevant in educational settings,
where numerous interacting factors influence student behaviors and attitudes toward
technology (Yang et al., 2022). ANN often demonstrates superior predictive accu-
racy compared to linear models, making it a valuable tool for forecasting outcomes
based  on  a  range  of  input  variables  (Almufarreh,  2024).  Through  ANN,  research-
ers can determine the relative importance of various factors influencing technology
adoption, offering actionable insights for educators and policymakers (Almufarreh,
2024).

4   Results

4.1   Common method variance

To mitigate the possibility of common method variance (CMV), this study utilized
survey design as a procedural control in advance (Fuller et al., 2016; Malhotra et al.,
2006). Additionally, the Harman single-factor test was applied as post-hoc statistical
controls (Howard et al., 2024; Rogelberg, 2017). The subsequent principal compo-
nent factor analysis indicated that the first factor accounted for only 45.945% of the
variance, which is well below the 50% threshold (Kock, 2021). Therefore, CMV is
not considered a significant issue in this study.

4.2   Measurement model assessment

The  research  commenced  with  a  thorough  evaluation  of  the  measurement  model,
scrutinizing the constructs for convergent validity, discriminant validity, and inter-
nal consistency reliability prior to proceeding with the structural model assessment
(Hair  Jr  et  al.,  2016).  For  convergent  validity,  the  study  inspected  both  the  “fac-
tor  loadings”  and  the  “Average  Variance  Extracted  (AVE)”.  The  factor  loadings
exceeded  the  0.7  mark,  and  AVE  values  were  above  0.5,  meeting  the  criteria  set
forth for establishing convergent validity (Hair Jr et al., 2016). Composite Reliabil-
ity (CR) and Cronbach’s Alpha (CA) were utilized to assess the constructs’ reliabil-
ity. The results, as shown in Table 1, indicate that all CA and CR values surpass the
generally  accepted  threshold  of  0.70,  thus  confirming  the  robust  reliability  of  the
constructs (Hair Jr et al., 2021; Ramayah et al., 2016).

Moreover, the discriminant validity was verified using the “Heterotrait-Monotrait
ratio (HTMT)” with a cutoff value of 0.9 (see Table 2). This more stringent crite-

below the threshold, confirming that the constructs are distinctly separate (Henseler

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the factors influencing the use of Genera-
tive  AI  tools  and  their  subsequent  impact  on  academic  outcomes.  A  theoretical
model was developed by integrating the TTF and TPB frameworks to accomplish
this aim. The model was validated using data gathered from university students,
employing a hybrid SEM-ANN approach.

The  results  supported  the  role  of  task  characteristics  in  positively  affect-
ing  task-technology  fit,  supporting  prior  outcomes  (David  &  Weinstein,  2024;
Sharma et al., 2024). This result suggests that when task demands, such as essay
writing, data analysis, or coding, are well-matched to the functionality provided
by Generative AI tools, students are more likely to perceive a strong fit between
the  task  and  the  technology  they  are  using.  For  instance,  a  Generative  AI  tool
that assists in brainstorming ideas or suggesting relevant literature for a research
paper  aligns  well  with  the  cognitive  demands  of  those  tasks.  This  finding  also
emphasizes  the  importance  of  not  only  providing  access  to  Generative  AI  tools
but  also  ensuring  that  students  are  guided  in  selecting  tools  that  enhance  their
productivity and learning based on the task at hand.

In line with prior findings (Dahri et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024), the results found
that  technology  characteristics  positively  affect  task-technology  fit.  This  means
that  when  technology  is  designed  with  characteristics  that  align  with  students’
needs—such as ease of use, adaptability, or relevant features like natural language
processing for essay writing or code generation for programming—students per-
ceive  a  stronger  alignment  between  the  tool  and  the  task.  For  example,  an  AI
tool  with  advanced  language  capabilities  can  help  students  quickly  draft,  refine,
or edit essays, thereby fitting tasks requiring writing proficiency. This alignment
between  the  tool’s  technological  attributes  and  task  requirements  is  critical  for
maximizing the effectiveness of AI in educational settings.

The results revealed that task-technology fit plays a key role in promoting the
use of Generative AI, harmonizing with prior outcomes (Dahri et al., 2024; Lousã
& Lousã, 2023). This finding suggests that when students perceive a strong align-
ment  between  the  tasks  they  need  to  accomplish  and  the  capabilities  of  the  AI
tool, they are more likely to adopt and use these tools regularly. This points to the
idea that students are not simply using Generative AI tools because they are avail-
able but because they see a direct benefit in task efficiency, productivity, or ease
of  completion.  For  example,  a  student  working  on  a  literature  review  may  find
that an AI tool capable of summarizing articles and suggesting relevant research
not only saves time but also enhances the quality of their work.

Consistent with earlier findings (S.-W. Kim & Lee, 2024; Latorre-Cosculluela
et al., 2024), our results confirmed that attitude has a positive influence on Gen-
erative  AI  use.  When  students  hold  a  positive  attitude  toward  Generative  AI—
believing  it  to  be  helpful,  innovative,  or  capable  of  enhancing  their  academic

Education and Information Technologies (2025) 30:17557–17587

17575

work—they are more likely to use it regularly and integrate it into their learning
processes. For example, if students perceive AI as an efficient tool for generating
ideas, structuring essays, or solving problems in complex subjects like mathemat-
ics or programming, they are more inclined to leverage it frequently.

The  results  revealed  that  subjective  norms  encourage  the  use  of  Generative  AI
in a positive manner. This result corresponds to previous conclusions (Bai & Jiang,
2024; Chiu et al., 2023). When students observe their classmates, professors, or aca-
demic leaders positively endorsing or frequently using Generative AI, they are more
likely  to  feel  encouraged  to  follow  suit.  For  example,  if  a  professor  integrates  AI
tools into classroom assignments or peers demonstrate how AI assists them in com-
pleting  tasks  more  efficiently,  students  may  feel  a  stronger  motivation  to  use  such
tools themselves. This social reinforcement builds a culture of AI adoption, where
using Generative AI becomes a normative, accepted, and even expected part of the
academic experience.

In accordance with previous results (AL-Nuaimi et al., 2024; Chiu et al., 2023),
it  was  revealed  that  perceived  behavioral  control  encourages  greater  use  of  Gen-
erative  AI.  This  finding  reflects  students’  confidence  in  using  Generative  AI  tools
effectively.  When  students  feel  they  have  the  necessary  competence  to  operate  AI
tools—whether  through  prior  experience,  training,  or  ease  of  use—they  are  more
likely to use them. For instance, a student comfortable with navigating AI interfaces,
understanding prompts, and interpreting outputs is more likely to use Generative AI
for tasks like writing essays, coding, or problem-solving.

It was also found that employing Generative AI contributes positively to students’
academic  performance,  conforming  to  past  findings  (Malik  et  al.,  2023;  Shoaib
et al., 2024). This finding underscores the practical benefits of integrating Genera-
tive AI tools into educational processes. By using these tools, students can enhance
their efficiency and output in tasks such as writing, research, coding, and problem-
solving, which in turn can improve their overall academic outcomes. For example, a
student might use an AI tool to generate ideas or structure an essay more effectively,
producing a higher-quality paper in less time. Similarly, AI-assisted coding tools can
help students debug or optimize their programs, leading to better results in computer
science  assignments.  Thus,  it  can  be  suggested  that  Generative  AI  can  serve  as  a
valuable supplement to traditional learning methods, offering students an additional
layer of support that helps them achieve better academic results.

While  Generative  AI  tools  provide  substantial  advantages,  such  as  boosting
productivity, aiding academic work, and enhancing learning outcomes, their use
also  presents  ethical  challenges  that  demand  careful  consideration.  One  major
concern  is  the  risk  of  over-reliance  on  AI-generated  content,  which  could  hin-
der students’ ability to cultivate critical thinking, problem-solving, and creativity
(Dwivedi et al., 2023). When students lean too heavily on AI for assignments or
research,  they  may  sidestep  fundamental  cognitive  processes  essential  for  deep
learning  and  skill  development.  Another  pressing  issue  is  academic  dishonesty
(Al-Emran,  2024).  AI-powered  tools  can  be  misused  to  generate  work  that  stu-
dents  submit  as  their  own,  violating  academic  integrity  principles  (Yusuf  et  al.,
2024).  Additionally,  some  AI-generated  content  may  contain  fabricated  refer-
ences  or  unverifiable  sources  (K.  B.  Ooi  et  al.,  2023),  further  complicating  the

ethical responsibilities are crucial. By fostering awareness and promoting respon-

6   Conclusion

6.1   Theoretical contributions

The  theoretical  contributions  of  this  study  are  multifaceted,  expanding  the  current
understanding  of  the  factors  influencing  the  use  of  Generative  AI  tools  and  their
subsequent  impact  on  students’  academic  outcomes.  First,  by  integrating  the  TTF
and TPB frameworks, this research provided a conceptual model that captures both
task-related  and  behavioral  determinants  of  Generative  AI  adoption  in  educational
settings. Previous studies have often focused on either technological factors or indi-
vidual  attitudes,  but  this  study  bridges  these  perspectives,  offering  a  more  holistic
understanding of how task characteristics, technology attributes, and individual atti-
tudes converge to drive Generative AI adoption.

Besides,  this  study  advances  the  literature  on  TTF  by  demonstrating  its  cen-
tral role in promoting the use of Generative AI tools. By showing that a strong fit
between academic tasks and AI tool functionalities increases adoption, this research
reinforces  the  importance  of  considering  both  task  demands  and  technology  char-
acteristics in educational technology design and implementation. The positive link
between  task-technology  fit  and  Generative  AI  use  supports  the  extension  of  TTF
theory in the context of AI-driven educational tools, contributing to a deeper under-
standing of how perceived fit influences technology acceptance.

In addition, the study contributes to the body of knowledge on the TPB by high-
lighting the role of social and behavioral factors, such as subjective norms and per-
ceived  behavioral  control,  in  encouraging  the  use  of  Generative  AI  tools.  These
findings emphasize that student adoption of AI is not purely a matter of individual
utility but is also shaped by external influences and perceptions of ease of use. This
expands the application of TPB within the educational technology domain, specif-
ically  in  the  context  of  Generative  AI,  and  highlights  the  need  to  consider  social
dynamics and self-efficacy when promoting technology use among students.

Further,  this  research  contributes  empirical  evidence  to  the  ongoing  debate
about  the  impact  of  Generative  AI  on  academic  performance.  By  confirming  that
the  use  of  AI  tools  positively  influences  students’  outcomes,  this  study  provides  a
concrete foundation for further exploration of the academic benefits of AI, particu-
larly in enhancing task efficiency and learning quality. This contribution is crucial
as  it  moves  beyond  theoretical  claims  to  provide  data-driven  insights  into  how  AI
tools can be effectively integrated into learning environments to support academic
success.

6.2   Practical implications

The  practical  and  managerial  implications  of  this  study  are  particularly  relevant  for
educators, academic institutions, and technology developers seeking to enhance student
learning through the integration of Generative AI tools. Educators play a central role in
supporting students’ use of AI tools. They can incorporate structured training sessions
into course curricula to ensure students understand how to use AI tools effectively for
specific academic tasks, such as research, essay writing, or problem-solving. Dedicated
AI toolkits, tailored to different tasks, can be developed and distributed among students
to guide them on best practices. Besides, educators should provide ongoing technical
support  and  create  assignment  guidelines  that  encourage  responsible  AI  use  without
compromising academic integrity.

Academic  institutions  should  introduce  comprehensive  AI  literacy  programs  that
include  hands-on  workshops,  practical  training  modules,  and  case-based  learning  to
help  students  build  confidence  and  competence  in  AI  tools.  These  programs  should
align with students’ learning objectives and course requirements, ensuring that AI tools
are readily available, easily accessible, and supported by robust infrastructure. Institu-
tions should also invest in dedicated AI resource centers, staffed with trained profes-
sionals, to serve as hubs where students can seek assistance with AI-related challenges.
To maximize adoption, institutions should promote a culture where AI use is normal-
ized,  leveraging  peer  learning,  faculty  endorsement,  and  visible  academic  leadership
to foster positive subjective norms around AI usage. Structured training sessions and
ongoing technical support can further enhance students’ perceived behavioral control,
ensuring  they  feel  confident  and  capable  of  effectively  using  AI  tools.  Additionally,
institutions should establish clear policies and guidelines that define acceptable AI use,
focusing on ethical considerations and academic integrity principles to mitigate risks of
misuse and over-reliance.

For technology developers, the findings emphasize the importance of designing intu-
itive, adaptable, and highly accurate Generative AI tools that align with students’ aca-
demic needs, especially in tasks like research and essay writing. The study confirmed
that task-technology fit positively affects the use of Generative AI, meaning that when
AI  tools  are  well-suited  to  the  demands  of  a  task,  students  are  more  likely  to  adopt
and  rely  on  them.  One  critical  factor  in  achieving  this  fit  is  improving  the  accuracy
of  AI  tools,  particularly  in  providing  reliable  and  verifiable  references  for  academic
work. Currently, many AI systems may generate plausible but incorrect or fabricated
references, which can undermine the academic integrity of students’ assignments and
research. To strengthen task-technology fit in research-related tasks, developers should
enhance  the  AI’s  ability  to  cross-check  and  verify  sources  from  credible  databases.
Ensuring that citations and references produced by AI tools are accurate and directly
relevant to the topic will make them much more suitable for tasks like writing essays or
conducting literature reviews.

6.3   Limitations and future work

While  this  study  provides  valuable  insights  into  the  factors  influencing  the  use  of
Generative AI tools and their impact on students’ academic outcomes, several limi-
tations should be acknowledged. One notable limitation of this study is the absence
of  moderators  in  the  developed  research  model.  Moderators,  such  as  demographic
variables (e.g., age, gender, or academic discipline), learning styles, or prior experi-
ence with technology, could potentially influence the relationships between the fac-
tors  in  the  model  and  the  use  of  Generative  AI  tools.  For  instance,  it  is  plausible
that students from technical disciplines, such as computer science, may perceive a
stronger task-technology fit than students from non-technical fields or that students
with  higher  digital  literacy  may  have  greater  perceived  behavioral  control  over  AI
tools. Incorporating moderators in future research would allow for a more nuanced
understanding of how different subgroups of students experience and adopt Genera-
tive AI.

While this study provides valuable insights into the factors influencing Genera-
tive  AI  use  and  its  impact  on  students’  academic  performance,  it  is  limited  by  its
cross-sectional research design. This approach captures data at a single point in time
and does not account for changes in students’ attitudes, behaviors, or academic out-
comes as they become more familiar with AI tools. Future studies could adopt a lon-
gitudinal design to examine how students’ use of Generative AI evolves over time,
how  their  reliance  on  such  tools  may  increase  or  decrease,  and  whether  sustained
use  leads  to  improvements  or  unintended  consequences  in  academic  performance.
A longitudinal approach would allow for a more dynamic assessment of the factors
influencing AI adoption and provide deeper insights into its long-term educational
impact.

Further, this study primarily explored factors affecting Generative AI use and its
positive  impact  on  academic  performance.  However,  future  research  could  delve
deeper  into  potential  challenges  or  negative  consequences  of  AI  use  in  education,
such as over-reliance on AI, ethical concerns, or its effects on critical thinking and
creativity.  In  addition,  the  findings  are  based  on  data  collected  exclusively  from
university  students  in  Malaysia.  As  a  result,  the  generalizability  of  these  findings
to other cultural or educational settings may be limited. AI adoption patterns, task-
technology  fit  perceptions,  and  behavioral  influences  may  differ  significantly  in
regions with varying cultural values, technological maturity, and educational prac-
tices.  Future  research  might  consider  replicating  this  study  across  diverse  cultural
and  educational  contexts  to  examine  whether  the  relationships  identified  are  con-
sistent or moderated by cultural dimensions such as individualism vs. collectivism,
power distance, or uncertainty avoidance.
