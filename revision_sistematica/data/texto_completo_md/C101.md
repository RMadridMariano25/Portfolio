The rapid adoption of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in higher education environments is transforming
how learners access guidance and feedback. Yet, students' AI self-efficacy (AISE) in using AI tools may critically
influence  how  they  engage  with  these  technologies.  This  study  developed  a  GenAI  agent  integrated  with  a
structured knowledge graph (KG) to provide personalized, real-time feedback in an online Preschool Health and
Hygiene course. Ninety-eight university students were grouped by AISE levels (high, medium, low) to explore
differences in AI feedback adoption, critical thinking, and cognitive load during two learning tasks. The results
indicated that high-AISE learners leveraged knowledge graph-integrated generative AI feedback more strategi-
cally,  showing  greater  self-monitoring  skills,  improved  self-evaluations, and  lower  cognitive  load  on complex
tasks. It was also found that the high-AISE group showed higher self-evaluation ratings than the low-AISE group
only on their second revised tasks, while no significant difference was found between the two groups in the first
initial  and  revised  tasks,  or  in  the  second  initial  task.  Qualitative  findings  suggest  evolving  learner-agent  re-
lationships, shifting from reliance on AI authority toward reflective collaboration. In particular, the high-AISE
group  was  more  inclined  to  perceive  GenAI  as  a  collaborative  learning  partner,  demonstrating  higher-order
abilities  in  information  evaluation  and  integration.  These  insights  highlight  the  need  to  scaffold  AI  literacy
and tailor GenAI-supported learning environments to students' AISE levels, informing future designs of adaptive
higher  education  systems.  The  findings  also  imply  the  importance  of  conducting  further  research  to  support
students in improving their AI self-efficacy in the future.

1. Introduction

The  ability  to  utilize  feedback  information  is  recognized  as  a  key
factor  influencing  student  academic  achievement  in  higher  education
(Carless & Boud, 2018). Within educational contexts, AI feedback refers
to  AI-generated  information  that  functions  as  feedback  on  learners'
work,  evaluating  performance  and  providing  guidance  for  improve-
ment;  it  is  often  mediated  by  platforms  that  support  coursework  and
assignments (Almegren et al., 2025; Alsaiari et al., 2025; Zhang & Yang,
2025).  In  recent  years,  generative  artificial  intelligence  (GenAI)  has

been widely adopted in higher education, demonstrating considerable
potential  in  feedback  provision  (Jensen,  Buhl,  et  al.,  2025).  GenAI  is
transforming  traditional  modes  of  feedback  provision  by  offering
learners timely, targeted, and in-depth suggestions, and supporting self-
evaluation and adaptive learning (Nieminen et al., 2025). AI-generated
feedback  refers  to  the  real-time,  automatically  generated  suggestions
provided by the GenAI agent supporting students' learning tasks (e.g.,
revision  suggestions,  explanations,  critiques,  and  actionable  guidance
based on students' task work). However, such AI-generated feedback is
not  necessarily  reviewed  by  experts.  Hallucinations  in  GenAI  outputs

GenAI incorporates reflective prompts, the effectiveness of its feedback
may be limited if students do not engage with it actively (Hawkins et al.,

cultivation of critical thinking as an essential skill for students to discern
and utilize such information effectively (Essien et al., 2024; Ji, Zhan,
et al., 2025; Lin, Lee, et al., 2025).

To  enhance  the  cognitive  support  and  accuracy  of  AI-generated
feedback,  several  studies  have  integrated  knowledge  graphs  (KGs)  to
help students organize knowledge, generate new ideas, and deepen their
understanding,  thereby  improving  the  effectiveness  of  feedback  utili-
zation (e.g., Zheng, Long, et al., 2023). KGs provide structured, visual
representations of domain knowledge that make semantic relationships
among  concepts  explicit  (Cui  &  Yu,  2019;  Yang  et  al.,  2025).  Conse-
quently, KGs can improve the interpretability and effectiveness of AI-
generated  feedback,  by  enabling  them  to  anchor  new  information  to
existing cognitive structures and engage more productively in reflective
learning processes (Jiang et al., 2024; Liu, Zhou, et al., 2025; Qu, Li,
Wong,  Liu,  et  al.,  2024).  A  KG  consists  of  various  semantic  units,
including  entities,  features,  attributes,  relationships,  and  knowledge
points,  and  it  is  characterized  by  structural  coherence  and  semantic
clarity, which effectively support knowledge association and contextual
reasoning (Qu, Li, Wong, Wu, & Liu, 2024; Xia & Qi, 2023). This tech-
nology has been widely applied in educational contexts such as curric-
ulum  structuring,  intelligent  question  answering,  and  learner  state
assessment  (Zheng,  Niu,  et  al.,  2023).  More  recently,  scholars  have
proposed  hybrid  models  that  combine  large  language  models  (LLMs)
with  KGs,  leveraging  their  semantic  strengths  to  enhance  contextual
understanding and interpretability within smart learning environments
(Abu-Salih & Alotaibi, 2024).

The effective utilization of AI feedback further depends on learners'
beliefs in interacting with AI, a construct termed AI self-efficacy (AISE;
Wang & Chuang, 2024). Grounded in social cognitive theory (Bandura,
1978),  AISE  refers  to  an  individual's  belief  in  their  capability  to
comprehend,  operate,  and  integrate  AI  tools  within  learning  contexts
(Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). Learners with heightened AISE
demonstrate superior task comprehension, more flexible application of
AI tools, and enhanced self-assessment capabilities (Chen et al., 2025;
Wang  et  al.,  2024).  These  students  exhibit  greater  proficiency  in
leveraging AI-generated information for critical judgment and reflective
thinking (Li et al., 2025). Their familiarity with AI interfaces reduces
cognitive  load  during  task  execution,  thereby  freeing  up  mental  re-
sources for deeper knowledge construction (Jung et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2025). Conversely, learners with limited AISE frequently lack effective
strategies for processing AI feedback, potentially resulting in confusion,
disengagement, and compromised academic performance (Wang et al.,
2024).

Although  existing  research  has  examined  students'  adoption  of  AI
technologies (Shahzad, Xu, &  Zahid, 2025; Sun &  Zhou, 2025), there
remains a lack of empirical investigation into task evaluation, critical
thinking  awareness,  cognitive  load,  and  AI  feedback  adoption  among
students  with  varying  levels  of  AISE.  In  particular,  the  impact  of
instructional systems that integrate KGs with GenAI agents on learners
with  different  AISE  levels  represents  a  significant  gap  in  the  existing
literature. To address this gap, the present study investigated differences
in the learning performance of students with varying AISE levels when
adopting  a  KG-GenAI  learning  system.  The  analysis  focused  on  task
evaluation (including self- and instructor evaluations), critical thinking
awareness,  cognitive  load,  and  AI  feedback  adoption.  This  study  not
only addresses a theoretical gap concerning the interaction between KG-
GenAI agents and students with varying AISE levels, but also provides
empirical evidence to inform the design and implementation of future
personalized intelligent instructional systems.

2. Literature review

2.1. AI feedback adoption

AI is rapidly transforming teaching and learning in higher education
(Jensen, Buhl, et al., 2025; Lin, Zou, & Liu, 2025; Zhan & Yan, 2025).
Tools such as ChatGPT and Copilot not only broaden access to learning
resources but also boost learners' motivation and efficiency through real-
time interactive feedback (Jensen, Bearman, et al., 2025; Lin, Lee, et al.,
2025). Researchers have indicated that text-based GenAI functions more
like a “cognitive enhancer,”  meaning it can reduce learners' cognitive
load on foundational and repetitive tasks. This allows students to redi-
rect their time and energy toward analyzing and solving more complex
problems with deeper critical thinking (Essien et al., 2024; Ji, Zhong,
et al., 2025). Scholars have indicated that learners with a higher critical
thinking tendency are inclined to actively seek out additional informa-
tion and revise their existing beliefs based on newly acquired evidence;
this  competency  is  also  regarded  as  a  moderating  factor  influencing
learners'  trust-building  process  when  using  GenAI  (Lin,  Zou,  &  Liu,
2025).

Furthermore,  integrating  appropriate  learning  strategies  (e.g.,  the
incremental  prompt  strategy)  with  the  use  of  GenAI  could  enhance
learners' critical thinking while reducing their cognitive load (Li et al.,
2025).  On  the  other  hand,  several  factors  may  constrain  learner
engagement  with  feedback,  potentially  inducing  a  state  of  meta-
cognitive laziness where learners become overly reliant on AI-generated
feedback (Fan et al., 2024; Zhan &  Yan, 2025). To address this issue,
Zhan and Yan (2025) identified that learners who actively process AI
feedback  typically  employ  three  key  cognitive  strategies:  selective
attention,  extraction  of  key  information,  and  comparative  analysis.
These strategies enable learners to evaluate which AI feedback merits
incorporation into their learning. Understanding the considerations that
underlie learners' decisions to accept or reject AI feedback is therefore
essential  for  explaining  GenAI's  effectiveness  in  terms  of  supporting
learning (Young & Carless, 2024).

Moreover, learners' ability to interact effectively with GenAI, as well
as to evaluate and adapt the feedback it provides, directly influences the
effectiveness of using GenAI tools for self-regulated learning (SRL) (Jin,
Lin and Lai, 2025). Hence, learners' ability to adopt, modify, and reflect
on AI-generated feedback depends on their varying AISE levels, which
may be a critical factor affecting both their adoption of and the quality of
AI feedback generated by GenAI (Zheng et al., 2025). Yet, most GenAI
tools remain focused on language generation, and lack a deep under-
standing of knowledge structures and contextual semantics. This limi-
tation often leads to semantic errors and hallucinations in the feedback
they produce  (Bearman  et  al., 2024; Zhan &  Yan,  2025). As  a  result,
improving  the  semantic  accuracy  and  conceptual  coherence  of  AI-
generated  feedback  has  become  a  key  issue  in  optimizing  its  use  in
educational settings.

2.2. Mechanisms of knowledge graph (KG)–LLM integration

KGs  have  been  widely  applied  in  higher  education  to  support
knowledge organization and learning guidance (Qu, Li, Wong, Liu, et al.,
2024;  Wang  et  al.,  2025;  Xia  &  Qi,  2024).  By  structuring  nodes  and
edges, they integrate heterogeneous information and visualize concep-
tual hierarchies and relations, helping learners build systematic frame-
works  and  improving  the  comprehension  and  retention  of  complex
concepts (Cui & Yu, 2019; Qu, Li, Wong, Wu, & Liu, 2024; Yang et al.,
2025).  Prior  research  indicates  that  KGs  facilitate  internalization  and
assist learners in visualizing complex information, which not only fa-
cilitates schema construction and information retrieval, but also reduces
cognitive load by externalizing complex interconnections that learners
would  otherwise  need  to  maintain  mentally  (Abu-Salih  &  Alotaibi,
2024;  Peng  et  al.,  2023).  In  personalized  and  adaptive  learning,  KG-
based learning path recommendations and interpretable models adjust

&  Qi,  2023;  Yang  et  al.,  2023).  As  a  semantic  scaffold,  KGs  inject
structured,  verifiable  relations  among  entities  into  LLMs,  thereby
enhancing their factual consistency and logical coherence (Jiang et al.,
2024; Liu, Zhou, et al., 2025).

Building on this premise, Liu, Zhang, et al. (2025) introduced Dual-
Reasoning  (DualR),  a  Graph  Neural  Network  (GNN)-based  external
system for performing explicit learning by referring to KGs to comple-
ment the LLM's implicit reasoning. The LLM-empowered GNN extracts
high-quality  reasoning  chains  that  are  distilled  into  knowledge-
augmented  multiple-choice  prompts,  guiding  a  (frozen)  LLM  toward
final answer selection. This explicit–implicit coupling narrows the hy-
pothesis space and localizes salient entities and relations, boosting ac-
curacy  and  robustness;  from  the  learner's  perspective,  externalizing
evidence selection and linkage reduces working-memory demands and
thus reduces learners' cognitive load. Converging evidence from Peng
et  al.  (2023) and  Omar  et  al.  (2023) also  demonstrated  that  KG-
integrated AI systems could generate higher-quality and more targeted
responses.  Similarly,  Liu,  Zhou,  et  al.  (2025) and  Zhao  et  al.  (2024)
found that KGs helped reduce hallucinations while enhancing semantic
consistency  and  logical  depth.  In  addition,  KGs  support  higher-order
cognitive  tasks  and  generative  instructional  design.  For  instance,  Qi
et al. (2025) proposed the IntelliChain framework, which integrated KGs
with  LLMs  for  chain-of-thought  dialogues,  to  improve  the  quality  of
learners'  interactions  with  intelligent  agents  and  their  learning  out-
comes. Wang et al. (2025) developed a question-generation system that
employed KGs to align with the Evaluation and Creation levels of Bloom's
cognitive taxonomy.

Within the integrated framework of LLMs and KGs, LLMs serve as
dynamic  knowledge  engines  that  leverage  their  semantic  comprehen-
sion  capabilities  to  identify  and  establish  meaningful  connections  be-
tween concepts, thereby continuously enriching the KGs. In turn, the KG
serves  as  a  structured  cognitive  framework,  providing  precisely  orga-
nized information that grounds the LLM's responses to increase students'
comprehension,  and  reduces  their  cognitive  load  when  addressing
complex  queries  from  them  (Chen  et  al.,  2025).  This  collaborative
approach offers dual benefits: it substantially reduces the risk of LLM
hallucinations  by  providing  verifiable  knowledge  anchors,  while
simultaneously  alleviating  learners'  cognitive  burden  through  clear
conceptual  organization  during  information  processing  and  complex
concept comprehension (Gaur & Sheth, 2024; Qu, Li, Wong, Wu, & Liu,
2024).  Above  all,  KGs  are  not  only  tools  for  organizing  structured
knowledge, but are also foundational frameworks that enhance the se-
mantic and logical performance of GenAI.

2.3. The role of AI self-efficacy (AISE) in AI-supported personalized
learning

Self-efficacy is an individual's belief in their capability to execute the
actions  required  for  a  task  and  to  attain  desired  outcomes  (Bandura,
1978,  1997).  According  to  social  cognitive  theory,  self-efficacy  arises
from four sources (performance accomplishments, vicarious experience,
verbal persuasion, and physiological/affective states), and reflects one's
subjective judgment of ability to complete a given task (Bandura, 1978;
Chen et al., 2025). In AI-assisted learning, AISE denotes learners' beliefs
in their ability to effectively use and interact with AI (Chen et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2025) and He et al. (2025) indicated that
AISE  is  a  key  factor  linking students'  AI-related  anxiety, literacy, and
engagement.  To  investigate  the  KG-GenAI-supported  learning  perfor-
mances  and  perceptions  of  students  with  different  AISE  levels,  two
factors, digital literacy and prior domain knowledge, need to be taken
into account. As indicated by scholars, students with higher digital lit-
eracy tend to interpret, evaluate, and apply KG-integrated GenAI feed-
back  more  effectively,  whereas  those  with  limited  digital  skills  may
experience increased cognitive load or misinterpret AI outputs (Lai &
Bower, 2019; Yaseen et al., 2025). Similarly, learners with greater prior

domain  knowledge  can  integrate  new  information  within  established
cognitive  schemas,  thereby  benefiting  more  from  AISE-supported
learning, while novices may struggle to contextualize or validate feed-
back  (Essien  et  al.,  2024;  Kalyuga,  2007).  Bewersdorff  et  al.  (2025)
highlighted  the  roles  of  AI  literacy,  interest,  and  attitudes  in  shaping
AISE. Wang et al. (2024) pinpointed AISE as the most significant pre-
dictor  of  willingness  to  engage  with  AI,  and  showed  that  it  amplifies
intrinsic  motivation while moderating  extrinsic  motivation. Similarly,
AISE has been recognized as a key variable influencing AI tool adoption,
trust, and effective use (Liang & Wu, 2010), and has been found to be
positively correlated with learners' well-being and beliefs (Huang et al.,
2024).

In  GenAI-supported  learning,  higher  AISE  is  associated  with  more
efficient  cognitive  resource  management,  stronger  critical  thinking,
better  performance,  and  greater  intention  to  continue  using  GenAI
agents (Chen et al., 2025). Furthermore, the extent to which GenAI can
foster  critical  thinking  remains  contested.  While  some  studies  have
highlighted its potential (Korchak et al., 2025; Shahzad, Xu, &  Zahid,
2025; Zhan & Yan, 2025), others have reported the possibilities of re-
ductions in depth of thinking (Fan et al., 2024; Georgiou, 2025; Jin et al.,
2025). This implies the need to examine students' learning processes and
performance within a KG-GenAI environment.

2.4. The current study

While existing research has demonstrated that GenAI can enhance
learning potential, and has identified variations in learners' behavioral
patterns  and  cognitive  strategies  during  AI interactions  (Essien et  al.,
2024; Korchak et al., 2025; Zhang & Yang, 2025), there remains a lack of
in-depth empirical investigation into how students with different AISE
levels evaluate task outcomes and feedback following interactions with
KG-GenAI  agent.  Furthermore,  little  is  known  about  how  such  in-
teractions influence their critical thinking awareness and cognitive load.
In  particular,  the  effects  of  AI-assisted  learning  activities  on  learners
with varying AISE levels remain underexplored.

To  address  these  gaps,  the  present  study  developed  a  KG-GenAI
learning system that integrated KGs with GenAI and applied it to the
course, Preschool Health and Hygiene. This system combined the language
understanding of LLMs with the semantic structuring functions of KGs to
facilitate knowledge navigation and AI feedback. The study examined
differences in the learning performance of students with varying AISE
levels with the support of the KG-GenAI agent, focusing on their task
evaluation (including self- and instructor evaluations), critical thinking
awareness, cognitive load, and AI feedback adoption.

Grounded  in  prior  research  on  AI  self-efficacy  and  cognitive
engagement,  this  study  hypothesized  that  students  with  higher  AISE
levels would demonstrate superior learning outcomes and more efficient
cognitive processing in the KG-GenAI learning environment. Building on
the hypotheses, the following research questions were raised.

RQ1. In a KG-GenAI learning environment, do students with higher
AISE levels show higher self-evaluation and instructor evaluation than
students with lower AISE levels?

RQ2. In a KG-GenAI learning environment, do students with higher
AISE levels have higher critical thinking awareness than students with
lower AISE levels?

RQ3. In a KG-GenAI learning environment, do students with higher
AISE  levels  have  lower  cognitive  load  than  students  with  lower  AISE
levels?

RQ4.  In  a  KG-GenAI  learning  environment,  how  do  students  with
different AISE levels adopt AI feedback provided by the KG-GenAI agent,
and what factors influence their adoption?

RQ5. What are the reflections of students with different AISE levels

on KG-GenAI learning?

3. KG-GenAI learning system and learning activities

This  study  developed  a  KG-GenAI  learning  system  that  integrated
GenAI with KGs, using the course, Preschool Health and Hygiene, as the
content for constructing the graph.

In terms of system architecture, the backend integrated LLMs with
structured knowledge sources, including the KG and vector embeddings.
This  study  adopted  DeepSeek  v3  as  the  base  model,  which  was  fine-
tuned  on  course  content  knowledge.  The  knowledge  structure  was
organized  around  four  main  topics  (i.e.,  Physiological  Foundations,
Nutrition and Diet, Disease Prevention, and First Aid in Nursing), and

was hierarchically decomposed into seven levels of knowledge points,
comprising 195 nodes. Two experienced instructors with over 10 years
of  teaching  experience  extracted  knowledge  points  from  the  course
chapters, which were semantically validated and academically verified
by  cross-referencing  with  English  Wikipedia  to  eliminate  redundancy
and informal content. Moreover, with expert input, logical relationships
among the knowledge points, categorized as prerequisite, post-requisite,
and  related,  were  identified  to  construct  a  structured,  semantically
connected knowledge dependency graph. This graph not only represents
the knowledge structure, but also supports concept mastery diagnosis,
semantic

In terms of system architecture, the backend integrates LLMs with
structured  knowledge  sources,  including  the  KG  and  vectors.  When  a
user submits a prompt, the system retrieves relevant information and
contextual  data  from  the  KGs  to  generate  prompts,  which  are  then
processed  by  the  LLMs  to  produce  semantically  coherent  and  inter-
pretable responses (see Fig. 1). The system employs a structured prompt
template  that  combines  user  queries  with  retrieved  knowledge  nodes
and  contextual  relationships  to  maintain  generation  consistency  and
interpretability. Teachers can subsequently make manual adjustments
based on the generated KG content to achieve satisfactory results. All
model  configurations,  parameter  settings,  and  KG  construction  pro-
cesses  are  version-controlled  to  ensure  reproducibility  and  technical
transparency.

The system comprises two databases: a learning management data-
base and a learning records database. The former records students' basic
information, course details, enrollment records, and learning activities,
while the latter stores interaction logs, self-reflection content, learning
behavior  data,  and  AI-generated  records  to  support  learning  process
tracking  and  individualized  analysis.  The  frontend  includes  the  KG-
GenAI  agent,  visualization  of  the  KGs  linked  to  the  learning  content,
personalized  learning  resources,  and  learning  path  recommendations.
Students  primarily  interacted  with  the  system  through  two  main  ap-
proaches: (1) receiving real-time feedback via the KG-GenAI agent, and
(2) engaging with semantic guidance and exploring knowledge through
the KGs. Regarding parameter configuration, the temperature was set to
0.6 to ensure logical consistency while maintaining a certain degree of
creativity, meeting the learning needs of most students. The max_tokens
parameter  was  set  to  600,  ensuring  concise  feedback  that  reduces
learners'  cognitive  load  and  enhances  response  speed.  To  minimize
redundant  information  and  improve  precision,  the  frequency_penalty
was set to 0.5, while the presence_penalty was set to 0.4 to enhance the
stability and instructiveness of the output. The top_p value was set to
0.7,  further  narrowing  the  distribution  range  of  generated  results  to
make responses more focused and consistent.

The learning activities were designed and implemented across five

stages, as detailed below:

(1).  Self-learning:  Students

independently  reviewed

the  KGs,
instructional  materials, and  videos without assistance  from the
KG-GenAI agent to develop an initial understanding of the topic
(see Fig. 2).

(2).  Plan  drafting:  Based  on  their  self-learning  outcomes,  students
drafted an initial plan to address or prevent issues related to the
topic.  They  then  conducted  a  self-evaluation  of  their  plan,  for
example,  rating  their  satisfaction  with  the  drafted  prevention
plan on a 5-point scale (see Fig. 3).

(3).  KG-GenAI agent interaction: Students engaged in at least three
rounds  of  interaction  with  the  KG-GenAI  agent  based  on  their

initial  plan,  proactively  posing  questions  and  receiving  AI-
generated feedback (see Fig. 4).

(4).  Comparison  and  reflection:  Students  compared  the  suggestions
from the KG-GenAI agent with their draft to identify similarities
and differences, decide whether to incorporate the suggestions,
and explain their reasoning. Additionally, students completed a
reflective  activity  responding  to  the  prompt:  “During  your  in-
teractions with the KG-GenAI agent, did you tend to adhere to
your own ideas or adopt the agent's suggestions? Please explain.”
(5).  Revision  and  submission:  Based  on  their  reflections,  students
revised  their  drafts  and  finalized  their  submissions.  They  also
provided a summative evaluation of the feedback provided by the
KG-GenAI  agent  and  their  overall  learning  outcomes,  for
instance, rating their satisfaction with the final prevention plan
on a 5-point scale (see Fig. 5).

4. Method

4.1. Participants

The participants in this study were first-year undergraduate students
majoring in Early Childhood Education at a university in China. After
excluding four students who did not complete the learning activities, the
final valid sample consisted of 98 students (8 male and 90 female) who
participated in a 7-week learning program. The participants' average age
was 20.3 years. The higher proportion of female students reflected the
typical gender distribution within the academic discipline. All partici-

4.4. Data analysis

For RQ1 (i.e., differences in learning performance among students
with varying AISE levels), the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to compare
self-evaluation scores among the HAISE, MAISE, and LAISE groups, as
the data violated the assumption of normality. In contrast, because the
instructor  evaluation  data  were  normally  distributed,  an  analysis  of
covariance (ANCOVA) was employed to examine group differences.

For RQ2 and RQ3 (i.e., differences in critical thinking awareness and
cognitive  load  among  students  with  different  AISE  levels),  Quade's
ANCOVA  was  adopted  to  control  for  pre-questionnaire  effects  and  to
analyze the post-questionnaire scores. This method is appropriate when
the  assumptions  of  normality  and  homogeneity  of  variance  are  not
satisfied.

To address RQ4 and RQ5, we employed a mixed-method approach
combining descriptive statistics, epistemic network analysis (ENA), and
qualitative content analysis to examine students' adoption of AI feed-
back provided by the KG-GenAI agent, the factors influencing their de-
cisions, and their reflections on its use. The qualitative analysis, guided
by grounded theory procedures (Strauss, 1987) to identify salient pat-
terns and themes underlying students' feedback-adoption behaviors and
perceptions of the KG-GenAI agent, was carried out by two researchers,

5. Results

5.1. Differences in task performance across the AISE groups: Self- and
instructor evaluations

Table  2 presents  the  results  of  students'  self-evaluation  of  their

learning tasks.

For  the  first  learning  task,  the  Kruskal-Wallis  H  test  revealed  no
significant differences among the three groups in either the initial self-
evaluation (H = 0.83, p = .660) or in the revised self-evaluation (H =
5.07, p = .079) (see Fig. 7). These findings suggest that AISE level did not
significantly influence students' self-evaluation for this task.

In terms of the second learning task, no significant difference was
found in initial self-evaluation (H = 5.78, p = .055), but there was a
significant difference in revised self-evaluation (H = 8.01, p = .018) (see
Fig.  8).  This  result  indicated  that  students'  revised  self-evaluation
differed significantly according to their AISE levels. Post hoc analysis
revealed a significant difference between the LAISE group (M = 4.52)
and the HAISE group (M = 4.88; p < .05), while no significant differ-
ences were found between the LAISE and MAISE groups or between the
MAISE and HAISE groups (p > .05).

Table  3 presents  the  results  of  instructor  evaluation  of  the  two
learning tasks. The ANCOVA results indicated no statistically significant
differences in instructor evaluation among the three groups (F = 0.25, p
= .78). These findings showed that students' AISE levels did not have a
significant effect on instructor evaluation of their final work.

5.2. Differences in critical thinking awareness across the AISE groups

To  examine  differences  in  critical  thinking  awareness  among  stu-
dents  in  the  different  AISE  groups,  this  study  employed  Quade's
ANCOVA,  which  controlled  for  pre-questionnaire  effects  to  compare
post-questionnaire scores across the three groups. The results revealed a
statistically significant difference in critical thinking awareness among
the AISE groups (F(2, 95) = 26.73, p < .001, η2  = 0.36), with a large
effect size (Pallant, 2020) (see Table 4). The mean scores of the post-
questionnaire were 3.73 (SD = 0.42) for the LAISE group, 3.89 (SD =
0.29) for the MAISE group, and 4.59 (SD = 0.48) for the HAISE group.
Pairwise  comparisons  showed  no  significant  difference  between  the
LAISE  and  MAISE  groups  (p  > .05);  however,  significant  differences
were found between the HAISE and LAISE groups (p < .001), as well as
between the HAISE and MAISE groups (p < .001). These findings un-
covered that the HAISE group demonstrated significantly higher critical
thinking awareness on the post-questionnaire compared to the MAISE
and LAISE groups.

5.3. Differences in cognitive load across the AISE groups

Quade's ANCOVA was performed to examine differences in cognitive
load  among  the  three  AISE  groups,  with  pre-questionnaire  scores
controlled  as  a  covariate  to  compare  post-questionnaire  scores  (see

5.4. AI feedback adoption and related considerations for the KG-GenAI
agent across the AISE groups

5.4.1. Students' inclination to adopt AI feedback provided by the KG-GenAI
agent

Fig. 9 illustrates the AI feedback adoption from the KG-GenAI agent
among the three AISE groups during the first and second learning tasks.
Overall, most students tended to adopt about half or the majority of the
feedback, with relatively few opting to adopt none or all of it. In the first
learning task, no students stuck to their own ideas. The majority chose to
either adopt half (LAISE: 12; MAISE: 10; HAISE: 11) or adopt the majority
(LAISE: 17; MAISE: 19; HAISE: 16) of the feedback. A smaller number of
students adopted a small portion (LAISE: 4; MAISE: 3; HAISE: 5), while
adopting fully was reported only in the HAISE group (n = 1). The dis-
tribution in the second learning task followed a similar pattern, although
the number of students who adopted fully increased slightly (LAISE: 2;
HAISE: 3). Notably, the proportion of students who adopted more than
half  of  the  feedback  rose  from  87.76%  in  the  first  learning  task  to
94.90% in the second learning task, suggesting a growing tendency to
incorporate feedback as the learning process progressed.

6. Discussion and conclusions

6.1. Discussion

This study examined the KG-GenAI learning processes of university
students  with  varying  AISE  levels,  focusing  on  their  self-evaluation,
instructor evaluation, critical  thinking awareness, cognitive load,  and
AI feedback adoption. Drawing on ENA and content analysis, the study
explored both the structural patterns of students' feedback adoption and
their perceptions of the KG-GenAI agent's strengths and limitations. The
findings indicated that AISE was a key factor influencing students' use of
learning  strategies  and  the  quality  of  human–AI  interaction.  Students
with  high  AISE  demonstrated  stronger  self-evaluation  skills,  critical
thinking awareness, and metacognitive regulation skills, enabling them
to actively interpret and integrate AI feedback from the KG-GenAI agent.
In contrast, students with medium or low AISE tended to rely on surface-
level  cues.  The  ENA  and  content  analysis  further  revealed  a  shift  in
students'  feedback  adoption  processes,  from  viewing  the  agent  as  an
authoritative source to engaging in reflective integration, underscoring
the  critical  role  of  AISE  in  fostering  active  learning  and  feedback
internalization.

While it may appear intuitive that students with higher AISE would
perform better, the self-efficacy literature does not support a universal
or  unconditional  “higher  self-efficacy  implies  better  performance”
claim. Self-efficacy is defined as a belief about one's capability to orga-
nize and execute actions to attain performance, and it does not neces-
sarily align with actual capability or skill in a domain, as indicated by
several scholars (e.g., Artino Jr., 2012; Bandura, 1997). Moreover, evi-
dence  syntheses  indicate  that  self-efficacy  and  performance  relations
can  vary  across  contexts  and  may  involve  reciprocal  influences  and
boundary conditions, rather than there being a simple one-directional
effect (Honicke & Broadbent, 2016). In AI-mediated tasks specifically,
high perceived capability or confidence may increase reliance on system
outputs;  however,  overreliance  can  reduce  vigilance  and  verification,
introducing  errors  and  weakening  performance  when  critical

6.1.2. Stronger critical thinking awareness and lower cognitive load for the
HAISE group (RQ2 and RQ3)

This study found that the HAISE group outperformed the other two
groups in both critical thinking awareness and cognitive load, suggest-
ing that AISE played a crucial role in cognitive engagement and resource
regulation.  Within  the  KG-GenAI  learning  environment,  the  HAISE
group  demonstrated  higher  levels  of  critical  thinking  awareness  and
experienced lower cognitive load. Unlike the MAISE and LAISE groups,
who relied on system support, the HAISE group could better identify and
reorganize knowledge, showing a higher level of cognitive engagement
(Essien  et  al., 2024). Moreover,  the HAISE group experienced  signifi-
cantly lower cognitive load, highlighting their ability to focus on tasks

and  regulate  resources  more  effectively  (Lin,  Lee,  et  al.,  2025).  Ac-
cording to Bandura's (1997) social cognitive theory, students with high
self-efficacy demonstrate stronger task control and learning regulation,
enabling them to identify and restructure knowledge and exhibit more
advanced  critical  thinking  skills.  Likewise,  Sweller  et  al.'s  (1998)
cognitive load theory posits that the effective use of learning strategies
can  reduce  extraneous  load.  In  addition,  Hartnett  et  al.  (2023) and
Nieminen et al. (2025) pointed out that learner self-efficacy enhances
learners'  agency  and  self-regulation  in  digital  learning  environments.
This supports the finding of this study, showing that the HAISE group
could actively interpret AI feedback without becoming overly dependent
on the KG-GenAI agent. Nonetheless, the LAISE group lacked strategy
training  and  feedback  scaffolding,  and  was  more  likely  to  passively
accept AI-generated responses and experience conceptual confusion. As
a  result, the  instructional design of the  KG-GenAI agent should move
beyond an efficiency-driven, real-time feedback model toward an educa-
tional  approach  that  prioritizes  learner  agency  and  cognitive  regulation
support (Lan & Zhou, 2025; Schwartz et al., 2016).

Moreover,  Freire  (1996) and  Dewey  (2007) emphasized  that  edu-
cation should cultivate learners' agency and their capacity for knowl-
edge  construction.  Thus,  the  KG-GenAI  agent  should  be  learning-
oriented,  supporting  critical  thinking  and  knowledge  interpretation
over mere efficiency. For instance, the LAISE group could benefit from
targeted training modules that strengthen feedback literacy, while the
HAISE group could engage in open-ended, challenging tasks aimed at

6.1.3. From perceived authority to reflective engagement: AISE-driven
adoption patterns of the AI feedback provided by the KG-GenAI agent (RQ4)
Regarding students' adoption of feedback from the KG-GenAI agent
and their considerations across different AISE levels, the ENA revealed
that although all three groups adopted more than half of the feedback,
their underlying rationales varied. In the first learning task, most stu-
dents  evaluated  the  feedback  based  on  surface-level  features  such  as
accuracy and linguistic precision, treating the AI tool as an authoritative
source of information. This aligned with Jensen, Bearman, et al.'s (2025)
tendency  to  rely  on  perceived  authority.  That  is, when  students  lack  in-
ternal  evaluation  criteria,  they  are  more  likely  to  trust  feedback  that
appears  professionally  formulated  and  linguistically  polished.  Such
behavior reflects an instrumental and compensatory use of the tool (Lin,
Lee, et al., 2025). In this study, as students gained experience with the
task and as their AISE improved, more distinct differences emerged in
how  they  perceived  and  adopted  AI  feedback.  The  HAISE  group
demonstrated  their  reflective  integration  skills  (Zimmerman,  2002),
shifting from reliance on surface characteristics. They actively selected,
adapted, and internalized feedback based on the task scenario, reinter-
preting the GenAI agent as a collaborative learning partner capable of
conversation  and  inspiration  (Jensen,  Bearman,  et  al.,  2025).  This
transformation  not  only  demonstrated  students'  capacity  to  recon-
ceptualize the role of AI and develop trust in the tool, but also reflected a
more advanced level of learning belief and cognitive engagement during
the  processes  of  strategic  adjustment  and  conceptual  construction
(Bearman et al., 2024). In addition, several system-level failure modes
were  observed,  such  as  semantic  hallucinations,  procedural  incom-
pleteness, and interaction fragmentation, which occasionally disrupted
learners'  reflective  engagement.  Addressing  these  issues  through  KG
constraint refinement and post-generation validation could enhance the
coherence  and  reliability  of  future  KG-GenAI  learning  systems  (Lee
et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2025).

The LAISE group remained reliant on surface-level features such as
linguistic clarity and perceived feasibility, demonstrating lower levels of
critical thinking and selective evaluation skills. Their feedback adoption
was primarily aimed at reducing uncertainty, reflecting a compliance-
oriented pattern. This tendency suggested that this group had limited
awareness  and  capacity  to  transform  AI  feedback  into  meaningful
knowledge resources. The MAISE group, by comparison, showed tran-
sitional characteristics. They engaged in a more balanced evaluation of
feedback,  considering  its  content  accuracy,  interactive  potential,  and
integrative value, revealing their potential in integrative and strategic
reflection.  This  group  could  be  regarded  as  a  critical  juncture:  from
instrumental trust toward collaborative trust. These observations align
with  J¨a¨askel¨a  et  al.'s  (2017) trust  construction  stage  in  human–AI
interaction. Overall, the results were consistent with Bandura's (1997)
assertion  that  self-efficacy  influences  learners'  strategic  adoption  and
regulation behaviors. The HAISE group not only exhibited more selec-
tive  and  critical  engagement  in  AI  feedback  adoption,  but  also  pro-
gressively  constructed  new  learning  beliefs  and  approaches  to
knowledge  interpretation  through  interaction  with  the  GenAI  agent.
This transformation, from knowledge consumers to knowledge constructors,
underscored that AISE was not merely a predictor of behaviors but also
played  a  pivotal  role  in  shaping  students'  learning  beliefs,  cognitive
orientations, and understanding of technology.

6.1.4. KG-GenAI agent perceived as valuable, with differentiated
expectations across AISE levels (RQ5)

This study found that students across different AISE levels recognized
the potential of the KG-GenAI agent as a learning support, particularly in

terms  of  task  feasibility  and  logical  clarity,  contextual  relevance  to
health scenarios, and knowledge support and health literacy develop-
ment. Nevertheless, students also identified several areas for improve-
ment, including the depth of feedback, integration of information, and
interaction  fluency,  which  appeared  to  influence  the  agent's  overall
practicality and user experience. In addition, while thematic responses
were generally consistent across groups, the HAISE group demonstrated
significantly  better  skills  in  the  depth  of  evaluation  and  semantic
discernment compared to the MAISE and LAISE groups. Specifically, the
HAISE group was particularly effective in terms of recognizing problems
in the KG-GenAI agent, including ambiguous expressions, information
overload, and weak strategic integration, reflecting a more critical and
integrative evaluation approach. These students tended to expect the AI
agent not only to provide information but also to support task-oriented
planning and knowledge synthesis. This finding was consistent with
Lodge et al. (2023), who argued that enhancing AI literacy or critical
thinking alone may be insufficient to harness the educational potential
of AI. These skills may need to be integrated with SRL and social SRL
(SSRL) strategies to foster deep engagement and knowledge construc-
tion among students across varying levels of self-efficacy.

Likewise, Edwards et  al.  (2025) proposed the  framework of  meta-
cognitive AI, emphasizing that AI could recognize learners' needs, guide
their  reflection,  and  support  their  strategic  adjustments,  enabling
human–AI co-learning. Additionally, Korchak et al. (2025) found that
social influence positively impacted undergraduate students' willingness
to use GenAI, with peer influence playing a particularly prominent role.
Shahzad,  Xu,  and  Asif  (2025) also  reported  that  perceived  usefulness
and ease of use moderated the relationship between AISE and trust. In
this study, although most students accepted GenAI as an ideation tool
and recognized the potential of the KG-GenAI agent, their feedback also
indicated  persistent  limitations  in  interaction  quality  and  semantic
coherence  that  require  further  refinement.  Based  on  these  findings,
future research could proceed along two directions. First, more attention
could be directed to the contextual adaptability and linguistic expression
strategies  of  AI-generated  feedback.  Particularly  for  students  with
LAISE,  the  development  of  structured  feedback  scripts  and  scenario-
based  dialogue  modules  may  help  enhance  their  comprehension  and
adoption.  Second,  informed  by  SSRL  theory,  GenAI  interface  design
could emphasize collaborative interaction. Future studies may explore
the  GenAI  agent's  role  and  limitations  in  group  decision-making,  co-
planning,  and  knowledge  co-construction,  clarifying  its  position  as  a
member of the learning community.

6.2. Limitations and future research

The learning support provided by GenAI prompts us to reconsider its
educational role and positioning. GenAI agents should not merely focus
on  operational  efficiency  and  functional  optimization,  but  ought  to
support  learners'  development  of  agency,  critical  judgment,  and
knowledge interpretation skills (Hwang & Chen, 2023; Nieminen et al.,
2025). Their  role is progressively  evolving into  a cognitive facilitator
and value guide throughout the learning process, becoming a dialogic
and collaborative partner in knowledge construction. While the findings
indicate that KG integration may be associated with improved coherence
and knowledge synthesis, these effects should be interpreted as corre-
lational rather than causal, as no GenAI-only or ablation comparison was
included.

While  this  study  provides  empirical  evidence  of  the  learning  pro-
cesses of university students with varying AISE levels supported by the
KG-GenAI  agent,  there  are  several  limitations.  First,  the  study  design
was  observational  rather  than  tightly  controlled;  we  did  not  include
experimental controls, and the sample was drawn from a single region
and academic department, with an unbalanced gender ratio, limiting the
external validity of the findings. Second, the observation period spanned
only 7 weeks and covered two learning tasks, failing to capture potential
long-term behavioral changes and learning transfer associated with the

use of the KG-GenAI agent. Third, the study focused solely on university
students,  excluding  perspectives  from  other  stakeholders  such  as  in-
structors and curriculum designers. Fourth, as most data were derived
from learners' self-reported responses, the findings may be influenced by
factors  such  as  common-method  variance  or  social  desirability  ten-
dencies. In addition, learners' perceived limitations may reflect system-
level  failure  modes,  such  as  semantic  hallucinations  (contextually
plausible but inaccurate content), procedural incompleteness (missing
preconditions  or  steps),  and  interaction  fragmentation  (abrupt  topic
shifts or incoherent transitions). Addressing these through KG constraint
refinement and post-generation validation may enhance reliability and
contextual  consistency  in  future  system  iterations.  Finally,  this  study
centered on AISE and did not explore other potential moderators. The
GenAI tool employed was limited to a single type, lacking comparative
analysis across different tools and disciplines.

Future  research  could  be  conducted  in  several  directions.  First,
incorporating additional individual variables, such as learning motiva-
tion,  mindset,  digital  literacy,  and  academic  integrity,  could  explore
their interactive effects with AISE on AI feedback adoption and learning
regulation.  Second,  longitudinal  studies  could  be  conducted  to  track
students' usage patterns and trajectories with the KG-GenAI agent across
different  learning  phases.  Third,  future  studies  could  integrate  per-
spectives from instructors and curriculum designers to investigate the
possibility  of  adaptive  feedback  strategies  tailored  to  learner  charac-
teristics, as well as the feasibility of such strategies in teaching contexts.
Fourth,  extending  the  research  to  diverse  disciplines  and  multimodal
GenAI  tools  could  strengthen  the  interdisciplinary  applicability  and
theoretical  foundations  of  the  findings.  Fifth,  incorporating  experi-
mental  or  quasi-experimental designs  with appropriate  control  condi-
tions would help establish causal inferences regarding the effectiveness
of  GenAI-supported  learning.  Sixth,  applying  mixed-method  or  multi-
source data (e.g., log data, peer evaluations, instructor ratings) could
reduce  potential  self-report  bias.  Seventh,  future  studies  could  also
adopt appropriate statistical adjustments to account for multiple com-
parisons across numerous outcomes and groupings, thereby enhancing
the  robustness  and  reproducibility  of  the  findings.  Additionally,  it  is
suggested  that  future  research  take  into  account  digital  literacy  and
prior  domain  knowledge  when  investigating  the  KG-GenAI-supported
learning performances and perceptions of students.

In instructional practice, the KG-GenAI agent should be regarded as
supplements rather than substitutes for human teachers, with their value
lying in expanding learners' opportunities for exploration and reflection.
Specifically, instructors and curriculum designers may consider incor-
porating AISE as a parameter into the design of the personalized learning
