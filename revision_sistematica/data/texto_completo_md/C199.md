In  2022,  the  release  of  ChatGPT  marked  a  significant  advancement  in  the  use  of  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)
chatbots, particularly impacting fields like computer science and education. The ability to generate code snippets
using AI chatbots has introduced new opportunities and challenges in teaching programming. However, there is
limited agreement on how students integrate them into their learning processes. This study aims to explore how
students utilize AI chatbots in the "Object-Oriented Programming" course and examine the relationship between
chatbot usage and academic performance. To address this, 231 students completed a survey assessing the fre-
quency  and  manner  of  chatbot  usage.  Descriptive  statistical  methods  were  employed  to  analyze  usage  and
perceptions, while Spearman’s correlation was used to investigate the connection between chatbot usage and
course  performance.  Results  indicated  that  students  primarily  relied  on  AI  chatbots  for  programming  tasks.
Interestingly, students’ performance negatively correlates with the reported frequency of using these tools. These
findings  provide  valuable  insights  for  programming  educators,  offering  a  better  understanding  of  students’
perceptions and use of AI chatbots. This knowledge can inform strategies for integrating these tools effectively
into computer science education.

1. Introduction

The  year  2022  marked  an  important  moment  for  Artificial  Intelli-
gence (AI), highlighted by the public release of ChatGPT. This occasion
provoked a huge interest in AI assistants and chatbots, while also raising
concerns about their potential to replace human roles and make certain
jobs  redundant.  One  domain  particularly  influenced  by  this  develop-
ment is Computer Science and its education (Denny et al., 2024; Kasneci
et al., 2023; Prather et al., 2024). The use of AI in computing education
has  sparked  widespread  discussions  regarding  its  benefits  and  risks
(Prather et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2024).

The  rapid  advancement  of  AI  has  boosted  a  rise  in  research  into
topics such as AI proficiency in university curricula (Denny et al., 2024;
Finnie-Ansley et  al., 2022; Finnie-Ansley et al., 2023; Richards et  al.,
2024), its use as a learning tool (Denny et al., 2023; Jukiewicz, 2024;
Kiesler et al., 2023), and its influence on study habits (Sun et al., 2024;
Yilmaz & Karaoglan Yilmaz, 2023a). Despite this growing interest, fewer
studies  specifically  examine  how  Computer  Science  students  perceive
and  use  AI  in  programming  courses  and  how  this  usage  affects  their
academic performance. Some research highlights that using AI chatbots

can enhance programming self-efficacy and computational skills during
the learning process (Gardella et al., 2024; Yilmaz & Karaoglan Yilmaz,
2023a), while other studies report no statistically significant differences
between  users  and  non-users  (Sun  et  al.,  2024).  In  addition,  supple-
mentary  AI-driven  educational  materials  have  been  developed  (Wu
et  al.,  2024),  with  findings  suggesting  increased  student  motivation
when interacting with such resources. Generally, students seem to rely
on  AI  tools  more  for  coding  tasks  and  less  for  explanations  or  other
purposes (Sun et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the influence of AI on student
performance  and  skill  development  in  object-oriented  programming
courses  remains  underexplored.  A  recent  systematic  literature  review
found  that  only  four  out  of  125  papers  have  examined  AI  usage  in
object-oriented programming courses (Raihan et al., 2025). Given that
object-oriented  programming  is  considered  a  threshold  concept  in
Computer Science (Boustedt et al., 2007), it is essential to focus research
efforts on this area.

The main aim of this study is to examine students’ perceptions, self-
reported usage, and opinions about AI assistants, as well as to explore
the relationship between AI chatbot usage and academic performance in
the  "Object-Oriented  Programming"  course.  Academic  performance  in

2. Background

The perceptions, usage, and opinions of students regarding AI chat-
bots  in  higher  education  have  become  a  prominent  research  topic
following AI’s recent surge in public awareness. This study, however,
focuses on computer science and programming courses—areas with a
developing body of research and conflicting findings, yet where AI tools
can provide additional support for students. For example, it has been
suggested that code-generation AI tools can aid students by providing
exemplary  solutions,  showcasing  multiple  approaches  to  solving  a
problem, and demonstrating code written with high quality and style
(Becker et al., 2023; Prather et al., 2023). However, this should be done
with caution because code generated by AI assistants often exhibits poor
style,  implying  that  their  use  for  improving  code  quality  should  be
approached  with  care  (Cipriano  &  Alves,  2024).  AI  assistants  can

every line; however, the explanations often include minor inaccuracies,

While  much  of  the  research  in  the  field  of  Computer  Science  has
concentrated  on  ChatGPT  (e.g.  Abdulla  et  al.,  2024;  Budhiraja  et  al.,
2024; Rahman & Watanobe, 2023; Shoufan, 2023), some studies have
explored other tools, such as GitHub Copilot (e.g. Finnie-Ansley et al.,
2022) and OpenAI Codex (e.g. Kazemitabaar et al., 2023; Sarsa et al.,
2022). AI-powered code explanation tools have also been integrated into
development environments. For instance, GPTutor uses ChatGPT to offer
in-editor explanations within Visual Studio Code (Chen et al., 2023). A
similar tool, acting as an AI tutor, was developed to support students
learning  object-oriented  programming  (Wang,  2023).  Both  tools
received  positive  feedback,  suggesting  that  integrating  AI  code  ex-
plainers  into  integrated  development  environments  could  facilitate
programming education, though their limitations and the potential for
errors must be clearly communicated. Additionally, AI-powered assis-
tants like WorkedGen, which generates step-by-step solution guides with
code examples and comments, have been positively evaluated by stu-
dents (Jury et al., 2024). Most students found it useful and expressed
willingness to use it for tasks and homework. These findings highlight
the promise of AI assistants in computer science and programming ed-
ucation, provided their quality is thoroughly assessed.

Research has also examined the influence of supplementary AI tools
on  students’  engagement  and  learning.  It  was  observed  that  incorpo-
rating a chatbot designed to assist students in interacting with course
content boosted their intrinsic motivation to study and engage with the
material  (Wu  et  al.,  2024).  This  heightened  motivation  holds  the  po-
tential to enhance programming education (Chang et al., 2025). How-
ever, it remains unclear whether the observed increase was specifically
due  to  the  chatbot’s  AI  capabilities  or  simply  the  result  of  having

additional  support  materials,  which  are  known  to  positively  impact
motivation.  Furthermore,  a  comparison  was  conducted  between
AI-generated  learning  materials  and  resources  created  by  students
(Denny  et  al.,  2023).  The  findings  revealed  that  students  rated  both
types of materials as equally valuable for their learning.

The  adoption  of  AI  tools  by  university  students  was  investigated
using the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology frame-
work  (Strzelecki,  2023)  which  highlighted  that  students’  habits,  per-
formance expectations, and hedonic motivation played a significant role
in  their  use  of  AI  chatbots.  Essentially,  when  students  found  AI  tools
effective and developed habits of consulting them, their usage became
more  frequent.  Similarly,  it  was  found  that  positive  experiences  and
accurate responses from AI chatbots encouraged consistent usage among
students (Lai et al., 2023). Moreover, an increase in perceived useful-
ness, ease of use, and intention to use AI assistants after engaging with
them has been observed among students (Sun et al., 2024). However,
their overall attitudes toward AI tools remained unchanged, suggesting
that while students may grow more accustomed to and reliant on these
tools,  their  broader  perspectives  on  AI’s  role  in  education  do  not
necessarily shift. Furthermore, this can depend on the student’s disci-
pline, as students in technology and engineering fields reported signif-
icantly  greater  familiarity  with  ChatGPT  compared  to  their  peers  in
social sciences, humanities, medicine, and natural sciences (St¨ohr et al.,
2024). They also used AI chatbots more frequently and demonstrated
the lowest level of concern regarding the ethical implications of AI usage
in education. However, some students may express concerns about the
potential  misuse  of  AI  chatbots,  particularly  fearing  accusations  of
plagiarism (Espartinez, 2024; Kizilcec et al., 2024). In addition, a trend
was found that the use of AI tools is perceived as more appropriate in
courses focused on applying programming rather than learning to pro-
gram, while also revealing that students remain interested in learning,
often  expressing  skepticism  about  AI  responses  and  recognizing  inac-
curacies or unhelpful outputs (Keuning et al., 2024).

Speed and constant availability have been identified as the primary
benefits  of AI chatbots for  students, due  to their capacity  to expedite
problem-solving and learning (Abdulla et al., 2024; Schefer-Wenzl et al.,
2025; Yilmaz & Karaoglan Yilmaz, 2023b). Some students even began
perceiving AI as a teacher or on-demand tutor, reflecting a high level of
reliance  (Budhiraja  et  al.,  2024).  Furthermore,  students  increasingly
perceive  AI  assistants  as  essential  tools  for  seeking  help  when  facing
challenges, even to the point of preferring to consult them instead of
asking  their  teachers  (Keuning  et  al.,  2024,  pp.  1–12;  Prather  et  al.,
2024).  However,  drawbacks  included  occupational  anxiety  and  in-
stances of incorrect answers. Students predominantly utilized AI tools
for  coding-related  tasks,  such  as  writing  and  debugging  code,  while
using them less for activities like task decomposition or improvement of
theoretical  understanding  (Kizilcec  et  al.,  2024;  Sun  et  al.,  2024).
However,  in  other  studies,  students  have  reported  relatively  limited
chatbot  use  for  tasks  such  as  generating  code  compared  to  its  more
frequent  application  for  explaining  concepts  or  generating  ideas  (Lee
et al., 2024; Lyons et al., 2024). In addition, ChatGPT has been used for
searching for the appropriate syntax, identifying the right libraries to
import,  and  pinpointing  runtime  errors  in  programs  (Abdulla  et  al.,
2024). It has also been discovered that students place less emphasis on
using  generative  AI  tools  as  support  resources  in  introductory  pro-
gramming courses compared to more advanced courses (Keuning et al.,
2024) and they tend to use ChatGPT more for solving complex problems
compared to simple ones (Rahman &  Watanobe, 2023). These contra-
dictory results might suggest that students do not yet have strong as-
sociations with the use of AI assistants.

explored but research remains inconclusive regarding the effect of AI on
academic  performance.  Some  studies  (Abdulla  et  al.,  2024;  Gardella
et  al.,  2024;  Kazemitabaar  et  al.,  2023;  Yilmaz  &  Karaoglan  Yilmaz,
2023a) indicate that AI assistants seem to enhance the performance of

3. Methods

3.1. Context

Object-oriented programming (OOP) is a commonly used paradigm
in  contemporary  programming.  At  the  University  of  Tartu,  first-year
university students learn this paradigm in the course "Object-Oriented
Programming," which is taught using the Java programming language.
"Object-Oriented  Programming"  is  primarily  aimed  at  first-year  Com-
puter  Science  students,  as  it  is  a  mandatory  part  of  their  curriculum.
However, it is also a popular elective among students from other fields,
resulting in an annual enrollment of 270–330 students. Serving as the
second programming course in the curriculum, it requires students to
have  completed  an  introductory  Python  programming  course  before-
hand (Leping et al., 2009). Since prior experience with object-oriented
programming or Java is not expected, most students begin the course
as complete novices in both the language and the paradigm.

The course runs for 16 weeks and includes weekly lectures, home-
work  assignments,  and  practice  seminars,  all  following  a  flipped-
classroom model (Lepp &  T˜onisson, 2015). Each week, students must
watch lecture videos, complete a brief quiz, work on homework related
to the weekly topic, and participate in practice seminars to deepen their
understanding.  Most  homework  assignments  include  automated  tests
that offer instant feedback, enabling students to resubmit their work if
necessary. Over the course, students are required to complete two major
programming tests, two group projects, and a final exam (in the form of
a quiz).

Students  are  allowed  to  use  AI  chatbots  for  their  homework  and
group assignments. In the materials, it is even advised to ask AI for ex-
planations and help, e.g. "If you don’t understand the code, you can ask
an  artificial  intelligence  (ChatGPT,  Copilot,  or  something  else)  to
explain this logic to you." or "You can also ask an artificial intelligence
for help to find the location of the mistake." However, it is not allowed to

use AI assistants during programming tests and the exam. Students are
notified about this at the beginning of the course, as well as before the
tests and the exam. During the first lecture, it is stressed that students
should not rely too much on AI when solving homework assignments.

3.2. Participants and data collection procedures

The  sample  consisted  of  students  enrolled  in  the  "Object-Oriented
Programming" course during the spring semester of the 2023/2024 ac-
ademic  year,  comprising  323  students,  of  whom  70%  were  male  and
30% were female. Students’  use of and opinions on AI assistants were
investigated through a survey conducted via the course Moodle page.
The survey took place in the 8th week, approximately at the midpoint of
the course. The survey consisted of six sections, with most focusing on
general  feedback  about  lectures,  seminars,  tests,  and  homework  as-
signments,  including  a  question  about  the  perceived  difficulty  of  the
homework  assignments  on  a  5-point  Likert  scale  ranging  from  "Too
difficult" to "Too easy". One of the sections was dedicated to AI assis-
tants.  Participation  in  the  survey  was  voluntary,  but  completing  it
awarded  students  a  bonus  point.  A  total  of  231  students  responded,
representing  about  72%  of  the  course  participants.  Out  of  the  re-
spondents,  157  (68%)  were  male  and  74  (32%)  were  female,  which
reflects a typical computer science student population in terms of gender
distribution (Berdousis & Kordaki, 2019; Womentech, 2023). While the
survey was not anonymous—allowing bonus points to be assigned and
responses  to  be  linked  with  individual  scores—full  anonymity  was
maintained during the analysis of the results.

The AI section of the survey included a mix of single- and multiple-
choice  questions,  open-ended  responses,  and  Likert  scale  items.  It
began with a single-choice question asking whether the student had used
any AI assistants for the course. Depending on their answer, students
were presented with different follow-up questions:

• Non-users answered a multiple-choice question about their reasons
for not using AI assistants, with four predefined options: "I want to
learn on my own  and don’t  want to use aids."; "I haven’t thought
about using them."; "I’m afraid of being accused of plagiarism."; "I’m
afraid that the topics will remain unlearned by using them." Addi-
tionally,  they  had  the  opportunity  to  elaborate  in  an  open-ended
response.

• AI users were asked eight questions about their experiences with AI
assistants. First, the frequency of use was measured with a single-
choice  scale  ("Once,"  "A  couple  of  times,"  "Every  other  week,"
"Almost every week," "Every week"). Secondly, students were asked
to evaluate on a 5-point Likert scale (1 - not at all … 5 - always) the
level  of  help  they  received  from  the  assistants.  Next,  students  re-
ported  specific  tasks  for  which  they  used  AI  with  five  predefined
options: "Understanding code examples," "Solving homework tasks,"
"Answering lecture quizzes," "Doing the group project," and "Study-
ing for the test." Students also answered a multiple-choice question
about the ways of using AI assistants, with five predefined options:
"To get ideas," "To get answers to theory questions," "To find errors in
my own code snippet," "To get code solutions when programming,"
"To explain the logic of the existing code snippets." The section also
included three open-ended questions, allowing students to elaborate
on what they liked and disliked about AI assistants and to describe
how they integrated these tools into their learning process. To assess
students’  perceptions  of AI assistants, a  set  of five  statements was
included, where respondents indicated their level of agreement using
a  5-point  Likert  scale  ranging  from  "Strongly  disagree"  (1)  to
"Strongly agree" (5).

Additionally, points for programming test 1, programming test 2, the
exam and total points were obtained for the 231 students who responded
to the survey. The programming tests are worth 16 points each, with a
passing score of 12 points for programming test 1. The exam is worth 33

3.3. Data analysis

A mixed-method approach was used for the study, using qualitative
data to obtain an in-depth understanding of the results of the quanti-
tative  data  analysis.  The  Python  Pandas  library  was  used  for  data
analysis. The survey questions were analyzed using descriptive statistics.
As  the  Shapiro-Wilk  test  showed  a  significant  departure  from
normality for all course points (W(231) in range .56-.78 and p < .001 in
all cases) and ordinal scales were used in the survey, Spearman’s rank
correlation  analysis  was  performed  to  examine  the  strength  of  the
relationship between different scores and reported use and helpfulness.
The  interpretation  of  the  r  value  is  performed  according  to  Akoglu
(2018),  where  Spearman’s  correlation  coefficient  is  interpreted  as  a
weak relationship (r < 0.29), moderate relationship (0.30 < r < 0.39),
strong relationship (0.40 < r < 0.69), and very strong relationship (r >
0.70).

Inductive  thematic  analysis  (Braun  &  Clarke,  2006)  was  used  to
analyze  students’  responses  to  open-ended  survey  questions.  This
approach allowed us to identify patterns in students’  answers without
imposing  predefined  categories.  It  included  multiple  readings  of  re-
sponses to gain familiarity with the data, the assignment of initial codes
to describe key aspects, and the iterative grouping of these codes into
broader  categories.  Both  authors  qualitatively  coded  the  open-ended
answers, and any differences were thoroughly discussed between them
until a common ground was found. The inter-rater reliability score was
84%.

5. Discussion

The first research question examined the extent and manner in which
students  utilized  artificial  intelligence-assisted  methods  during  the
course. Our findings align with previous research (Rahman & Watanobe,
2023; Schefer-Wenzl et al., 2025), showing that approximately 80% of
students  experimented  with  AI  assistants.  However,  the  limited  fre-
quency of weekly usage (3.9%) suggests that while students recognize
AI’s value, they have not fully integrated it into their learning routines.
This contradicts concerns that AI might quickly become indispensable
and lead to dependency (Denny et al., 2024; Espartinez, 2024; Keuning
et al., 2024). Instead, students use AI selectively, primarily for debug-
ging  and  understanding  code  rather  than  generating  entire  solutions,
mirroring prior research (Becker et al., 2023; Kizilcec et al., 2024; Sun
et al., 2024).

A particularly interesting insight is that non-users primarily cited a
desire to learn independently as their reason for avoiding AI tools. This
aligns with findings from Yilmaz and Karaoglan Yilmaz (2023b), which
identified concerns over learning integrity in programmer discipline as
key reasons for AI avoidance. However, this raises a deeper question: Is
AI use perceived as a shortcut that undermines fundamental learning?
Prior  research  suggests  students  are  more  comfortable  using  AI  in
courses where learning to program is not the primary objective (Keuning
et al., 2024). This distinction could explain why many students hesitate
to use AI even when it offers efficiency.

The impact of AI on student-instructor interactions presents another
nuanced finding. While previous studies suggested that AI tools could
replace traditional help-seeking behavior (Keuning et al., 2024; Prather
et al., 2024), our results indicate a more complex dynamic. Some stu-
dents  found AI useful for  initial troubleshooting, potentially reducing

8

the demand for teaching assistants. However, a notable proportion of
students still sought human guidance, reinforcing the idea that AI should
complement, rather than replace, traditional instructional support. This
suggests that AI integration should be designed to enhance, rather than
undermine,  student-instructor  engagement,  a  concern  also  noted  by
Prather et al. (2023).

Regarding  motivation,  most  students  remained  neutral  about
whether  AI  assistants  encouraged  them  to  solve  more  homework,
diverging  from  previous  findings  where  course-specific  chatbots
enhanced motivation (Wu et al., 2024). This discrepancy may stem from
the lack of a tailored AI assistant for this course, highlighting that while
AI can enhance engagement in structured settings, general-purpose AI
tools may not significantly boost motivation across all students. How-
ever, those who agreed with the statement suggest that AI can still serve
as a motivational aid for certain learners.

The last research question is connected to the relationships between
the reported use and perceived helpfulness of AI assistants and students’
performance in the course and attitudes towards AI assistants. One of the
most  significant  findings  of  this  study  is  the  negative  correlation  be-
tween AI usage frequency and academic performance. Unlike previous
studies that found either a neutral or positive effect (Abdulla et al., 2024;
Deng et al., 2024; Kazemitabaar et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2024), our re-
sults suggest that frequent AI users tended to struggle more with cour-
sework. While one plausible explanation is that students who perceive
the  course  material  as  difficult  rely  more  on  AI  as  a  supplementary
aid—aligning with prior findings that lower-performing students tend to
use additional resources more frequently (Lepp & Kaimre, 2022)—other
factors could also contribute to this trend. For instance, over-reliance on
AI  for  problem-solving,  regardless  of  a  student’s  ability  level,  may
hinder the development of fundamental programming skills, which was
also  suggested  by  prior  research  (Lehmann  et  al.,  2024;  Smith  et  al.,
2024). This raises the question of whether students in our study were
using AI as a supplement to their learning or as a primary tool for solving
problems.  Future  research  should  differentiate  between  these  usage
patterns, as the impact of AI may vary based on how it is integrated into
the learning process. Additionally, structured AI usage—such as guided
AI-assisted  exercises—could  mitigate  this  performance  gap.  Wu  et  al.
(2024) found  that  AI-enhanced  coursework  improved  motivation  and
engagement when AI was embedded within course design. Rather than
students  relying  on  AI  independently,  they  may  benefit  more  from
structured integration of AI tools into the curriculum, ensuring that AI
serves  as  a  support  mechanism  rather  than  a  substitute  for
problem-solving and conceptual understanding. Further investigation is
needed to determine whether instructional scaffolding can help students
develop programming competence while leveraging AI effectively.

Beyond performance, AI usage was strongly linked to student atti-
tudes  toward  learning.  Frequent  users  reported  less  struggle  with
homework and increased motivation, supporting previous research on
AI’s  role  in  enhancing  engagement  (Chang  et  al.,  2025;  Espartinez,
2024; Kasneci et al., 2023). However, a concerning trend emerged: the
more students used AI, the more they bypassed alternative solutions and
course materials. Notably, this behavior was unrelated to perceived AI
helpfulness, suggesting that some students turned to AI assistants even
when  they  did  not  find  them  particularly  useful.  This  raises  critical
questions about the role of AI in shaping study habits—whether it serves
as  a  beneficial  support  tool  or  fosters  over-reliance  remains  an  open
issue for further investigation.

and drawbacks, and their potential influence on student performance.

The study also underscores the importance of targeted support for
weaker students who are more inclined to rely on AI assistants and other
supplementary materials. Prior research has suggested that struggling
students  often  turn  to  external  aids,  but  this  study  explicitly  links  AI
chatbot  "dependency"  with  lower  academic  performance,  raising  con-
cerns about the unstructured use of AI tools. Educators should recognize
this  trend  and  implement  additional  interventions,  such  as  offering
guidance on how to critically evaluate AI-generated solutions or inte-
grating  AI  tools  into  classroom  instruction  in  a  way  that  enhances
learning.

Furthermore,  this  study  highlights  potential  risks  associated  with
overuse as the observed negative correlation between frequent AI usage
and  academic  performance  may  still  suggest  that  unstructured  or
excessive reliance on AI tools can inhibit learning outcomes. To address
this,  educators  can  implement  practices  that  balance  AI  usage  with
traditional  problem-solving  strategies,  such  as  offering  tasks  that
encourage students to compare AI-generated solutions with their own
work.

Finally,  concerns  about  AI  limiting  the  exploration  of  alternative
solutions  highlight  the  need  for  educators  to  encourage  creative
problem-solving  by  designing  tasks  that  require  students  to  critically
engage with AI responses and generate multiple approaches to a prob-
lem. Additionally, improving AI tool reliability or training students to
identify  and  manage  AI  errors  could  help  address  frustrations  and
improve the overall effectiveness of these tools.

7. Limitations and future research

A key limitation of this study is its focus on a single course, a single
university  and  one  academic  year,  due  to  the  relatively  recent  emer-
gence of AI chatbots. The policies and rules set by the university may
have  influenced  students’  perceptions  and  behaviors,  limiting  the
generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the study was conducted
in  the  context  of  an  "Object-Oriented  Programming"  course,  where
problem-solving  and  coding  tasks  play  a  central  role.  The  extent  to
which these findings apply to other computer science courses, such as
theoretical computer science or human-computer interaction, remains
uncertain. Additionally, while the study does not specify a particular AI
chatbot, the timeframe suggests that most students likely used ChatGPT.
Differences in AI assistant capabilities, such as variations in model up-
dates,  interface  design,  or  institution-specific  AI  tools,  could  further
impact the generalizability of the results. Expanding future research to
include multiple courses, universities and academic years would provide
a broader perspective. In addition, future research could explore how to
better integrate AI into learning environments and assess its long-term
impact on student performance and problem-solving abilities.

Secondly, the sample of students who responded to the survey could
have been biased towards those who were more motivated to participate
due  to  the  incentive  offered—a  bonus  point  for  completing  the  ques-
tionnaire—which required students to supply their names. Despite as-
surances of confidentiality, some students might have been hesitant to
disclose  using  AI  assistants  for  unapproved  purposes  in  the  course,
possibly  due to  concerns  about potential  negative  consequences. This
social desirability bias could lead to underreporting of AI use in certain
contexts or misrepresentation of how students actually engaged with the
technology. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the broader
student  population.  Future  research  could  mitigate  this  limitation  by
allowing truly anonymous participation to gain a more comprehensive
understanding of student perceptions and usage of AI tools.

Thirdly, students’ self-reports may not always accurately reflect their
actual  behavior.  This  may  influence  the  results  in  various  ways;  for
instance, these respondents might overestimate or underestimate their
perceived  usefulness  and  impact.  Future  research  could  address  this
limitation  by  combining  self-reported  data  with  alternative  methods,
such  as  analyzing  usage  logs  or  conducting  qualitative  interviews,  to
gain  a  more  accurate  and  nuanced  understanding  of  AI  usage  in
educational settings.

While this paper primarily examined students’ use of AI tools, it is
important  to  note  that  AI  tools  are  also  accessible  to  instructors.
Exploring how teaching and grading automation can be integrated with
AI assistants offers another promising direction for research that could
yield valuable findings.

8. Conclusion

The aim of this paper was to explore how students utilize AI-based
chatbots in the "Object-Oriented Programming" course and to examine
the relationship between their usage and academic performance. Data
was  collected  through  a  questionnaire,  focusing  on  frequency  of  use,
methods  of  use,  and  the  perceived  impact  on  learning.  The  findings
revealed that the majority of students used AI assistants, primarily for
coding-related tasks such as debugging and understanding code exam-
ples. However, only a small fraction reported using AI assistants on a
weekly  basis,  suggesting  that  students  have  not  yet  developed  a  de-
pendency on these tools. Students appreciated the AI assistants’ speed
and  availability  but  expressed  frustration  with  errors  and  mis-
interpretations in the responses provided by the tools. Although some
students raised concerns that AI chatbots might limit their exploration of
alternative solutions, the results indicated that overall, AI tools did not
reduce the variety of solutions students attempted and instead provided
helpful support for studying course materials. Furthermore, students did
not feel that using AI tools prevented them from learning the material or

exploring  different  approaches  to  problem-solving.  These  insights
contribute to the ongoing discussion on the pedagogical integration of AI
tools,  highlighting  the  need  for  educators  to  help  students  critically
assess  AI-generated  content  and  engage  with  it  actively  rather  than
relying on it uncritically.

A  key  finding  of  this  study  is  that  frequent  AI  assistant  use  was
negatively correlated with academic performance. While this could be
explained by weaker students relying more heavily on AI tools, it also
raises important pedagogical questions about whether unstructured AI
use  supports  or  hinders  learning.  While  prior  research  has  provided
contradictory results on the use of AI in education, this study contributes
by  providing  quantitative  evidence  that  frequent  AI  use  does  not
necessarily  translate  into  better  academic  outcomes  in  programming
courses.

The results of our study give new insights into how students perceive
and use AI chatbots. These insights can be useful for other instructors of
Computer  Science  courses,  offering  a  better  understanding  of  the  re-
lations between students and AI-based tools. The findings of the study
can  enhance  learning  for  students  using  AI  and  benefit  education  by
aiding  other  educators  and  researchers  in  adapting  AI  assistants  for
teaching  and  learning.  This  knowledge  can  help  inform  changes  to
course design and educational practices.
