1. Introduction
This article contributes to the debate on the impact of the concept of generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) in the context of
higher education and assessment, an impact that may be transformative but at the same time needs to be diligently embraced as an
opportunity to develop educational practice (Lim et al., 2023; Ratten & Jones, 2023; Wang, 2024). While there are recent studies (e.g.,
Shahzad et al., 2024) which highlight positive student perceptions of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology, and its contribution to
enhanced academic performance, these studies recommend further investigation on the use of AI in diverse cultural or varied higher
education demographics.
Our study was motivated by concerns that GenAI significantly disrupts higher education, particularly affecting learning practices
and assessment methods, as well as raising questions around authorship and originality (Chatterjee & Dethlefs, 2023; Stokel-Walker,
2022; Sweeney, 2023). We also recognised that widespread adoption of AI (Kumar et al., 2023) requires management educators to
rethink how we prepare leaders for a future where work, and the workplace, are transformed by AI and other innovative technologies

As AI technologies will be an essential part of future workplaces, prospective business professionals must have opportunities to
familiarise themselves with the use of AI tools in their respective fields. Our case study adds to a growing body of literature that
discusses how educators can incorporate AI into their teaching, while also managing the various challenges posed by AI (see Xu &
Babaian, 2021). While some argue the recent rise of GenAI undermines the credibility of assessment processes and university degrees
(Becker & Kress, 2023; Lukpat, 2023; Wood, 2023), others note the opportunities that GenAI offers to students, educators, and re-
searchers (Crompton et al., 2020; Johnston et al., 2024), emphasising that it is important to encourage students to work appropriately
with new GenAI instruments, to ensure a motivating and high-quality learning experience (Banihashem et al., 2023; Bearman et al.,
2024; Sparrow, 2023). Developing a responsible application of AI technology in management education requires a focus on several key
priorities, including changing the approach to teaching, to encourage students to develop expansionist rather than reductionist
mindsets (Fischer & Dobbins, 2024), and to intensify and expand research on AI in assessment and learning (Niemi, 2020, 2021;
Roschelle et al., 2020). We further suggest that the responsible use of AI is fundamental to a broad ambition of delivering responsible
management education, which depends upon innovation to ensure a sustainable future in accordance with UN sustainable develop-
ment goals (Lim et al., 2024).
Our project is situated in a research-intensive UK business school, selected not only for its three-pronged policy on GenAI in as-
sessments (it is the lecturer’s decision to forbid, authorise, or encourage the use of GenAI in assessments), but also for its diverse
student body, including students for whom English is an additional language; together these provide a broad scope for potential GenAI
usage by students. We focus on a specific module in which student reflection on GenAI use was encouraged and built directly into the
assessment, with the students’ assessments providing the data studied in this research.
In May 2023 as part of their Digital Marketing module 118 postgraduate management students were asked to reflect in 500 words
on their use of GenAI for their overall 2500-word essay-style assessment. Students’ claims on their use of GenAI for the assessment are
contrasted with educators’ evaluation of the students’ use of AI for the same assessment.
As well as contributing to the growing literature around the use of GenAI in higher education, we highlight the utility of incor-
porating a sensemaking approach into educational practice, including the implications for assessments. In line with other educational
research based on sensemaking (e.g., Lim & Kumar, 2023; Preuss et al., 2023), for the educator’s perspective we draw on Preuss et al.’s
(2023)interpretation of Daft and Weick’s (1984)theory on sensemaking to develop a three-stage taxonomy that displays different
stages of students’ AI engagement. Sensemaking is a three-level approach that positions scanning and generating information and
knowledge as a first level, interpreting and rephrasing information as a second level, and critiquing and evaluating actions as the third
level.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: first, we outline the research methodology of the case study; then we present
our findings, beginning with a review of the students’ claims of how they used GenAI, before explaining how, for the same assessment,
the assessors evaluate students’ use of GenAI. Next, we discuss our findings and conclude with recommendations to management
educators and policymakers for setting assignments that encourage responsible and reflective use of GenAI.

2.1. Research design
The case study pertains to a research-intensive UK business school’s postgraduate Master’s degree programme and a specific course
(Digital Marketing) for which students’ learning outcomes were partly measured by an assessment that incorporated AI-related
knowledge. The course encouraged students to interact with AI tools (in line with the School’s general guidance on the use of AI)
both in the classroom and in assessments.
2.1.1. The assessment
The aim of the assessment was to connect teaching and learning, to reflect authentically on the demands of the ‘real world’ (Lund,
1997, p. 25), and to help students prepare for future employment in environments substantially shaped by burgeoning AI technologies
(Brown & Sambell, 2021; Lim, 2023; Ratten & Jones, 2021; Sparrow, 2023). Considering that formal academic essays are frequently
seen as not reflecting the skills that employers expect (Flaherty, 2021; Tomlinson, 2022), we aimed to provide a creative assessment
design that provided opportunities for reflective and idiosyncratic contributions (Tomlinson, 2022), and that gave students the op-
portunity to publish their work online on social media post-submission, for example via LinkedIn.
Specifically, students were asked to write an authentic assessment in three parts (see Fig. 2), with each part aimed at different
audiences on a topic of their choice within the course. Part 1 was a scholarly blog post of 1500 words, ready to be published on social
media after submission on the assessment portal, where students had to position themselves as a subject-matter expert demonstrating
professional competence. Students were explicitly asked to emphasise analysis and critical thinking and to illustrate their points using
examples. Part 2 of the assessment consisted of a 500-word board briefing paper, simulating formal business-style writing addressed to
senior executives. The board briefing paper required students to request funding from the leadership team for a digital marketing
initiative related to the topic they had chosen for Part 1 of their assignment. In the third part, crucial to this research, students were
asked to write a 500-word essay reflecting on how they used AI, including GenAI, for their assignments. In doing so students were
explicitly asked to copy and submit the prompts they used and the (refined) conversations they had with GenAI into the appendix. It is
school policy to exclude students’ appendices from the wordcount.
2.1.2. Assessment criteria
When writing their assignments, we assumed that the students were motivated to get a good grade based on the assessment criteria
of the assignment, which were the standard criteria set by the business school for all written assignments: (1) Comprehension, (2)
Analysis, (3) Critical Evaluation, and (4) Academic Writing.
Students received detailed information on the assessment criteria. ‘Comprehension’ was defined for the course as demonstrating an
understanding of digital marketing generally, including its positioning in a specific organisational context, as well as in-depth
knowledge of literature in one area of theory or technology application in marketing. Showing an in-depth understanding of the
theory and/or technology (ies) used, was listed as important for this criterion. ‘Analysis’ was defined as presenting logical arguments
supported by well thought-through and convincing evidence, including ‘visuals’, such as images. Creativity, design and aesthetics of
the blog were evaluated as part of this criterion too. ‘Critical Evaluation’ was defined as showing capacity for original thought by
questioning relevant arguments from contrasting literature, as well as AI, and their implications. And finally, ‘Academic Writing’ was
defined as presenting a clear and structured assignment; using relevant literature founded in academic honesty for referencing and
citation, as well as showing effective writing skills for different audiences.

2.2. Data collection
Research ethics approval from the relevant business school was in place to allow us to review the assignment submissions of the
course after completing the marking process, with a commitment to ensure that students’ identities and demographics remain un-
disclosed and anonymous. Data was therefore collected from all students who had submitted assignments for the Digital Marketing
course, representing 118 unique reflective essays. Students and staff of the business school used an online virtual learning environment
(an intranet web portal) to manage all their learning and teaching activities such as access to the course learning material, assessment
question, assessment submission, student feedback and final marks. The data was submitted in May 2023, marked by members of the
module team using an online marking system that preserved student and assessor anonymity, and grades were released in July 2023.
2.3. Data analysis
This case study employed an inductive qualitative research design, adhering to a bottom-up approach as outlined by Yin (2014, p.
282). This entailed analysing the content of the written assignments to identify patterns and trends that could provide insights on the
use of AI in assessments. To analyse the deployment of AI and the stage of skills students demonstrated, we categorised assessments
according to predetermined evaluation criteria (EC) that captured various attributes related to the use of AI exhibited in the essays.
These categories were then mapped to the three stages of the sensemaking educational taxonomy (Daft & Weick, 1984; Preuss et al.,
2023). In addition to mapping students’ assignments onto the taxonomy we calculated the percentage of students belonging to each of
the three stages of the sensemaking taxonomy. We repeated the analysis twice, once based on the students’ own explicit claims and
once based on the educators’ evaluation of the skills displayed.
2.3.1. Evaluation criteria
To devise the evaluation criteria (EC) for this research we inductively analysed the students’ submissions and categorised the
emerging themes according to questions such as: What kind of AI tools did the students use? For what purposes? Did they focus on AI
purely in the context of the assignment, or did they learn lessons for the future? We developed six overarching ECs, each with several
sub-criteria.
For our first EC, we reviewed which AI tools were explicitly referred to in the 500-word reflective essays. ChatGPT, Gemini (called
Bard at the time of our project), Grammarly and the school’s own AI-based Essay Analyst tool were introduced in the course. Other AI
tools that were referenced, were collected as an additional group. This led to the formation of the first EC and sub-criteria that we show
in Appendix A. The overarching question for this EC was whether students actually claim usage of the various tools.
For the second EC, we reviewed how students expressed perceived acceptance (or non-acceptance) of AI tools in general. Using
ideas from the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) as a guide (Davis, 1989), we examined both AI’s perceived usefulness and its
perceived ease-of-use, consistent with a positive perspective on the sometimes-contested utility of TAM (Lim, 2018). A further four
sub-criteria were devised based on the comments observed in the submitted assignment as a positive or a negative comment on
perceived usefulness and ease-of use. See Appendix Bfor further information.
As a next step, we analysed the top five reasons students attributed to a positive usefulness of AI. We identified several recurring
themes (generating five more sub-criteria as shown in Appendix C) to assess the use of AI in an essay-based assignment. Conversely, we
also captured the top five reasons students attributed to a negative usefulness of AI, mainly issues surrounding privacy of the students’
input data, and issues regarding the intellectual property ownership of the output, as captured as an EC in Appendix D.

repeating phrases verbatim or were they applying knowledge based on their experience? We determined this through the style and

2.3.2. Sensemaking level
Having decided on six sets of evaluation criteria (EC1-EC6), we performed a quantitative syntactic analysis of each criterion’s
relative frequency within the set of submissions - usually, the number of submissions with an occurrence - accompanied by a quali-
tative manual analysis of the content. All observations were recorded in a spreadsheet that mapped the unique student identifier for the
submission according to the presence or absence of each evaluation criterion. To consolidate the evaluation criteria of students’ AI
engagement we developed a three-stage taxonomy which draws on Preuss et al. (2023)educators’ interpretation of Daft and Weick’s
(1984)theory of sensemaking. Daft and Weick propose that sensemaking is a three-stage process: (1) scanning for information, (2)
interpreting that information, and (3) identifying and evaluating alternatives of action. Lim and Kumar (2023)similarly refer to three
stages of sensemaking: (1) scanning - familiarisation with content, (2) sensing - interpretation, and (3) substantiating - confirming
reliability and transferability to other contexts.
For our context we adapted sensemaking to provide us with a framework for understanding underlying themes, trends, and root
causes, and for deriving actionable insights beyond the surface-level patterns of data within the evolving concept of GenAI application
in the context of higher education. To interpret students’ claims and evaluators’ opinions of students’ use of generated AI, we con-
verted the taxonomy to (1) scanning and generating information and knowledge, (2) interpreting and rephrasing information, and (3)
critiquing and evaluating frameworks, theories, affordances, approaches, and actions.
Sensemaking should be seen as a sequential progression with each stage building on each other with the previous stage needing to
be fulfilled before the next stage can be achieved. Similar to a pyramid, not all students progress from one stage to the next. Daft and
Weick (1984)and Preuss et al. (2023)postulate that to pass to the next stage, the previous stage needs to be achieved. As there is no
sequential progression for the assessment criteria, we cannot contrast the sensemaking stages directly with the actual assessment
criteria of the assignment. However, we were able to apply alternative models, namely Driscoll’s reflective model and Bloom’s tax-
onomy, to juxtapose sensemaking (see Fig. 4).What the application of these frameworks allowed us to do is to map broad assessment
bands, where level 1 is perceived as descriptive and commensurate with a Pass level work, level 2 equates to more interpretive
engagement comparable to an Upper Second at undergraduate level, or Merit at postgraduate level, while level 3 demanded strong
personal engagement, insightful evaluation, and/or novel conceptualisations and application, reflecting First Class work at under-
graduate level, or Distinction at postgraduate level.

2.4. Research validity
As a cross-sectional study built on interpretivist principles, there are natural implications for the replicability of the findings.
Applying the trustworthiness factors described in Lim (2024), the research validity of our study derives from three factors: credibility,
confirmability, and transferability of the findings. Firstly, because students were asked to write 2500 words across three independent
sections of the assignment, we were able to ensure credibility of the results through triangulation of written evidence, thereby asserting
that the expressed student sentiment was as intended. Secondly, confirmability derives from the independence of the marking and
research teams and the pseudonymous systems over which assignments are assessed. Finally, the combination of a flexible essay
assignment structure, relatively large sample size and the diverse range of student nationalities and backgrounds helps ensure
transferability of the findings despite the study being focussed on a single cohort.

3. Findings
We analysed the data and outline the findings in this section in three parts. The first part shows how students claim how they used
GenAI while the second part reviews how assessors evaluate how students seem to have used GenAI. In the third part students and
assessors’ views are contrasted.
3.1. Students’ claim of how GenAI helped them for the assessment
The adoption and use of specific technology applications were influenced by a multitude of factors, including their perceived
usefulness and ease of use (Davis, 1989). In their written reflections, students claimed that they were overwhelmingly positive about
their experiences of AI, with over 80% of comments expressing positive usefulness, which contrasts with just over 10% of comments
expressing negative usefulness. Only very students commented on ease of use, and those who did perceived AI’s ease of use more
negative than positive (see Table 2).
In terms of students’ perceived limitations of the AI tools, the most frequently expressed problem with the AI tools was the accuracy
of information returned, with over 32% of students referring to this. Only approximately 15% of students raised the concern that
ChatGPT, which was only trained on data up to September 2021 at the time of the study, may not provide up-to-date information.
Around 24% of students mentioned the inability of AI to critically evaluate its output due to lacking a holistic view. Observations
around potential plagiarism in using AI-generated content, or privacy concerns were rare, discussed by 7% and 3% of students
respectively. Table 3provides further examples of students’ perceived drawbacks in using GenAI.
As for mapping students’ comments to our proposed taxonomy of (1) scanning and generating information and knowledge, (2)
interpreting and rephrasing information, and (3) critiquing and evaluating actions, no single student stated that they used GenAI to write
their assignment in its entirety as this claim would have risked failing their assignment. A quarter of students (26.3%) described their
use of AI as a tool for generating knowledge, which suggested the lowest stage of sensemaking in the context of AI usage (scanning and
evaluating). Students at stage 1 did not claim to have used GenAI for anything more than stage 1, while at the next stage students have
displayed both stage 1 and stage 2; over half of students (56.8%) claimed to have used AI also for interpreting and rephrasing either their
own writing or external sources – the middle stage of AI usage in our taxonomy. Less than a fifth of students (16.9%) claimed to have
used AI also for critiquing and evaluating their work, the highest stage of sensemaking in the context of AI usage (see Table 4).
3.2. Assessors’ evaluation of how students might have deployed GenAI for their assessment
Using the same sensemaking taxonomy, we categorised the students’ submissions from the assessors’ points of view. Instead of
using the students’ claimed usage from the reflective essays, assessors considered demonstrated usage, as defined by the evaluation
criteria.
All participating students displayed the initial stage of sensemaking, ‘Scanning and Generating’, demonstrating how to generate
text by entering prompts. The 24 students (20.3%) who remained at stage 1 were seen to provide in their GenAI prompts very little
input with long AI-generated responses. Indicative comments from students in this category included: ‘AI helped generate outlines, and
structure the assignment, by providing a logical flow of ideas and concepts’; eight students incorrectly included ChatGPT as a primary
research source in their bibliography in lieu of journal articles or other secondary sources.
As shown in Table 5, most students (82 students, 69.5%) demonstrated the next stage 2 of sensemaking by using GenAI to refine and
rephrase text. One student in this category remarked that the AI ‘helped me refine my language and adapt it to a more academic
context’. Typically, the length of input prompts compared to generated output was longer for students in the stage 2 category than for
students of stage 1.

3.3. Contrasting students’ claims and assessors’ evaluation of the use of GenAI for the assessment
Drawing on the numerical classifications of the previous two sections, Fig. 5compares the students’ claims of AI usage from within
the third reflective part of their assignment, with the assessors’ evaluations of AI usage displayed throughout the three parts of the
students’ assignments. Notably, while there is some alignment in percentage terms between students’ and assessors’ evaluations, the
lower number of students in the higher categories as judged by the assessors, could be seen as evidence of students overestimating their
abilities. Alternatively, or additionally, this also suggests that some students used language in their reflective essays that did not
necessarily match the additional evidence provided to the assessors, with students possibly telling the assessors what they thought
assessors wanted to hear.
There is a particular data point that supports the idea of some students writing about fictitious use rather than real use of AI, i.e.,
some students writing not about their true AI usage but according to their perception of what assessors want to hear: The assignment
question explicitly mentioned several AI tools, including a bespoke AI tool developed by the assessors, with students only receiving
access to the bespoke AI tool by filling in specific research ethics forms and formally requesting access. While no student requested
access, 16 of the 118 students in the sample claimed to have used the tool, in some cases fabricating a narrative of feedback provided by
the bespoke tool, even though the bespoke tool could not have provided the feedback that students claimed to have received.
Why did some students do this? Our assumption is that in some cases students are looking for clues in the assignment question and
brief as to what constitutes a ‘perfect’ answer in the eyes of the assessor. Much has been written about model answers and the effect
they have on student behaviour (Huxham, 2007; Frost, 2016; Kember, 2013). By incorporating all tools indicatively outlined in the
assignment brief into their writing, students might have felt that they fulfilled the brief, and thus deserved a better mark than if they
had not mentioned all the tools listed indicatively for use in the assessment brief.
4. Discussion
This discussion explores the integration of GenAI into higher education, particularly its implications for assessment, communi-
cative competence, and critical thinking skills. The term ‘generative’ is commonly used in the research community to describe complex
AI models that produce high-quality, human-like content (García-Pen˜alvo & V´azquez-Ingelmo, 2023). Although AI had already been
integrated into various educational contexts (Baker & Inventado, 2014; Fischer, 2023; Niemi, 2021; Suresh et al., 2019), a widespread
application surged with the launch of ChatGPT in late 2022 (Cao et al., 2023). This case study evaluates the current use of GenAI tools,
such as ChatGPT and Gemini for text generation and summarisation, Grammarly for language refinement, and Dall.E for image cre-
ation. The aim of this discussion is to assist educators and university policymakers to make sense of the educational shifts prompted by
GenAI, with the technology offering new insights into existing academic deficiencies. One such issue is communicative competence.
Communicative competence encompasses skills like critical thinking, analytical ability, and proficiency in the language of instruction
(in this case English).
4.1. Sensemaking of critical skills and graduateness
Developments in AI are rapid and hard to predict. Based on our findings we argue that universities should embrace GenAI’s po-
tential to enhance pedagogy, and build, rather than lose, proficiency in written and oral communication, and by doing so we
recommend practices that limit threats to the quality and integrity of assessment. By incorporating sensemaking principles into our
teaching, learning, and assessment, similar to Preuss et al. (2023)we found that only 10% of students reached the ‘critiquing’ state,
representing a critical aspect of ’graduateness’. Graduateness is defined as ‘the effect on knowledge, skills and attitudes of having
undertaken [a] degree’ (Glover et al., 2002, p. 294). This implies achievement of a set of standards. Glover et al. (2002)cite an un-
derstanding of graduateness as:
a set of transferable skills including planning; gathering, selecting, appraising, organising, and recording information;
communicating and realising results, and evaluating achievements (Library Association, 1996, cited in Glover et al., 2002:295)
[emphasis added by the authors].
In a Higher Education Quality Council survey, students identified five attributes:
1. The ability to research, analyse and present information coherently.
2. Breadth of vision – the ability to continue learning, the ability to relate to a wide range of subjects, a command of a foreign lan-
guage, a curiosity about other subjects, a breadth of knowledge.
3. Expertise in their chosen field, the ability to achieve a balanced view, an open and flexible mind.
4. A good knowledge of the English language – the ability to write and spell.
5. Impetus to reach a goal, in a disciplined manner (London Guildhall University, 1997; cited in Glover et al., 2002, p. 295).
The integration of GenAI into education presents both opportunities and challenges in achieving ’graduateness’, i.e., the set of
attributes expected of university graduates. Sensemaking, the process of making meaningful connections from complex information,
plays a crucial role in helping students navigate AI tools critically and reflectively. This aligns with the goal of fostering critical
thinking and communication skills, key elements of graduateness. By leveraging GenAI in a in a thoughtful way, educators can promote
this development while countering risks, such as academic dishonesty or the erosion of communicative competence.

To understand how GenAI can help universities increase the proportion of students reaching the ‘critiquing’ stage, and to develop
graduates with the skills employers expect, we must consider its impact on pedagogy in the context of producing career-ready
graduates. Universities seem reluctant to admit that claims of graduateness are frequently dubious (Jenkins, 2023). A concern
regarding GenAI is that it may mask poor communicative competence and weak language proficiency. It may also lead to new forms of
academic dishonesty, defined as ‘(any) deceitful or unfair act intended to produce a more desirable outcome on an exam, paper,
homework assignment, or other assessment of learning’ (Miller et al., 2017, p. 121; Sweeney, 2023). While GenAI chatbots generate
correct writing on demand, potentially giving an inaccurate impression of the student(s)’ language proficiency,
Other AI tools such as Grammarly and bespoke feedback tools, in contrast, tend to work like a personal language tutor, providing
effective teaching and learning aids, highlighting errors, and offering possible solutions (Calma et al., 2022).
Our analysis of students’ reactions to AI technologies showed support for Grammarly as a useful aid to learning, but scepticism
about GenAI, in part due to insufficient institutional guidance on acceptable use of GenAI, as well as concern about equality of access to
new technologies (Johnston et al., 2024), e.g., access to the free versus the payable versions of GenAI tools. If universities offer degree
courses that no longer test the students’ ability to write independently and spontaneously, then this would run counter to evidence that
employers consider good writing a required core skill (Calma, 2022; Cohen, 2019; Succi & Canovi, 2020). It would also contradict
guidance from the UK Office for Students which affirmed that English language proficiency should be integral to assessment and
universities’ awards (Office for Students, 2021). Also, currently, students with weaker language skills are left continually disadvan-
taged, unable to fully contribute to - or benefit from - their programme.
To respond positively to generative AI technology, educators must adopt wide-ranging assessment strategies that focus on
developing all learners’ communicative competence and confidence (Fischer & Luiz, 2024). Applying principles of sensemaking
(Preuss et al., 2023) can help to demonstrate and enhance graduateness. Educators must ensure that graduates are equipped for
employment and better able to meet contemporary and future challenges. GenAI clearly challenges management educators to rethink
teaching and learning, including assessment. We need to exploit potential benefits that AI can deliver to teaching and learning pro-
cesses. Until now, business and management education has struggled to keep up with innovation, especially AI-related tools (Sollosy &
McInerney, 2022). We must now change to a more agile environment and respond with a sense of urgency to remain relevant. Rather
than narrow functional approaches, a better response might be programme-level synoptic assessments and a broader curriculum that
deals effectively with management processes and embraces emergent AI technologies that are (re-)shaping a ‘hypercompetitive
environment’ (Sollosy & McInerney, 2022:para.35).
The Covid-19 pandemic led to more flexible working, and an emphasis on training and lifelong learning. It spurred considerable
adaptation towards a ‘digital transformation of education’ (Mahajan et al., 2023:para.25). A recent study conducted in the Netherlands
highlights the importance of integrating flexibility and digital technologies into higher education. It suggests that successful learning
experiences increasingly depend on embedding digital tools within course delivery, offering students a more adaptable and enriched
educational environment. This approach aligns with the broader trend towards digital transformation in education, which was
accelerated by the pandemic, and positions technology as a critical element in ensuring students’ engagement and motivation in
evolving academic contexts (Banihashem et al., 2023).
Accommodating transformative AI technologies as integral to pedagogy, means embracing change and adopting GenAI in-
struments, while ensuring students’ critical engagement. This means developing the interpretive and critical thinking skills that are
key to the sensemaking approach. Effective management education requires a clear understanding of the skills and knowledge needed
for navigating future technological changes and ethical concerns. The workplace of the future will feature more advanced automation
and transformative digital technologies, with the workplace and work itself evolving (Fischer, 2024). Building on the findings of our
case study and Preuss et al. (2023), i.e., which both finding that only about 10% of students currently demonstrate critical thinking
skills, we should welcome more explicit use of AI applications provided their use encourages all, or at least more, students to explore
the tools in ways that develop critical thinking skills. For example, in class, when reviewing novel applications of AI tools, students
could debate the consequences of AI imitating human attributes but falling short on genuine human expression, compassion, empathy,
critical thinking, mindfulness, leadership, and physical presence.
4.2. Adaptation of assessment processes
While GenAI offers opportunities for enhancing critical thinking, the next challenge lies in adapting assessment processes to ensure
(Gen)AI tools are used effectively and ethically. A key principle of assessment is that it aligns with a pedagogical approach that reflects
the demands of the ‘real world’ (Lund, 1997, p. 25). Not only is the formal academic essay potentially undermined by AI bots like
ChatGPT (Stokel-Walker, 2022), in addition, it frequently misaligns with the evolving skills that employers prioritise (Flaherty, 2021;
Tomlinson, 2022). Authentic assessments are a way to connect with the ‘real world’ and can develop the students’ aforementioned
graduateness, especially their creativity (Jenkins, 2023), communicative competence, and confidence (Fischer & Luiz, 2024).
Moreover, assessment portfolios across programmes of study should use a wide range of methods (Pugh, n.d.). Assessments should
also provide the essential feedback loop between teacher and student, serving to evaluate both the students’ learning and the teachers’
teaching. It therefore goes to the core of the pedagogical relationship. Assessment in higher education plays a pivotal role in shaping
both student learning and teaching practices. Both formative and summative assessments play important roles in ensuring that stu-
dents can engage with GenAI technologies while achieving the learning outcomes required by higher education institutions such as the
Quality Assurance Agency (QAA) in the UK. In this context, summative assessments serve as a mechanism for verifying that students
have successfully achieved the specified learning outcomes of their courses, which, in turn, contribute to the overall integrity of degree
programmes. With QAA (or equivalent in other countries) benchmark criteria in mind, assessments must satisfy both internal academic

requirements and external regulatory standards. The grading of results reflects the attainment of these standards, providing
accountability and ensuring quality across educational institutions.
Assessment in higher education should extend beyond the summative function. It should also foster cognitive development through
formative assessments, which enable students to actively and continuously engage with their learning. Formative assessments are
designed to support students in improving their thinking and learning processes by providing feedback that helps them refine their
skills and understanding. This type of assessment encourages students to develop metacognitive skills, enabling them to judge their
own learning and, potentially, to become lifelong learners. As Huisman et al. (2018) argue, formative assessment should offer a
transparent, inductive process that not only clarifies the goals of assessment for students but also has the potential to improve the
quality of their academic work, such as writing.
Incorporating students’ use of GenAI into formative assessments provides an additional layer of evaluative reflection. As noted by
Bearman et al. (2024), this reflective practice enhances students’ evaluative judgement and fosters a shared understanding of how to
effectively harness GenAI technologies in academic settings. Formative assessment thus plays a central role in promoting higher-order
thinking skills, as it encourages students to critically evaluate their use of such technologies and apply these tools to enhance their
learning. This process is also supported by the findings of Wu et al. (2024), who highlight how formative assessment can drive in-
tellectual engagement and promote deeper cognitive development.
Cognition, in this sense, refers to the learner’s ability to intellectually grasp subject content, nurturing critical engagement with the
discipline. This process aligns with the concept of sensemaking (Daft & Weick, 1984; Preuss et al., 2023) where students move from
simply understanding information to making meaningful connections within their learning. Moreover, as students engage in sense-
making, they refine the communicative competence essential for academic and professional success.
Metacognition, on the other hand, reflects the degree to which this understanding informs lifelong learning, enabling graduates to
apply their knowledge and skills in real-world contexts. In the framework of sensemaking, metacognition represents the stage where
understanding transitions into action, empowering students to become effective and responsible participants in their professional and
social environments.
As briefly touched on earlier, in addition to benefiting students, assessment outcomes also offer crucial insights for educators. Both
formative and summative assessments provide feedback that can be used to improve teaching strategies and the design of future
assignments. By reflecting on assessment results, educators can refine their own thinking and develop tasks that promote deeper
student engagement with the subject matter. This process encourages not only critical reflection on the content being taught but also an
ongoing, reflective dialogue between teachers and students. Through this dynamism, teachers can foster an environment that supports
reflective thinking and enhances students’ engagement with their own learning journey.
Ultimately, assessment in higher education is not a one-dimensional tool for measuring achievement, but a complex process that
bridges the gap between teaching and learning. It serves as both a mechanism for accountability and a means of fostering intellectual
growth, encouraging students to become reflective learners capable of critical thinking and continuous personal development. By using
both summative and formative assessments effectively, educators can create a pedagogical environment that nurtures deep learning
and prepares students for lifelong intellectual engagement.
Developing responsible application of AI technology in management education requires a focus on several key priorities (Niemi,
2020, 2021; Roschelle et al., 2020), including stronger policies around ethics and equity (Niemi, 2020, 2021; Roschelle et al., 2020).
Equitable instructional design for assessments requires a good understanding of students, including an appreciation that while students
often have a range of prior learning experience relating to geographical (place) and economic (means) circumstances, they also come
from very different starting points compared to educators. They also often bring a different understanding and experience of AI. Most
students are digital natives (Fischer & Dobbins, 2024), but probably do not have long-lasting AI-related research interests. As edu-
cators, we need to take the role of a subject-matter expert and of a facilitator (Fischer & Dobbins, 2024) to help students make sense of
GenAI for assessments and to develop a critical relationship to the technology. This needs to be reflected in the design of assessment
tasks and questions.
Sensemaking can help to clarify the steps through which students need guidance. This requires understanding of the relationship
between sensemaking and student learning, including increased focus on the third stage of sensemaking, where we need evidence of
critical thinking and commitment to a programme of action on the part of graduates that involves lifelong learning, career devel-
opment, and ambition to make a difference in addressing major challenges, including how to deploy ethical AI in creative and
innovative ways. We believe that incorporating a sensemaking approach into assessments adds real-world authenticity to the teaching
and learning process, since it can help to develop cognitive skills including critical analysis and personal reflection, key competences
that graduates need in a world in which AI is already commonplace at work and in the wider society.
5. Recommendations
In this section we first make hands-on suggestions for assessment design at course-level, before recommending holistic approaches
at institutional-level.
5.1. Recommendations for educators for assessment design
The approach taken in our study was to encourage students to deploy AI in relation to the subject-matter and to document their
findings in a reflective essay. This seemed successful for the higher achieving students who were able to interpret their discovered
findings but was less successful in helping lower achieving students move on from the scanning stage of sensemaking. They were

unable to stop applying reductionist techniques to complete their assignments. Our findings suggest that simple modifications to the
assignment brief might have helped in this regard.
(1) Making better use of examples in the assignment brief: We observed that lower-achieving students claimed usage of exactly those AI
tools that were mentioned in the brief, even if this was not an accurate reflection of their personal experience. Either avoiding
the use of examples entirely, or listing a larger number of examples, might have helped lower-performing students to under-
stand that it was acceptable and desirable that the tool selection was of their own choosing. This creative freedom had already
been achieved in the first part of the assignment, which asked students to select a digital marketing topic of their choosing, and
without leading recommendations; this resulted in uniqueness of subject-matter across the cohort.
(2) Encouraging lesson-learning and the development of more rounded arguments: We observed that lower achievers had a lower
variance of observations related to the Technology Acceptance Model: they often restricted their comments to a positive use-
fulness of AI, as opposed to higher achievers who were able to make comments that applied across the four quadrants of the
model. Similarly, lower-achieving students, on average, did not document many instances of learning that would shape their
future interests or behaviours particularly well, as demonstrated by a lower proportion of comments in the ‘future personal’
voice in the essay. It is possible that these lower-performing students applied a narrow definition of the word ‘reflective’ from
the assignment brief, which restricted their confidence to internalise their findings and learn future skills from their experience.
We suggest that more directive language in the assignment brief might encourage more interpretive behaviours.
(3) Considering how and when to supply model answers produced by humans or by GenAI: In the discussion of the assignment during the
delivery of the course, we observed several student requests for a template or model answers. While model answers can help
students learn by example, for assessments that encourage the development of skills through the application of knowledge,
supplying answers must be weighed against the potential for students to follow a reductionist approach when tackling the
assignment. Students who are struggling, or do not want to spend any time on writing the assessment, feel that they could take
the structure (and sometimes, the content) from the model answer, replicate it and expect a high mark. Hence, it is sometimes
better to focus on the desirable qualities of the best performers and encourage students to emulate them. Having said this,
providing students with the answer(s) generated by GenAI in advance has the advantage that students understand that the
assessors know what the GenAI answer looks like and that they have to differentiate themselves, and have to deliver much more
than solely use GenAI.
In summary, while we recommend clear signposting of what knowledge and skills students are meant to demonstrate, the desirable
qualities of the best performers often include a creative spark or uniqueness that is difficult to express in a marking guide. With the
increasing adoption of GenAI, these sparks and uniqueness are likely in future to differ from what assessors have previously looked for.
Providing students with model answers produced by GenAI demonstrates a quality of answer that needs to be exceeded and an avenue
to discuss creativity and sought-after uniqueness.
5.2. Recommendations for policymakers and university leaders
We observed widespread student use of AI for knowledge and clarity of expression, including some inappropriate use, such as
referencing it, similar to an academic article. This indicates that we need to enhance students’ AI literacy: we need to adapt our
teaching and share more effectively the opportunities for learning that AI provides, beyond the actual text generation. University
leaders therefore need to consider appropriate ways to integrate AI into students’ programmes of study across the curricula and
evaluate how to balance the teaching of core AI skills, including critical thinking on AI ethics, with module subject-matter, and with
transferable skills, such as communicative competence.
We recommend developing both faculty and students’ understanding of the ethical use of AI within a broader commitment to
responsible management, prioritising sustainability, for example in accordance with UN sustainable development goals (SDG). Level 3
of sensemaking, in particular, underscores critical evaluation which directly links to SDG goal 4, quality education, and SDG goal 10,
reducing inequalities. We recommend incorporating professional development initiatives for educators to equip them with the skills
necessary to effectively incorporate GenAI tools in their teaching practices, thereby fostering a more engaging and innovative learning
environment to facilitate quality education. The AI Pedagogy Project (MetaLab, n.d.) and AI Forge (Forge, n.d) are emerging examples
of initiatives that aim to upskill educators to engage with AI more critically. When making these GenAI tools more inclusive it is
possible to contribute to reducing educational inequalities and to promote inclusive education.
Our findings point to an alignment of the share of students demonstrating critical thinking skills in their assessments post-GenAI
introduction, compared to pre-GenAI (e.g., when comparing our findings with Preuss et al., 2023), indicating that the technology could
be simply providing a new perspective on existing academic deficiencies. Developing synoptic assessments at programme level and
testing the use of English (or the equivalent language of instruction) independently and spontaneously, possibly by deploying AI, will
ensure that students can collaborate with each other and with GenAI to achieve graduateness.
GenAI presents new opportunities for higher education to reduce current deficiencies, including new channels of delivery and
personalisation. AIcould, for example, generate content in different formats that resonate more naturally with specific learners,
supporting the learning journey rather than replacing aspects of the learning journey. Cognisance of this cannot be achieved by in-
dividual management educators alone. Developing these aspects of AI requires university leaders and policymakers to take an active
role in developing institutional AI strategies and providing clear guidance for students on the use of AI.

6. Limitations and further considerations
We already highlighted in the Findings section (see 3.3) the main limitation of our experiential work, namely that some - or even
many - students seemed to write about their AI use what they thought what the assessors wanted to hear. In addition, we recognise that
the recommendations of this case study are context specific. Our findings are based on pre-experience Master’s students from a
research-intensive business school in the UK, and their work in the summer term, towards the end of their studies. Assessment fatigue
might have meant that their work with GenAI was more ‘automated’ and at a lower sensemaking stage than at other moments in their
studies. Of course, pre-experience Master’s students are not a monolithic group. Further research is necessary to explore these aspects.
Finally, to reach the third stage of sensemaking, skills are needed that are not explicitly mentioned in this paper but are needed to
support critical thinking. These include curiosity, creativity, cognitive flexibility, abstraction, understanding and unpacking of com-
plex issues, reflection and empathising to adapt to different audiences, evaluating and anticipating ethical concerns, demonstrating
innovation and uniqueness, and adding to knowledge. Further research could investigate how best to leverage each of these aspects
towards developing critical thinking at a time when GenAI offers many affordances.
7. Conclusion
Considering the ‘groundbreaking technological advancements’ (Lim, 2023:para.1) of GenAI instruments, and their contribution to
a changing working environment within which societal expectations are changing (Lim, 2023), this case study evaluated students’
deployment of GenAI. By contrasting students’ claims and assessors’ observations we found that the majority of students use GenAI to
write or paraphrase parts of their work rather than helping them to move into the realm of critiquing and critical thinking, a sense-
making stage which currently only around one in ten students demonstrates.
In a rapidly changing world where AI affordances are increasingly pervasive, management educators need to embrace change and
accommodate transformative AI technologies. This requires educating students on the responsible use of GenAI, designing assignments
that require students to engage creatively and critically with GenAI, to complement rather than to replace aspects of learning, and to
provide students with opportunities to develop critical thinking and academic writing skills in line with graduateness. Given that
individual management educators cannot deal with contemporary and future higher education issues on their own, university leaders
need to develop AI strategies that facilitate an appropriate and effective use of AI by students and by educators. This will help students
to develop the mindset needed to thrive in an AI-powered workplace, and to address effectively and responsibly the grand challenges
confronting humanity.
