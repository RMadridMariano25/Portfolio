Recent advances in generative AI (GenAI) technologies have reshaped student learning in higher education.
However,  most  studies  treat  AI  usage  as  a  generalized  construct,  overlooking  differences  across  various
disciplines, educational levels, and regions. This gap is particularly relevant in China, where regional disparities
in access and readiness may influence student engagement with AI tools. This study examines how university
students in China perceive, utilize, and are influenced by generative AI tools across various disciplines and
regions, while also assessing the accuracy and disciplinary relevance of AI-generated responses in professional
coursework. The study includes four mixed-methods investigations: a national survey of students’ AI usage
patterns and attitudes; an evaluation of ChatGPT’s responses to subject-specific questions based on semantic
similarity and expert scoring; behavioral clustering and analysis of one-month AI usage logs to identify distinct
user profiles; and an exploration of AI tools’ impact on academic performance and long-term outcomes.
Findings show significant variation in AI engagement across disciplines and regions. Students in economically
developed areas report higher usage and broader application, while those in central and western regions
have more limited access. Students’ academic performance can be improved to varying degrees through AI-
driven learning, with engineering and natural sciences students primarily using AI for technical tasks, while
humanities and arts students employ it for linguistic and creative support. Evaluation of ChatGPT’s responses
reveals moderate accuracy, with disciplinary variations. Despite generally positive attitudes, both students and
experts express concerns about content reliability, over-reliance on AI, and its potential to hinder independent
thinking and creativity.

1. Introduction

Recent advancements in generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)
technologies,  particularly  the  emergence  of  Large  Language  Models
(LLMs), have dramatically lowered the threshold for engaging with
advanced AI capabilities (Chiu, 2024). In higher education, GenAI has
become deeply integrated into students’ academic routines (Chiu, 2024;
Shailendra et al., 2024), supporting activities such as research planning,
essay composition, literature review, data interpretation, coding, and
even  creative  production  (Holmes  et  al.,  2023;  Korseberg  &  Elken,
2024). Students, as the primary adopters of GenAI in educational set-
tings, not only use these tools to enhance productivity but also develop
evolving expectations, beliefs, and ethical stances toward AI-assisted
learning (Hsiao & Tang, 2024). Consequently, a deeper understanding

of student satisfaction and perception is urgently needed—not just in
terms of whether students accept AI tools, but how they integrate them
into academic work across varying contexts (Gu & Yan, 2024). Existing
studies (Beckman et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2025; Essien et al., 2025;
García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025) have begun to explore constructs
such as AI trust, general acceptance, and perceived usefulness, typically
drawing on frameworks such as the Technology Acceptance Model or
human-AI interaction theories developed in professional or commercial
domains.

While these instruments offer useful insights into general accep-
tance,  they  remain  insufficiently  adapted  to  educational  realities—
especially  in  terms  of  discipline-specific  applications  and  regionally
embedded student experiences. Firstly, most surveys conceptualize AI
usage  as  a  generalized  construct,  overlooking  the  diverse  academic

prioritize AI-assisted language refinement and idea generation (Li et al.,
2024), while students in STEM fields frequently utilize AI for tasks such
as coding, simulation, or symbolic computation (Wang et al., 2025).
This  functional  heterogeneity  is  often  obscured  in  existing  studies,
which fail to account for the discipline-specific nature of academic
engagement. Secondly, the majority of empirical studies are situated
within  narrowly  defined  or  high-tech  fields—such  as  computer  sci-
ence  or  medical  education—without  examining  whether  their  find-
ings generalize across the broader disciplinary landscape (Cervantes
et al., 2024; Hazzan & Erez, 2025). As a result, comparatively little
is known about how students in disciplines such as the social sciences,
teacher education, or the creative arts adopt and respond to AI, despite
their growing reliance on digital tools in varied pedagogical contexts.
Thirdly, most established frameworks emphasize traditional constructs
such as intention to use, perceived usefulness, and ease of use (Ng et al.,
2025; Nikolic et al., 2024). However, they often overlook experiential
and contextualized concerns that have emerged as central to students’
actual  interactions  with  generative  AI  in  education.  These  include
worries about academic integrity, the risk of over-reliance on AI tools,
and tensions between AI-supported convenience and the cultivation
of creativity and independent thinking. As AI becomes embedded in
students’ daily academic routines, these grounded experiences – rather
than abstract intentions – are increasingly critical for understanding
satisfaction and effective adoption.

These limitations are particularly pronounced in the Chinese higher
education context. China’s vast geographic and institutional diversity
creates wide disparities in digital infrastructure, pedagogical innova-
tion,  and  openness  to  emerging  technologies  (Xie  &  Zhang,  2024).
While major urban universities may actively promote AI experimenta-
tion in classrooms, many institutions in central and western provinces
face barriers in terms of faculty readiness, access to tools, and policy
clarity. Prior studies (Jiang et al., 2021; Wang & Xiao, 2017; Xiang
et  al.,  2020)  have  noted  significant  regional  variation  in  students’
digital literacy and online learning environments, yet few empirical
studies systematically compare AI attitudes and usage behaviors across
both academic disciplines and geographic regions in China.

To address these critical gaps, this study adopts a multi-dimensional
design to examine how university students perceive, engage with, and
are impacted by generative AI tools across different disciplines and re-
gions in China. The first stage explores students’ attitudes, satisfaction,
and usage patterns in professional coursework, highlighting variations
shaped by disciplinary context, education level, and geographic region.
The second stage assesses the reliability and disciplinary alignment of
AI-generated responses to course-specific questions, offering insights
into  how  well  generative  tools  support  domain  learning.  The  third
stage identifies distinct AI engagement profiles through clustering anal-
ysis and traces students’ behavioral patterns over time, revealing how
technological  proficiency,  engagement  depth,  and  academic  context
shape actual usage trajectories. The fourth stage evaluates the long-
term impact of AI usage on students’ academic performance, focusing
on improvements in problem-solving, knowledge retention, and over-
all learning outcomes. Together, these four stages provide a compre-
hensive,  context-sensitive  understanding  of  AI  integration  in  higher
education.

Through this four-stage approach, we go beyond surface-level ac-
ceptance  metrics.  Students’  perceptions  and  usage  patterns  provide
the foundation for evaluating AI-generated responses, which then in-
form the clustering of engagement profiles and their relationship to
long-term academic outcomes. This approach offers a comprehensive,
comparative, and context-sensitive analysis of how university students
in China perceive and engage with generative AI tools in real academic
settings.  Our  study  integrates  survey  responses,  large-scale  content
evaluation,  and  behavioral  log  analysis  to  capture  the  multifaceted
nature of AI use in higher education. Specifically, this research aims
to address the following questions:

Taken together, the four-stage design is guided by a single over-

and translated into learning outcomes in authentic university contexts.
Each stage addresses a necessary and logically ordered component of
this objective. Stage 1 establishes the contextual baseline by mapping
students’ perceptions, satisfaction, and self-reported usage across dis-
ciplines and regions. Stage 2 moves from perception to capability by
examining  whether  AI-generated  responses  are  sufficiently  accurate
and disciplinarily aligned to support academic learning. Building on
these foundations, Stage 3 captures how students actually engage with
AI  over  time  by  identifying  distinct  behavioral  profiles  and  usage
trajectories. Finally, Stage 4 connects these engagement patterns to
educational consequences by evaluating the impact of AI use on aca-
demic performance and longer-term learning outcomes. Collectively,
these stages form an integrated explanatory framework that links atti-
tudes, tool performance, behavioral engagement, and learning effects,
thereby providing a coherent and comprehensive answer to the study’s
overarching research question.

2. Methodology

This study presents four complementary empirical studies designed
to examine the role of AI-driven learning tools in professional course-
work within higher education in China, with each study addressing
a distinct research question. As illustrated in Fig.  1, the studies were
conducted independently, yet together they provide a comprehensive
perspective on the subject. Study 1 is a large-scale survey involving
1472 university students across five academic disciplines recruited from
39  institutions  distributed  across  four  major  geographic  regions  in
China: Eastern, Central, Western, and Northeastern. This stage inves-
tigates students’ attitudes toward generative AI tools and their usage
patterns in academic settings. By linking students’ responses to their
disciplinary affiliation, educational level, and regional context, Study
1 provides a multidimensional view of how AI tools are perceived and
adopted across diverse academic and geographic environments.

Studies 2–4 draw on representative subgroups from the original
cohort of 1472 participants to examine AI engagement in greater depth
across academic contexts. Study 2 examines the accuracy and disci-
plinary alignment of ChatGPT (GPT-4-Turbo) responses using expert-
validated course questions from a stratified sample of 240 students.
Study 3 identifies distinct AI engagement profiles among 800 students
via DBSCAN clustering and longitudinal analysis of one-month AI usage
logs. Study 4 investigates the effects of AI use on academic performance
in a controlled experiment with 400 students, combining assessment
outcomes with predictive modeling of learning trajectories. Together,
these studies extend the cross-sectional findings of Study 1 by provid-
ing a multidimensional analysis of AI accuracy, usage behavior, and
performance impacts, offering a comprehensive view of generative AI
integration in higher education.

2.1. Participants

This study utilized a structured questionnaire as the main instru-
ment  for  collecting  data  on  students’  attitudes,  patterns  of  AI  use,
and  their  perceptions  of  the  impact  of  AI-supported  learning  tools.
The survey included a total of 11 items—8 closed-ended and 3 open-
ended. The closed-ended questions gathered information on participant
demographics (such as gender, academic discipline, education level,
and region), frequency of AI use (e.g., daily, weekly, monthly, rarely,
or never), and specific use cases (e.g., summarization, problem-solving,
and writing support). The open-ended questions invited students to
reflect on their experiences with AI in academic contexts, including
perceived advantages, limitations, and the reliability of AI-generated
content. This mixed-question format enabled us to conduct both quan-
titative  analyses  of  usage  behaviors  and  qualitative  exploration  of
disciplinary differences in perception.

The survey was distributed over a three-month period to students
from 39 universities across mainland China. These universities were
selected using a stratified sampling strategy designed to ensure broad
coverage of China’s higher education system in terms of geography,
academic specialization, and institutional type. Specifically, universi-
ties were drawn from four macro-regions—Eastern, Central, Western,
and Northeastern China—which were defined by their geographic prox-
imity and economic connectivity. For example, East and South China
(e.g.,  Jiangsu,  Guangdong)  were  grouped  due  to  their  high-density
educational  and  industrial  bases,  while  North  and  Northeast  China
(e.g., Beijing, Liaoning) were grouped for their shared emphasis on
traditional heavy industries. This regional stratification allowed us to

explore whether geographic differences influence AI usage in higher
education.

The selected institutions included both comprehensive universities,
offering a diverse array of disciplines, as well as specialized institutions
with focused programs in areas such as medicine, business, arts, and
technology. Within each university, participants were recruited through
academic coordinators and campus announcements. Participation was
entirely voluntary and uncompensated. Although the sample does not
constitute a fully randomized national cohort, efforts were made to
ensure that it reflects the structural diversity of Chinese higher ed-
ucation. For instance, both top-tier institutions (such as Project 985
and Project 211 universities) and regular universities were included
in the sample. In addition, because Southeastern China hosts a higher
concentration of universities, more responses were collected from that
region, in proportion to its institutional density. This design enhances
the ecological validity of our sample and allows us to draw insights
that are broadly representative of student populations across different
regions and institution types in China.

During  the  data  collection  period,  we  received  1583  completed
responses. A rigorous data cleaning process was implemented to ensure
quality and reliability. We excluded surveys with incomplete or con-
tradictory responses, those with uniform answers across all questions,
and surveys completed in under 60 s, which suggested low engagement.
Open-ended responses containing fewer than 10 words were also dis-
carded, along with entries from students who did not meet our inclusion
criteria  (e.g.,  respondents  who  mistakenly  identified  themselves  as
students  from  non-target  disciplines  or  education  levels).  After  this
screening,  1472  valid  responses  were  retained,  yielding  a  response

2.2. Stage 1: Student attitudes and usage patterns (RQ1)

2.2.1. Usage patterns

To examine how students utilize AI-driven tools in their academic
work,  we  administered  a  survey  to  1472  respondents  representing
diverse disciplines and educational backgrounds. The analysis of usage
behavior was based primarily on closed-ended survey items designed
to  generate  quantitative  insights  into  students’  interaction  with  AI
technologies. These questions addressed whether students used AI tools,
how frequently they did so, and for what academic purposes. The struc-
tured nature of these responses enabled us to assess both the prevalence
and variation of AI adoption across different academic fields.

Usage status refers to whether students currently use any AI-driven
tools for academic purposes. Together with frequency of use, which
captures how often students engage with AI tools in academic contexts,
this allows us to construct temporal and behavioral dimensions of tool
engagement. Frequency was assessed using a five-point ordinal scale:
Daily,  Weekly,  Monthly,  Rarely,  and  Never.  Students  who  selected
‘‘Never’’ were classified as non-users and were excluded from further
usage  behavior  analysis.  In  contrast,  those  who  reported  any  level
of use (Rarely or more) continued with additional items related to
AI usage. Primary purposes identifies the main academic tasks for
which students apply AI tools. Based on prior literature and expert
input, we listed 24 AI application scenarios relevant to student learning,
such as summarization, writing support, problem-solving, data analysis,
exam preparation, and programming help. Students were allowed to
select multiple purposes from a checklist, enabling multi-dimensional
profiling.  These  components,  combined  with  demographic  variables
such as gender, academic level, discipline, and geographic region, were
used to construct a composite profile for each student. We then applied
K-means clustering to categorize students into distinct usage groups
based on their behavioral and demographic patterns.

2.2.2. Student attitudes

Beyond usage patterns, we aimed to examine students’ attitudes
toward AI-driven learning tools through open-ended survey responses.
Students were asked to respond to three qualitative questions: (1) What
do you think are the benefits of using AI tools in your academic studies?
(2) What concerns or limitations have you encountered while using AI
tools? (3) To what extent do you find AI-generated content accurate or
helpful in your coursework? To extract structured insights from these
qualitative responses, we employed a two-stage hierarchical sentiment

classification  pipeline  designed  to  categorize  student  attitudes  into
positive, neutral, or negative sentiment groups.

Stage 1: Preprocessing and Fragmentation. We first filtered out
incomplete responses or those containing fewer than 10 meaningful
words. Texts were then tokenized, lowercased, and stripped of punctu-
ation and stop words. To account for mixed sentiments within a single
response (e.g., ‘‘AI helps me brainstorm ideas, but sometimes gives
wrong answers’’), we split the responses into semantically independent
clauses based on punctuation marks (e.g., commas, semicolons, and
periods). Each clause was treated as an independent sentiment unit in
the subsequent analysis.

Stage  2:  Sentiment  Classification.  We  implemented  a  hybrid
attention-based model that combines BERT embeddings with a Bidirec-
tional LSTM network and an attention mechanism (Baqach & Battou,
2024; Pilicita & Barra, 2025). First, BERT embeddings were used to
generate contextualized word representations for each clause. These
embeddings were passed into a Bi-LSTM layer to capture sequential
dependencies in both forward and backward directions. An attention
mechanism then assigned weights to sentiment-relevant tokens, empha-
sizing emotionally charged words. A softmax layer outputted the final
sentiment label (positive, neutral, or negative).

To train and validate the model, we manually annotated 30% of
the clause-level responses using three linguists with PhDs and over five
years of experience in qualitative analysis. Inter-annotator agreement,
measured by Cohen’s 𝜅, reached 0.89, indicating high reliability. The
annotated dataset was then split into a training set (two-thirds) and
a validation set (one-third). After training and hyperparameter tuning,
the model was applied to the remaining 70% of the data (testing set) to
assign sentiment labels. In total, 11,375 labeled clauses were obtained
across all responses.

Once  sentiment  labels  were  assigned,  we  conducted  a  thematic
analysis using NVivo (Siregar & Siregar, 2024)to extract nuanced pat-
terns within each sentiment category (positive, neutral, negative). An
inductive coding approach was applied to the sentiment-labeled clauses
to  identify  recurring  themes,  including  perceived  benefits  (e.g.,  in-
creased efficiency, improved accessibility), concerns (e.g., inaccurate
content, over-reliance), and evaluative comments (e.g., usefulness in
various academic tasks). Each coded clause was further linked to the
respondent’s academic discipline and education level, enabling cross-
tabulation and matrix queries to explore variations in attitudes across
different student subgroups.

This approach enabled us to examine perceived advantages and lim-
itations within each sentiment group and to explore patterns of evalua-
tive emphasis across subgroups. By connecting sentiment classification
with structured thematic analysis, we were able to identify trends not
as changes over time, but as systematic variations in perception across
educational and disciplinary contexts.

2.2.3. Satisfaction with AI-driven learning tools

In this section, we further investigated students’ satisfaction with
specific AI-driven learning tools in the context of their professional
coursework. Here, ‘‘professional coursework’’ refers to the set of aca-
demic tasks—both theoretical and applied—that students are required
to complete as part of their formal university curriculum. These tasks
are not general academic exercises, but rather discipline-specific assign-
ments designed to reflect the practical and theoretical demands of each
field. The integration of AI tools within these assignments offers insight
into both students’ actual usage patterns and their evaluative judgments
regarding tool effectiveness in meeting disciplinary expectations.

To identify the tools students rely on most, we first analyzed survey
data to extract the most frequently cited AI applications across five
academic disciplines. After that, students were invited to complete an
open-ended  satisfaction  survey  administered  via  an  online  platform
for  one  month.  They  could  select  multiple  AI  tools  that  they  used
most frequently in their professional coursework and provide free-text

Y. Sun et al.

comments evaluating the tools’ usefulness, limitations, and alignment
with academic expectations.

During preliminary analysis, we observed a convergence of tool
usage within each discipline: despite the availability of diverse AI tools,
the majority of student responses concentrated around four primary
tools per discipline. Consequently, we selected these most frequently
reported tools for further sentiment and satisfaction analysis across
disciplines. This filtering approach ensured analytical focus while pre-
serving the contextual specificity of each academic field. All sentiment
analysis  procedures  in  this  stage  followed  the  same  preprocessing,
clause-level segmentation, and classification pipeline described in Sec-
tion 2.2.2, ensuring methodological consistency in evaluating students’
qualitative responses across datasets.

2.3. Stage 2: Evaluating AI accuracy in subject-specific knowledge (RQ2)

To assess the accuracy of AI-generated responses in domain-specific
coursework,  we  selected  a  representative  subgroup  of  240  students
from the original cohort of 1472 participants. The selection followed
a stratified sampling strategy to ensure representativeness across mul-
tiple dimensions. Specifically, students were proportionally drawn from
the  same  four  major  regions  of  China  –  Eastern,  Central,  Western,
and Northeastern – according to their original distribution in the full
sample. This ensured that regional diversity, including differences in
educational development and access to technology, was preserved. In
addition to regional balance, we also ensured diversity across institu-
tional types and academic disciplines. Students were recruited from
both  elite  universities  and  regular  universities  to  reflect  the  broad
spectrum of Chinese higher education. Within each institution type, stu-
dents were selected across five major academic disciplines. To ensure
academic readiness and familiarity with domain-specific content, only
students who had completed at least three full years of undergraduate
study  or  one  and  a  half  years  of  graduate  study  were  eligible  for
inclusion.

While domain experts could have independently generated the ques-
tions,  we  intentionally  involved  students  for  both  pedagogical  and
practical  reasons.  Firstly,  due  to  typical  teaching  loads  in  Chinese
higher  education  institutions,  over 95%  of instructors teach  only 1
to 3 core courses per semester, with only a minority (less than 5%)
covering 4 or more. This limited instructional breadth increases the risk
of redundancy or overlap in expert-generated content. In contrast, the
selected 240 students had engaged with a broader array of coursework,
giving them more comprehensive exposure to diverse curricular topics
within their fields. Secondly, the students sourced questions from actual
teaching materials, including instructor-provided textbooks, standard
homework assignments, and past examination papers. These materi-
als  reflect  the  authentic  assessment  environment  of  their  academic
programs.

To maintain academic rigor and ensure broad content representa-
tion, students engaged in structured roundtable sessions where they
collaboratively reviewed, refined, and selected the most relevant and
essential questions from their coursework. This process resulted in a
curated set of 1200 validated items, including multiple-choice (15%),
true/false  (15%),  short-answer  (30%),  and  design-based  or  applied
problem-solving tasks (40%). Each question was further classified by
difficulty level – introductory, intermediate, or advanced – and by cog-
nitive demand, such as conceptual understanding, application, or ana-
lytical reasoning. This carefully balanced structure allowed for a thor-
ough evaluation of AI performance across a diverse range of academic
challenges and cognitive domains.

To ensure disciplinary balance, the total number of questions for
each type was evenly allocated across the five academic fields. For
instance, the 180 multiple-choice items were divided equally, with each
discipline receiving 36 questions—a method consistently applied to the
other formats as well. All questions were carefully aligned with subject-
specific content, deliberately excluding topics from general education

To validate the quality of the constructed questions, subject matter
experts and course instructors conducted multiple rounds of review
and revision. This process helped align the final questions with actual
assessment standards used in university-level coursework. Each item
was systematically categorized by both difficulty level and question
type. This classification enabled a more nuanced and structured eval-
uation of AI performance across different levels of cognitive demand
and task complexity. After finalizing the question set, we employed
ChatGPT (gpt-4-turbo) as the representative generative AI model to pro-
duce responses for each item. We accessed gpt-4-turbo via the OpenAI
API (Application Programming Interface), which allows developers to
interact programmatically with the model by sending requests for text
generation and receiving responses in return.

These AI-generated answers were subsequently assessed for their
correctness,  depth,  and  congruence  with  academic  expectations.  To
measure semantic similarity between the AI responses and the bench-
mark human answers, we applied cosine similarity analysis (Januzaj
& Luma, 2022). Each response was vectorized using term frequency-
inverse document frequency (TF-IDF), allowing for quantitative com-
parison based on the textual alignment between paired responses. Sim-
ilarity scores ranged from 0 (no similarity) to 1 (perfect match), with
higher values indicating closer alignment. Reference answers were ob-
tained from a combination of official textbook solutions and instructor-
submitted materials, including assignments and exam keys archived
by  academic  affairs  offices.  This  approach  provided  a  standardized
and objective basis for evaluating the content accuracy and conceptual
fidelity of ChatGPT’s output.

In  addition  to  the  similarity-based  evaluation,  we  implemented
a  rigorous  expert  assessment  to  gauge  the  quality  and  precision  of
AI-generated answers. A panel of 47 professors – comprising subject-
matter specialists and experienced educators – participated in the re-
view process. Each evaluator independently assessed the AI responses
along three primary dimensions: correctness of content, explanatory
depth, and conformity to academic standards. A 5-point Likert scale
was used for scoring, where 1 denoted a poor or inaccurate response
and 5 indicated a response of high accuracy and structural quality.
To  enhance  evaluation  reliability  and  mitigate  individual  bias,  we
adopted a two-phase cross-validation strategy. In the first phase, one
group of experts rated the responses; in the second phase, a different

2.4. Stage 3: Segmenting students by AI engagement patterns (RQ3)

2.4.1. Participant sampling and AI usage feature definition

To explore variations in how students integrate AI-driven tools into
their academic work, we selected a subset of 800 students from the
original 1472 participants. The selection process followed a stratified
sampling strategy based on region, university type, and academic dis-
cipline to ensure proportional representativeness. Students were invited
to participate in the longitudinal assessment component, and only those
who voluntarily opted in were included in the final sample. Participants
were drawn according to the same regional distribution patterns used
in Study 1, ensuring balanced coverage of the four major geographic
regions of China.

The final sample included 302 students from Eastern China, 228
from Western China, 169 from Northern China, and 101 from Central
and Northeastern China. Students were also proportionally sampled
from  both  elite  and  non-elite  universities,  maintaining  consistency
with  the  broader  Chinese  higher  education  structure.  Within  each
region and institution type, students were further stratified by academic
discipline. The disciplinary composition of the 800 participants mir-
rors that of the full sample in Study 1, with a higher proportion of
students from engineering and humanities, reflecting the enrollment
proportions published by the Ministry of Education. This ensures that
disciplines with larger student populations in China were correspond-
ingly represented in the sample. The 800 student participants were
grouped  into  four  distinct  AI  user  profiles  according  to  four  prin-
cipal dimensions: technological proficiency, frequency and depth of
AI usage, academic discipline, and educational level. These variables
were  directly  extracted  from  the  survey  data  collected  in  Study  1,
where each dimension was measured and categorized through specific
questions designed to capture both subjective perceptions and objective
behaviors.

• Technological proficiency was assessed through a series of ques-
tions asking students to rate their familiarity and comfort with
AI-based  learning  tools.  Participants  were  asked  to  self-assess
their proficiency on a Likert scale, ranging from ‘‘Minimal expo-
sure’’ (i.e., limited or no usage of AI tools) to ‘‘Highly proficient’’
(i.e., regular and confident use of AI tools in academic tasks). This
self-reported measure reflects students’ perceptions of their own
skill level and their confidence in using AI tools effectively.

• Usage  frequency  and  depth  were  measured  by  asking  students
about the frequency (e.g., daily, weekly, monthly) with which
they used AI tools, and the depth of their engagement. Depth was
assessed based on the range of AI applications they used, such
as literature review, writing support, data analysis, and problem-
solving. This variable also captured the students’ reported depth
of engagement by distinguishing between basic tasks (e.g., gram-
mar checking) and more advanced tasks (e.g., programming or
complex data analysis).

• Academic discipline was included as a categorical variable, reflect-
ing the field-specific patterns of AI adoption across domains such
as engineering, humanities, social sciences, and natural sciences.
Students were asked to select their primary field of study, which
allowed us to observe how AI tools are utilized differently across
academic disciplines.

• Educational level was used to differentiate between undergraduate
and graduate students. Participants were asked to report their
current academic level, allowing us to examine whether AI usage
varied based on academic seniority, with graduate students likely
to  engage  in  more  specialized  or  advanced  applications  of  AI
tools.

The full set of classification criteria—including skill level, usage
behavior,  field  of  study,  and  degree  level—is  presented  in  Fig.   2,
which outlines the framework used to develop the student engage-

theoretical  relevance  to  technology  adoption  literature  but  also  for
their practical influence on AI engagement. For instance, an Advanced-
level graduate student in Engineering who uses AI tools weekly across
multiple technical domains (e.g., programming, modeling) may demon-
strate deeper integration than a Daily user in Humanities with only
Intermediate proficiency who primarily uses AI for summarization or
grammar correction. By examining combinations of these factors, the
clustering process aimed to uncover distinct usage profiles that reflect
both technical and contextual diversity in AI integration.

To prepare the dataset for clustering, we encoded categorical vari-
ables as follows: ordinal features (e.g., frequency, proficiency) were
transformed using integer encoding; nominal features (e.g., discipline,
education level) were converted into one-hot encoded vectors. The re-

validation.

2.4.2. Clustering and longitudinal behavior tracking

To segment students into distinct AI engagement profiles, we em-
ployed  a  density-based  clustering  algorithm  (DBSCAN)  (Montano  &

variable  types  and  uneven  density  distributions  (Gholizadeh  et  al.,
2021). Unlike k-means, DBSCAN does not require predefining the num-
ber of clusters and is capable of identifying outliers as noise, making
it particularly suitable for educational data with heterogeneous usage
patterns.

To  uncover  distinct  student  engagement  patterns,  we  conducted
four  independent  clustering  analyses—each  centered  on  one  of  the
following key dimensions. Instead of jointly modeling all features in a
single vector space, we treated each variable cluster as an independent
analytical axis, allowing us to identify and interpret student groupings

3. Results

3.1. Student attitudes and usage patterns (RQ1)

3.1.1. Analysis of students’ AI engagement patterns

Five  categories  were  established  to  classify  how  frequently  stu-
dents interact with AI-enhanced educational technologies: never, rare,
monthly, weekly, and daily. The daily category encompassed individ-
uals engaging with such technologies a minimum of once within each
24-hour period. Weekly classification applied to participants utilizing
these  resources  between  one  to  six  occasions  throughout  a  seven-
day  span.  Those  accessing  AI  technologies  a  minimum  of  once  yet
not exceeding three instances within a thirty-day period constituted
the  monthly  cohort.  Participants  whose  interaction  fell  below  once
monthly were designated as rare users, whereas individuals reporting
zero utilization of these technologies in educational contexts comprised
the never category.

The usage patterns of AI-driven learning tools were examined across
both geographical regions and academic disciplines, as shown in Table
4. Among the 1472 total respondents, 112 students (7.6%) indicated
they had never used AI for learning purposes. This non-user group was
most concentrated in Western China, where 49 students accounted for
43.8% of all respondents in this category—significantly higher than in
any other region. This disparity may reflect broader regional inequali-
ties in educational and technological infrastructure. Prior studies have
shown that Western China has fewer elite institutions—fewer than 7%
of China’s top-tier universities are located in this region—compared
to over 30% in Southeastern China. These structural imbalances may
translate into fewer AI-related educational initiatives, limited internet
penetration, and reduced access to advanced digital learning environ-
ments. While our study did not directly investigate these causal factors,
the observed geographic disparity in AI tool usage aligns with previ-
ously documented trends in educational development across Chinese
regions.

The  results  reveal  substantial  disciplinary  variation  in  students’
dependence on AI-supported learning tools. Engineering students ex-
hibited the highest proportion of daily users, while students in Natural
Sciences reported the lowest proportion of daily AI tool usage. One
possible  explanation  is  that  many  natural  science  fields  emphasize
empirical investigation and hands-on experimentation. Tasks such as
chemical analysis, biological experimentation, or theoretical modeling
in physics often require procedures that may not be easily automated
or supported by current AI technologies.

3.1.2. Analysis of students’ affective attitudes

The sentiment classification yielded a total of 11,375 labeled clauses
across all student responses. As shown in Table  5, 53.7% of all clauses
expressed positive sentiment, followed by 26.9% neutral and 19.4%
negative. Engineering and Social Sciences students reported the highest
levels of positive sentiment, particularly at the undergraduate level.
In contrast, students in the Humanities and Natural Sciences exhibited
more negative sentiment, especially in graduate cohorts. This suggests
that student experiences with AI vary considerably across academic
domains.

Further cross-tabulation revealed that undergraduates generally dis-
played more favorable attitudes than graduate students. For instance,
undergraduate students in Engineering and Social Sciences reported
positive sentiment rates of 60.9% and 64.3%, respectively, compared
to 57.0% and 59.8% among their graduate counterparts. On the other
hand, graduate students in Humanities and Natural Sciences expressed
higher proportions of neutral or negative attitudes, suggesting a more
critical stance toward AI-generated content.

Thematic coding of positive responses revealed frequent praise for
AI’s ability to improve study efficiency, facilitate language tasks, and
support structured problem-solving. Conversely, concerns about con-
tent inaccuracy, ethical issues, and over-reliance were recurrent themes
in negative responses. Notably, graduate students often questioned the
epistemic soundness of AI-generated material, while undergraduates
emphasized usability and convenience. These findings illustrate how
sentiment  not  only  differs  in  magnitude  but  also  reflects  divergent
evaluative priorities among learner groups.

To further explore differences in evaluative focus, we conducted
a comparative thematic analysis across educational levels. As shown
in  Table   6,  undergraduates  most  frequently  emphasized  pragmatic
themes such as Efficiency in Study Support (723 mentions) and Language
Assistance (511 mentions). In addition to structured problem-solving,
undergraduates uniquely highlighted Interface Usability (349 mentions)
and Lack of Personalization (322 mentions) as concerns, reflecting a
practical  and  experience-oriented  perspective.  These  results  suggest
that undergraduates prioritize usability, convenience, and workflow
improvement when engaging with AI tools in their coursework.

In contrast, graduate students exhibited a more epistemically critical
stance. Their most frequently coded theme was Content Inaccuracy or
Hallucination  (476  mentions),  followed  by  Ethical  Concerns  and  Pla-
giarism (429 mentions) and Epistemic Limitations of AI Reasoning (342
mentions), which did not appear in the undergraduate top themes.
These themes indicate heightened sensitivity to the reliability, validity,
and ethical boundaries of AI-generated content. While both groups val-
ued AI’s utility for structured problem-solving and efficiency, graduate
students showed greater awareness of the limitations and potential aca-
demic risks of relying on generative AI, possibly due to their advanced
training in scholarly standards and research integrity.

3.2. Evaluating AI accuracy in subject-specific knowledge (RQ2)

To assess the subject-specific accuracy of AI-generated responses,
we selected a cohort of 240 students representing a broad spectrum
of academic disciplines. After refining for clarity and curricular rel-
evance,  a  total  of  1200  discipline-aligned  questions  were  finalized.
To investigate the performance of AI across different academic levels,
the question set was divided into undergraduate and graduate tiers.
Undergraduate-level questions targeted foundational concepts and ba-
sic problem-solving capabilities, whereas the graduate-level items em-
phasized advanced theoretical reasoning, analytical tasks, and field-
specific applications. This stratification enabled a comparative analysis

of how AI-generated responses vary in terms of accuracy and depth
across differing levels of academic rigor.

We employed ChatGPT (gpt-4-turbo) as the AI tool for generat-
ing answers to all questions. Evaluation of objective question types
was  conducted  via  automated  comparison  with  established  correct
responses. For open-ended and applied tasks, a two-step evaluation
protocol  was  implemented.  First,  each  AI  response  was  vectorized
using term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF), allowing
for semantic comparison with reference answers. Cosine similarity was
then calculated, yielding a score between 0 and 1, where higher values
indicate stronger alignment in terminology, structure, and key ideas.

Reference answers for evaluation were sourced through multiple
channels. For questions derived from textbooks, official solution man-
uals were utilized whenever available. For assignment-based and ex-
amination  items,  we  retrieved  instructor-prepared  answer  keys  that
had been submitted to the academic affairs office as part of course
documentation procedures. These reference materials, accessed with
appropriate institutional permissions, served as the gold standard for
calculating  similarity  metrics  and  evaluating  response  quality.  This
setup  enables  an  objective  measurement  of  how  closely  ChatGPT’s
outputs align with human-crafted academic expectations. Table  8 sum-
marizes the performance outcomes across various academic disciplines
and educational levels. For subjective items, such as short-answer and
applied problem-solving questions, expert assessments were conducted
to determine response quality. For each academic discipline, instruc-
tors evaluated AI-generated responses using the original point values
assigned  to  different  question  types—typically  ranging  from  5  to  8
points for short-answer items and 15 to 25 points for applied problem-
solving tasks. The total score obtained by each group was then divided

4. Discussion

4.1. Summary of key findings

Previous  scholarship  (Song,  2023;  Xiang  et  al.,  2020;  Yu  et  al.,
2024) has identified how developmental variations across regions –
including internet infrastructure, instructional funding disparities, and
technological infrastructure inconsistencies – potentially create asym-
metric advanced AI technology implementation throughout China. Our
investigation  provides  original  insights  through  methodical  analysis
of  AI-facilitated  educational  resource  distribution  patterns  spanning
four principal geographic regions, thereby addressing this knowledge
deficit.  In  Stage  1,  based  on  survey  data  from  1472  students,  we
found significant variation in both the frequency and purpose of gen-
erative AI usage across academic disciplines and regions. Students in

economically developed regions – particularly Southeastern China –
reported more frequent and diversified AI usage, consistent with prior
findings  that  emphasize  the  role  of  digital  access  and  institutional
readiness  in  technology  adoption  (Fan  et  al.,  2024;  Salloum  et  al.,
2024). Disciplinary differences were similarly pronounced: engineering
and humanities students reported higher daily usage rates, whereas
students in natural sciences and social sciences used AI tools less often
and more cautiously. While earlier studies have linked AI uptake to
task type and disciplinary norms (Bauer et al., 2023; McDonald et al.,
2025), our inclusion of regional stratification offers a more nuanced
and context-sensitive understanding of AI integration across diverse
socio-educational landscapes.

Another significant strength of our study lies in its broad disci-
plinary coverage. While most existing research on AI in education tends
to  focus  on  specific  subject  areas  or  narrowly  defined  professional
contexts (Kim & Cho, 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Xu & Ouyang, 2022),
our investigation spans five major disciplines: Engineering, Humani-
ties, Social Sciences, Arts, and Natural Sciences. This multidisciplinary
approach  allows  us  to  uncover  distinct  usage  patterns  that  reflect
the varying cognitive requirements and epistemological orientations of
each field. Our findings demonstrate that students in Engineering and
Natural Sciences often use AI tools for technical tasks such as program-
ming, simulation, and data modeling—areas where automation and
computational precision offer clear advantages. By offloading routine
operations, AI enables students to allocate more cognitive resources to
complex academic tasks such as experimental design and analytical rea-
soning, thereby enhancing problem-solving effectiveness. In contrast,
students in the Humanities, Social Sciences, and Arts report using AI
primarily for text generation, summarization, and translation. In these
disciplines, the limitations of generative AI become more evident—
particularly in tasks requiring critical reflection, contextual sensitivity,
and original expression. This disparity highlights the difficulty of align-
ing AI-generated content with the interpretive and evaluative practices
that are central to meaning-making in these fields. As such, while AI
can support productivity and surface-level writing, its contributions
to deeper academic inquiry remain constrained in more interpretive
disciplines.

In  Stage  2,  we  assessed  the  disciplinary  accuracy  of  ChatGPT’s
responses  to  1200  professional  coursework  questions  and  found  a
clear pattern: while the model demonstrated high accuracy on binary
true/false items and fair performance on multiple-choice questions, its
responses to open-ended and design-intensive tasks were inconsistent
and often lacked depth. These results align with previous research (Lee
et al., 2024; Valmeekam et al., 2022), showing that LLMs excel at fact-
based retrieval but struggle with tasks requiring abstract reasoning and
contextual nuance. By combining expert evaluations with TF-IDF-based
similarity  scoring,  our  analysis  underscores  a  critical  limitation:  al-
though ChatGPT can assist with foundational understanding, it provides
limited support for complex cognitive tasks such as argumentation,
synthesis, and problem-specific reasoning. This finding highlights the
gap between AI’s surface-level fluency and the deeper conceptual rigor
expected in higher education.

In  Stage  3,  we  applied  DBSCAN  clustering  to  a  stratified  sam-
ple  of  800  students  to  identify  distinct  engagement  profiles  based
on  technological  proficiency,  usage  frequency  and  depth,  academic
discipline, and education level. While prior studies have employed clus-
tering techniques to examine learning behaviors (Pansri et al., 2024),
our approach is novel in integrating unsupervised machine learning
with temporal behavioral logs and qualitative reflections. Longitudinal
tracking of AI usage over a one-month period revealed that advanced
users leveraged AI for more complex tasks such as coding, statistical
modeling, and problem-solving. In contrast, users with less experience
primarily relied on AI for simpler functions. Usage frequency further in-
fluenced AI integration, with daily users engaging in advanced research
workflows, while weekly and infrequent users employed AI for more
basic tasks. Differences between undergraduate and graduate students

challenges related to AI’s interpretive limitations. Despite the broad
applicability of AI, students identified limitations in terms of conceptual
accuracy, contextual adaptability, and creative authenticity, underscor-

Stage  4  discoveries  regarding  AI-facilitated  educational  achieve-

such  as  model  design,  where  students  often  note  that  AI-generated

individual problem. These concerns transcend technical domains. Hu-

Taken together, our findings highlight both the promise and the pit-
falls of AI integration in higher education. On the positive side, students
consistently  report  that  generative  AI  tools  enhance  learning  effi-
ciency, support structured academic tasks, and improve accessibility—
especially in areas such as programming, design, and academic writing.
These  observations  reinforce  earlier  findings  regarding  the
productivity-enhancing potential of LLMs in educational contexts (Idris
et al., 2024; Peláez-Sánchez et al., 2024). However, the drawbacks
are equally apparent. Many students and experts expressed concerns
about the superficiality of AI-generated content, lack of domain-specific
rigor, and the potential erosion of critical thinking skills and academic
integrity. These limitations echo growing scholarly concerns (Omrani
et al., 2022; Yusuf et al., 2024) that caution against over-reliance on AI
as a substitute for human judgment, creativity, and deep disciplinary
engagement.

4.2. Limitations and contributions

While this study offers a comprehensive examination of AI-driven
learning  assistance  in  Chinese  higher  education,  several  limitations
must be acknowledged. With regard to participant selection, given the
vast size and heterogeneity of China’s higher education system – from
elite research-intensive universities to vocational colleges – it remains
challenging to ensure equal representation across all institutional tiers.
For instance, this study may underrepresent students from vocational
institutions or schools with limited digital infrastructure, where access
to AI tools and instructional support may differ substantially (Ashraf
et al., 2017; Gu et al., 2018). Moreover, the study was limited by its
two-month intervention period, which may not have allowed sufficient
time  to  capture  the  long-term  effects  of  AI  integration  on  learning
outcomes. Future research could explore the impact of a longer inter-
vention period and a larger sample size to yield more comprehensive
results.

A further limitation lies in the reliance on self-reported data for
AI  usage,  where  students  recorded  their  engagement  through  logs,
which could introduce bias or inaccuracies. While both objective and
subjective assessments were used to evaluate academic performance,

Y. Sun et al.

they  may  not  fully  capture  the  full  range  of  learning  behaviors  or
problem-solving strategies influenced by AI tools. Additionally, in RQ4,
we introduced historical examination scores from 2018 to 2020 as a
baseline for comparison. This period predates the use of GenAI tools,
offering  a  useful  point  of  reference  for  assessing  improvements  in
performance in 2024. However, we acknowledge that other variables –
such as changes in teaching methods, curriculum adjustments, or indi-
vidual student factors (e.g., motivation, study habits, prior knowledge)
– could have influenced the results.

4.3. Implications

This study contributes to the growing body of literature on AI in
education by introducing a multidimensional, context-sensitive frame-
work that accounts for both disciplinary and regional diversity in AI
engagement. Existing theoretical models (Abulail et al., 2025; Nikolic
et al., 2024) – such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) or the
Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) – often
conceptualize AI usage as a generalized construct. By incorporating
variables such as geographic region, professional coursework demands,
and behavioral log data, our study extends current models to better
reflect the socio-academic realities of higher education in China. Our
results demonstrate that the perceived usefulness and affective attitudes
toward generative AI vary significantly by discipline and educational
level,  indicating  that  AI  adoption  is  not  simply  a  function  of  tool
availability, but is also shaped by epistemological expectations and
learning culture.

In addition, the results of this study provide actionable insights
for  educators,  administrators,  and  technology  developers  aiming  to
support responsible and effective AI integration in higher education.
First, given the disciplinary disparities in AI engagement, institutional
support strategies should not adopt a one-size-fits-all approach. Instead,
AI training programs, tool recommendations, and curriculum design
should  be  tailored  to  the  specific  needs  and  cognitive  demands  of
different  academic  fields.  Regional  differences  in  access  and  usage
highlight the need for targeted policy interventions to ensure equi-
table AI adoption. Universities in less-developed regions may require
infrastructural support, faculty development programs, and inclusive
access to AI tools to close the digital divide. Additionally, the findings
underscore the importance of embedding AI literacy into the formal
curriculum—not just as a technical skillset, but as a component of

5. Conclusion and future research

Acknowledgments

This study examined how university students across China perceive,
engage with, and are affected by generative AI tools in professional
coursework through an integrated four-stage research design. By se-
quentially linking students’ perceptions and usage patterns, the dis-
ciplinary accuracy of AI-generated content, longitudinal engagement
behaviors, and academic performance outcomes, the study provides
a  coherent  explanation  of  how  generative  AI  operates  within  real
educational contexts. The findings demonstrate that while generative
AI offers substantial learning support, its effectiveness and risks are
systematically shaped by disciplinary norms, students’ educational lev-
els,  and  regional  contexts.  Together,  the  four  stages  move  beyond
isolated evaluations of AI adoption or performance and instead show
how attitudes, tool reliability, engagement trajectories, and learning
outcomes jointly determine the educational value of generative AI in
higher education.
