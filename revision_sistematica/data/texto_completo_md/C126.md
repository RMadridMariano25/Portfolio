This  study investigates how specific prompting behaviors  influence the academic performance of engineering
students using ChatGPT. A total of 128 fourth-year students across four engineering programs participated in
weekly sessions involving rotating task types: case analysis, engineering design, multi-step problem-solving, and
experimental data analysis. A Python-based interface connected students to ChatGPT-4o via the API, logging all
interactions and implementing stratified randomization of AI access across the 16-week semester. Written as-
signments were used for both grading and analyzing AI content integration. Ten metrics were designed to capture
student behaviors—eight focusing on prompting (AI Query Count, Query Depth and Structure, Query Efficiency,
Prompt Refinement Depth, Response Utility, Response Complexity, Response Reliance, and AI-Driven Problem-
Solving) and two assessing writing quality (Structural Complexity Score and Content Richness and Information
Density  Score).  Inter-metric  analysis  examined  how  these  behaviors  related  to  one  another  and  to  academic
performance. Results showed that AI Query Efficiency, which reflects how effectively students crafted clear, well-
structured prompts, and AI-Driven Problem-Solving, which captures how strategically they integrated AI output
into  their  reasoning,  were  the  strongest  predictors  of  academic  success.  These  findings  were  supported  by
Mann–Whitney U tests comparing AI and non-AI groups, as well as Spearman correlations, Random Forest re-
gressors,  partial  dependence  plots,  principal  component  analysis,  and  mixed-effects  modeling,  and  remained
significant even after accounting for students' cumulative GPA. The results suggest that prompting strategy plays
a meaningful role in shaping how effectively students use AI in engineering education.

1. Introduction

1.1. Context and rationale

Since 2022, ChatGPT has attracted widespread scholarly attention
across higher education (Ma, 2025). In engineering education, students
increasingly  use  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  tools  such  as  ChatGPT  to
support mathematical problem-solving, programming, laboratory anal-
ysis,  technical  writing,  and  iterative  design-oriented  learning  tasks
(Ghosh,  2024).  Despite  this  growing  integration,  current  research  on
ChatGPT within the discipline relies largely on self-reported perceptions
rather  than  observable  behavioral  evidence  (Qadir,  2023).  Existing
studies  frequently  examine  attitudes,  satisfaction,  and  perceived

usefulness  instead  of  examining  how  students  interact  with  ChatGPT
during academic tasks, including how they prompt AI systems, refine
generated  responses,  or  integrate  AI-generated  content  into  academic
work  (Haindl  &  Weinberger,  2024;  Sajawal  &  Kittur,  2024).  Experi-
mental  and  task-based  studies  have  similarly  focused  on  isolated  as-
signments  or  short-term  activities,  providing  limited  visibility  into
sustained  interaction  patterns  across  learning  contexts  (Nikolic  et  al.,
2023; Shoufan, 2023b). Consequently, there remains limited evidence
of whether AI-assisted learning contributes to measurable differences in
academic  achievement  and  engagement  (Ritchie  &  Warren,  2024;
Sawalha,  Taj,  &  Shoufan,  2024;  Shuhaiber,  Kuhail,  &  Salman,  2024;
Tsai, Ong, & Chen, 2023).

To address these gaps in the literature, this study develops and val-
idates  a  set  of  behavioral  AI  interaction  metrics  designed  to  capture
patterns of prompting, response refinement, and AI-response integration
during academic and laboratory tasks. By examining these interaction
patterns across a semester-long educational context, the study seeks to
provide deeper insight into AI-assisted engagement and support more
effective, evidence-based integration of AI systems within engineering
education.

Methodologically,  the  study  focuses  on  the  development  and  vali-
dation of behavioral interaction metrics, while educationally it seeks to
examine how these interaction patterns relate to academic performance,
engagement, and AI-assisted academic behaviors. Data were collected
using an adapted logging interface based on a prior version developed by
the  authors  (Isaza  Domínguez,  Velasquez  Clavijo,  Robles-G´omez,  &
Pastor-Vargas,  2024),  enabling  detailed  behavioral  analysis  of  stu-
dent–AI interactions across a university semester. The study additionally
employed  a  triangulated  analytical  framework  integrating  statistical
modeling, machine-learning analyses, and qualitative thematic valida-
tion to examine relationships between prompting behaviors, AI-assisted
interaction patterns, and academic performance outcomes.

1.2. Theoretical framework and objectives

The  study  examined  student  performance  through  graded  written
assignments completed during tutorial and laboratory sessions across a
semester-long randomized investigation involving engineering students
from four courses: Advanced Software Engineering, Intelligent Control
Systems, Process Optimization, and Environmental Impact Assessment.
To  expose  students  to  varied  cognitive  and  disciplinary  demands,
graded written assignments were structured around a rotating four-week
task cycle consisting of Case Study Analysis, Design Reports, Multi-Step
Problem Solving, and Data Analysis activities. These assignment types
were  selected  because  they  represent  complementary  forms  of  engi-
neering reasoning, technical communication, analytical interpretation,
and  procedural  problem-solving  commonly  used  within  engineering
education.  Case  Study  Analysis  activities  were  grounded  in  construc-
tivist and situated learning theory (Johri & Olds, 2011; Kantar, 2013),
requiring  students  to  interpret  engineering  scenarios  and  justify
analytical or design decisions within contextualized problem settings.
Design Report activities were informed by design- and problem-based
learning  principles  (G´omez  Puente,  van  Eijck,  &  Jochems,  2013),
emphasizing  iterative  reasoning,  constraint  evaluation,  technical
communication, and solution development. Multi-Step Problem-Solving
activities  were  aligned  with  cognitive  apprenticeship  frameworks
(Moseley  &  McCord,  2014),  guiding  students  through  structured
analytical reasoning processes involving procedural decomposition and
stepwise  problem  articulation.  Data  Analysis  activities  were  framed
within  inquiry-based  learning  and  quantitative  reasoning  approaches

(Chen &  Chen, 2025; Roohr, Graf, & Liu, 2014), requiring students to
interpret numerical evidence and justify conclusions through empirical
analysis.

Guided by this educational and task-based study design, the analysis
examined two complementary dimensions of AI-assisted academic per-
formance. The first focused on characteristics of students' written aca-
demic  work  through  writing-quality  metrics  applied  to  assignment
submissions.  The  second  focused  on  behavioral  interaction  patterns
derived  from  student–AI  exchanges  recorded  during  assignment
completion.  Together,  these  analytical  components  were  used  to
examine  how  prompting  behaviors,  writing  characteristics,  and  AI-
assisted  interaction  patterns  related  to  observed  academic  perfor-
mance outcomes across the evaluated engineering activities.

Writing  quality  was  evaluated  using  two  complementary  metrics:
Structural Complexity and Content Richness and Information Density.
Together, these metrics examined linguistic sophistication, information
density,  syntactic  organization,  and  cognitive  elaboration  within  stu-
dents'  written  academic  work  across  AI-assisted  and  non-AI  learning
conditions.  From  a  theoretical  perspective,  Structural  Complexity  re-
flects aspects of higher-order reasoning and metacognitive organization
during  written  expression  (Torrance,  Thomas,  &  Robinson,  2010),
whereas Content Richness and Information Density reflects aspects of
cognitive  elaboration  and  academic  discourse  sophistication  through
lexically rich and information-dense language use (Biber, 2006). These
measures  were  included  to  examine  whether  AI-assisted  writing  was
associated with  differences in the quality and  organization of written
academic work.

It  is  important  to  note  that  assignment  scores  represented  perfor-
mance  outcomes  on  engineering  tasks  completed  during  tutorial  and
laboratory  sessions,  while  the  writing-quality  metrics  evaluated  char-
acteristics  of  students'  written  expression  within  those  assignments.
These  measures  were  therefore  interpreted  as  indicators  of  academic
task performance rather than direct evidence of learning itself.

The second component of the study focused on student–AI interac-
tion  behaviors  and  was  grounded  in  self-regulated  learning  and
cognitive-engagement theory, which view learning as an active process
of  planning,  monitoring,  and  evaluating  one's  understanding  (Ng,
Leung, Chu, &  Qiao, 2021). Within these theoretical perspectives, be-
haviors such as prompting, response evaluation, prompt refinement, and
integration  of  AI-generated  information  into  written  assignments  and
laboratory  activities  may  reflect  broader  processes  associated  with
metacognitive  regulation,  feedback  interpretation,  cognitive  engage-
ment, and adaptive learning. ChatGPT's adaptive feedback may reduce
extraneous cognitive load and scaffold higher-order reasoning (Wu, Lee,
Li, Huang, &  Huang, 2024), although these benefits depend on delib-
erate and reflective use aligned with instructional goals (Kasneci et al.,
2023; Lo et al., 2024).

Accordingly,  prompting  behaviors  with  ChatGPT  may  serve  as
observable indicators of cognitive and metacognitive engagement dur-
ing  AI-assisted  academic  tasks.  In  this  study,  behavioral  interaction
metrics  refer  to  quantitative  measures  derived  from  student–AI  in-
teractions,  including  patterns  related  to  prompting,  response  refine-
ment, and integration of AI-generated content into student work. These
theoretical perspectives provided the conceptual basis for interpreting
observable AI-interaction behaviors during task completion.

To support interpretation of these interaction patterns, the metrics
were organized into three broader behavioral dimensions representing
related  forms  of  prompting,  reasoning,  and  AI-assisted  interaction.
These dimensions provided a higher-level conceptual structure for the
analysis, while the individual metric sections later in the methodology
describe the specific theoretical rationale and computational procedures
underlying each metric.

The first dimension, Strategic & Reasoning Behaviors, captures how
students used AI to support purposeful problem-solving, cognitive effi-
ciency, and structured reasoning during academic tasks. These behav-
iors reflect strategic effort regulation, metacognitive planning, and use

The  dimension  is  grounded  in  self-regulated  learning,  cognitive  load

The second dimension, Linguistic & Iterative Sophistication Behav-

prompt refinement and iterative AI interaction with self-monitoring and
evolving  cognitive  strategies  during  AI-assisted  tasks  (Chang,  Lin,

The third dimension, Regulation &  Over-Prompting Behaviors, ex-
amines patterns associated with prompting frequency, dependence on
AI-generated  material,  and  regulation  of  external  cognitive  support
during academic tasks. These behaviors are connected to cognitive load
management,  epistemic  agency,  and  task  regulation  within  AI-
supported  educational  environments.  Recent  studies  have  identified
prompting frequency, dependence on generated responses, and AI reuse
patterns  as  important  dimensions  of  AI  literacy  and  self-regulated
engagement  during  academic  work  (Ammari,  Chen,  Zaman,  &
Garimella, 2025; Lee & Palmer, 2025).

In  contrast  to  the  previous  dimensions,  which  focus  on  strategic

dents  regulated  reliance  on  AI  systems  during  task  completion.  High
prompting  frequency  or  excessive  reuse  of  generated  content  may
indicate  reduced  metacognitive  regulation  or  greater  dependence  on
external support, whereas balanced prompting behavior and selective
integration of AI-generated information may reflect more self-regulated
and reflective interaction patterns.

Methodologically, the study sought to develop and validate behav-
ioral  interaction  metrics  capable  of  capturing  prompting  patterns,
response refinement behaviors, and AI-assisted interaction characteris-
tics during assignment completion. Educationally, the study examined
how  these  interaction  patterns  related  to  academic  performance  out-
comes, writing-quality characteristics, and students' integration of AI-
generated content into coursework.

Accordingly,  the  study  pursued  the  following  methodological  and

educational objectives:

1.  To develop and validate behavioral interaction metrics that capture
how  engineering  students  interact  with  ChatGPT  during  academic
tasks.

2.  To examine whether AI-assisted learning conditions were associated
with differences in the quality and organization of students' written
academic work.

3.  To examine how prompting behaviors theoretically associated with
self-regulation  and  cognitive  engagement  relate  to  variation  in
assignment performance.

4.  To analyze how students adapt and integrate AI-generated content

into engineering assignments.

By  examining  associations  between  prompting  behavior  and  aca-
demic performance outcomes on assignments, this study contributes to
emerging frameworks for AI literacy and provides evidence to inform
curriculum  design  that  promotes  effective  and  responsible  use  of
generative AI in engineering education. Moreover, the strong predictive
relevance of prompting behaviors within the evaluated models in this
study  suggests  that  strategic  and  reflective  AI  use  may  represent  an

important  component  of  effective  AI-supported  academic  practice  in
engineering education.

2. Methods

2.1. Overview of methodological framework

This study consisted of two complementary methodological compo-
nents:  (1)  the  development  and  validation  of  writing  quality  and
behavioral  AI-interaction  metrics,  and  (2)  the  experimental  and
analytical  evaluation  of  these  metrics  within  AI-assisted  engineering
education contexts. The following sections first describe the theoretical
grounding,  validation  procedures,  computational  structure,  and  cate-
gorization  of  the  developed  metrics,  followed  by  the  study  design,
participant  structure,  data  collection  procedures,  and  statistical  and
machine-learning  analyses  used  to  evaluate  relationships  between
prompting behaviors, academic performance, and AI-assisted academic
engagement.

2.2. Metric design and multi-stage validation framework

Drawing from the theoretical framework presented in Section 1.2,
each metric was designed to translate theoretically grounded processes
into  quantifiable  writing  and  behavior-based  indicators.  Individual
metrics were structured and calculated through theoretically grounded
subcomponents and weighting schemes reflecting the educational pro-
cesses each metric was intended to capture, with all final metric outputs
normalized  to  a  standardized  0–100  scale  to  support  comparability
across metrics. The metric validation process is outlined below:

1. Expert Review of Components: Eight experts in engineering edu-
cation,  AI  literacy,  and  academic  writing  evaluated  the  rationale,
components,  and  weightings  using  Likert-scale  and  open-ended
feedback.  When  consensus  emerged,  weights  were  adjusted  using
the  average  of  suggested  values;  otherwise,  original  weights  were
retained to balance theoretical consistency with expert feedback. The
surveys are available at OSF (Isaza Domínguez, 2025a).

2.  Artifact Scoring and Rater Triangulation: A sample of 30 student
artifacts was independently scored by three expert raters to validate
each  metric.  Triangulating  across  disciplinary  domains  improved
interpretive validity and reduced potential rater bias (Schlunegger,
Zumstein-Shaha, & Palm, 2024). Scoring rubrics were grounded in
the  theoretical  basis  of  each  metric  and  applied  on  0–100  scales,
following best practices in content analysis (Krippendorff, 2018) and
construct  validation  (Rusticus,  2023). The  sample  size  aligns  with
instrument development norms (Johanson &  Brooks, 2010). These
scores are available at OSF (Isaza Domínguez, 2025a).

3.  Quantitative Validity and Reliability Checks: Construct validity
for each metric was assessed through Spearman's rank correlations
between  expert  ratings  and  automated  metric  outputs,  following
established validation practices in AI-supported education (Dai et al.,
2024).  Inter-rater  reliability  was  evaluated  using  the  two-way
random-effects  Intraclass  Correlation  Coefficient  (ICC[2,k]),  con-
firming stable agreement across independent raters. All correlation
and  reliability  values  met  or  exceeded  accepted  benchmarks  for
moderate-to-high  validity  (ρ  > 0.50)  and  reliability  (ICC  > 0.60),
indicating  that  the  automated  scoring  procedures  aligned  closely
with  expert  judgments.  A  complete  summary  of  the  quantitative

2.3. Writing quality metrics

The writing quality metrics included the Structural Complexity Score
and the Content Richness and Information Density Score, which evalu-
ated characteristics of students' written academic expression, syntactic
sophistication,  lexical  richness,  and  information  density  across  the
evaluated engineering tasks.

2.3.1. Structural complexity score

Structural  Complexity  captures  the  syntactic  sophistication  of  stu-
dent  writing  by  quantifying  both  sentence  length  and  grammatical
complexity. Syntactic elaboration is a recognized proxy for higher-order
cognitive activity in academic texts (Scott & Balthazar, 2013; Beers &
Nagy,  2011).  From  a
theory  perspective,  structural
complexity  may  reflect  aspects  of  reasoning,  metacognitive  planning,
and  expressive  capacity—particularly  valuable  in  engineering  educa-
tion, where students must articulate logical processes and layered ar-
guments  (Pikulski  &  Templeton,  2004;  Torrance  et  al.,  2010).
Accordingly, Structural Complexity helps evaluate how effectively stu-
dents organize and express technical knowledge in writing.

learning

The  metric  was  computed  by  combining  two  weighted  sub-
components:  Sentence  Length  (40%)  and  Grammatical  Complexity
(60%).  Sentence  length  measured  the  average  number  of  words  per
sentence,  and  grammatical  complexity  assessed  the  frequency  of
advanced syntactic structures such as subordinate and relative clauses.

2.3.2. Content richness and information density score

Content  Richness  and  Information  Density  assess  how  effectively
students express complex ideas using lexically rich, information-dense
language. It targets two core dimensions of academic writing quality:
lexical  diversity  and  lexical  density—both  widely  recognized  as  in-
dicators  of  cognitive  elaboration  and  expressive  depth  (Biber,  2006;
Nasseri & Thompson, 2021; Uccelli et al., 2015).

This  metric  integrates  two  subcomponents:  the  type–token  ratio,
which measures the ratio of unique word types to total tokens and re-
flects vocabulary diversity (Kettunen, 2014), and lexical density, which
captures the proportion of content words—nouns, verbs, adjectives, and
adverbs—relative  to  total  word  count  (Mohamed  &  Abdallah,  2021).
These features represent complementary aspects of linguistic sophisti-
cation: lexical diversity has been associated with conceptual elaboration
and  expressive  sophistication  and  flexible  language  use,  while  lexical
density  reflects  information  compression  and  meaning  concentration
(Crossley & McNamara, 2012). Equal weighting of type–token ratio and
lexical density was used, consistent with studies affirming their balanced
theoretical  value  for  writing  quality  (Johansson,  2008;  Malvern,
Richards, Chipere, & Dur´an, 2004).

2.4. Strategic & reasoning behaviors

This dimension included the AI Query Efficiency Score and the AI-
Driven Problem-Solving Score, which examined how students used AI
to  support  strategic  prompting,  cognitive  efficiency,  structured
reasoning, and problem-solving during academic tasks.

2.4.1. AI query efficiency score

AI Query Efficiency measures how effectively students engaged with
AI by quantifying the ratio between prompt quality and frequency. It
evaluates  how  much  cognitive  and  linguistic  sophistication  a  student
achieved per prompt submitted during a session. High values may reflect
efficient engagement, issuing few but well-structured, strategic prompts,
while low values often indicate inefficiency, such as excessive trial-and-
error or vague queries with limited precision.

This  metric  aligns  with  models  of  self-regulated  learning,  particu-
larly  those  emphasizing  resource  allocation  and  strategic  effort.
Zimmerman (2002) described proficient learners as those who optimize
task engagement by planning and regulating effort. In this context, such
regulation  is  observable  through  efficient  prompting,  where  fewer
prompts yield greater informational value.

This metric also draws on cognitive load theory, which posits that
instruction should minimize unnecessary effort while maximizing task-
relevant  processing  (Sweller,  Van  Merri¨enboer,  &  Paas,  2019).  Stu-
dents with high scores may be managing extraneous load by articulating
their needs clearly in fewer, more effective queries.

This metric was computed as the ratio of a student's AI Query Depth
& Structure Score to their total number of prompts submitted during a
session.

2.4.2. AI-driven problem-solving score

This  metric  evaluates  how  effectively  students  leveraged  AI-
generated content to support structured reasoning, conceptual integra-
tion, and original idea development, which have been identified as key
indicators of deep problem-solving in engineering education (English,
2023). It comprises three subcomponents: Stepwise Alignment (40%),
Conceptual Application (40%), and Independent Expansion (20%).

Stepwise  Alignment  draws  on  scaffolding  theory  and  cognitive
apprenticeship,  where  students  benefit  from  modeled  reasoning  pro-
cesses provided by an expert (or AI tutor). Following the AI's logic can
reduce  cognitive load and  promote  internalization  of  expert-like stra-
tegies (Fern´andez, Wegerif, Mercer, & Rojas-Drummond, 2015).

Conceptual Application reflects schema acquisition from Cognitive
Load  Theory  (Sweller,  Paas,  &  Renkl,  2003).  Students  reuse  domain-
specific  concepts  introduced  by  the  AI  to  build  transferable  mental
models. Renkl (2014) emphasized that learning from examples is most
effective  when  students  abstract  principles  rather  than  copy  surface
features—a  process  captured  by  this  metric.  Xu  and  Ouyang  (2022)
similarly  found  that  AI  tools  enhance  learning  when  they  scaffold
knowledge application and conceptual integration.

Independent Expansion assesses whether students move beyond AI
content  through  original  contributions.  Semantic  novelty  captures
divergence from the AI's ideas, while syntactic complexity, indexed via
clause elaboration, reflects linguistic maturity. Crossley, Muldner, and
McNamara  (2016) and  Casal  and  Lee  (2019) suggest  that  semantic
elaboration  and  syntactic  depth  predict  writing  quality.  Crossley  and
Kim  (2022) further  demonstrated  that  advanced  academic  writing
evolves through increased syntactic embedding and structural control.

This  dimension  included  the  Prompt  Refinement  Depth  Score,  AI
Response  Utility  Score,  AI  Response  Complexity  Score,  and  AI  Query
Depth  &  Structure  Score,  which  evaluated  how  students  refined
prompts,  interpreted  AI-generated  responses,  and  transformed  gener-
ated information into more linguistically and conceptually sophisticated

academic work.

2.5.1. Prompt refinement depth score

Prompt  Refinement  Depth  captures  the  extent  to  which  students
iteratively  enhanced  their  prompts  during  sessions  by  increasing  syn-
tactic  complexity  and  semantic  depth.  This  metric  is  grounded  in
Vygotsky  and  Cole's  (1978) theory  of  progressive  internalization  and
reflects  metacognitive  control  within  model-supported  learning  envi-
ronments.  Prompt  refinement  has  been  closely  tied  to  self-regulated
learning (Arora et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2023), serving as an exter-
nalization of evolving cognitive strategies. Shi et al. (2025) emphasize
the value of refined prompts as indicators of self-monitoring, adaptive
goal management, and literacy in interacting with AI.

Each  measure  of  Prompt  Refinement  Depth  was  derived  from  a
sequence of related prompts that demonstrated increasing complexity
and elaboration. The final value was calculated by averaging weighted
measures of syntactic gain and semantic evolution across all identified
transitions across prompts within a session.

2.5.2. AI response utility score

This metric quantifies how students incorporate AI-generated con-
tent into their writing, prioritizing originality, semantic transformation,
and selective integration, three hallmarks of pedagogically sound AI use.
As  Stojanovi´c,  Cvetkovi´c,  and  Stankovi´c  (2023) argue,  meaningful
engagement with generative AI involves discernment, not reproduction.
The Semantic Novelty component (45%), weighted most heavily, cap-
tures how much a student's writing diverges from AI output. This focus
on originality is grounded in educational theory: Wang (2024) contends
that preserving one's voice in AI-assisted writing enhances creativity and
learning,  while  Barrot  (2023) warns  that  overreliance  may  reduce
learning and raise plagiarism risks. Semantic novelty is further anchored
in Craik and Lockhart's (1972) depth-of-processing framework, which
posits that rephrasing and reconstructing ideas fosters deeper compre-
hension than copying.

The Conceptual Transformation subcomponent (40%) assesses how
students rephrase or reinterpret AI suggestions—an act of “constructive
learning”  per  Chi  and  Wylie  (2014).  This  aligns  with  the  knowledge-
transforming  model  (Scardamalia  &  Bereiter,  1987),  where  meaning-
ful reworking of input fosters higher-order thinking. Uemlianin (2000)
and  Thai  (2021) also  highlight  paraphrasing  as  a  metacognitive  and
memory-enhancing strategy, reinforcing this component's foundations.
The  third  subcomponent,  Contribution  to  Final  Assignment,  mea-
sures selective reuse of AI-generated ideas, weighted modestly at 15% to
avoid  rewarding  superficial  similarity.  It  draws  from  source-based
writing literature (Hirvela & Du, 2013) and is supported by Fangzhou,
Lin, and Lai (2024), who found that students using self-regulated stra-
tegies integrated and adapted AI content more meaningfully. Ng et al.
(2021) echo  this  view,  framing  ethical  and  selective  application  of
generative tools as central to AI literacy.

2.5.3. AI response complexity score

AI  Response  Complexity  provides  a  self-reported  measure  of  stu-
dents' perceived cognitive engagement when interpreting and applying
AI-generated  content.  While  behavioral  metrics  in  this  study  assess
observable interactions (e.g., prompt logs or writing samples), response
complexity  offers  complementary  insight  into  learners'  internal  pro-
cessing, specifically, how challenging, interpretable, and adaptable the
AI responses felt.

This  metric  is  grounded  in  five  constructs  from  recent  literature:
Semantic  Interpretation  Depth  (Baek,  Tate,  &  Warschauer,  2024),
Rephrasing  Behavior  (Gerlich,  2025),  Confidence  in  Clarity  (Cau  &
Spano,  2025),  Usefulness  Complexity  (Tierney,  Peasey,  &  Gould,
2025),  and  Cognitive  Effort  (Gkintoni,  Antonopoulou,  Sortwell,  &
Halkiopoulos,  2025).  These  dimensions  reflect  core  concerns  in  AI-
integrated  education:  depth  of  understanding,  adaptation  difficulty,
mental workload, and student trust in AI outputs.

The instrument consists of 10 Likert-scale items (two per construct),
administered immediately after every AI-access session. Responses were
retrospective and focused on the session as a whole. A two-phase vali-
dation process supported the reliability of this metric. First, three ex-
perts  in  AI  education  reviewed  each  item  for  clarity  and  construct
alignment. Second, internal consistency was tested using 30 responses
from  Week  5  (all  four  engineering  courses),  yielding  Cronbach's  α  =
0.81. The full survey can be found in OSF (Isaza Domínguez, 2025a).

2.5.4. AI query depth & structure score

This metric was developed to evaluate the cognitive and linguistic
complexity of student prompts submitted to AI. This metric serves as a
behavioral proxy for self-regulated learning and cognitive engagement,
grounded in Zimmerman's (2002) framework of metacognitive planning
and Sweller et al.'s (2019) cognitive load theory. It also reflects insights
from Schreiter and Gipp (2023), who identified syntactic diversity and
logical reasoning as primary dimensions of prompt quality in technical
education. High values in Query Depth and Structure reflect students'
ability  to  organize,  segment,  and  articulate  queries  that  promote
meaningful  model  responses  and  support  deeper  educational
engagement.

This  metric  was  computed  from  three  weighted  subcomponents:
lexical and syntactic structure (35%), multi-step problem-solving depth
(35%), and query focus and clarity (30%).

2.6. Regulation & Over-Prompting Behaviors

This dimension included AI Query Count and the AI Response Reli-
ance Score, which evaluated prompting frequency, dependence on AI-
generated content, and regulation of external cognitive support during
AI-assisted academic tasks.

2.6.1. AI query count (QC)

AI Query Count captures the number of distinct prompts issued by
students during each session, serving as an indicator of engagement and
task  persistence. This  behavioral metric  is  grounded in  empirical and
theoretical literature. Ammari et al. (2025), in a large-scale analysis of
log  data,  found  that  university  students  submitted  an  average  of  6.1
prompts  per  session—establishing  prompt  frequency  as  a  behavioral
marker of user interaction depth.

Similarly, Lee and Palmer (2025) identified prompt frequency as an
underexplored but important dimension of literacy and task regulation.
Thus, this metric functions as a foundational interaction metric reflect-
ing how often students engage with AI during academic tasks.

2.6.2. AI response reliance score

This  metric  captures  the  extent  to  which  students  reused  or  inte-
grated AI-generated content into their written assignments. Developed
as a behavioral proxy for epistemic agency and self-regulated learning,
this metric is grounded in theories of cognitive load (Sweller et al., 2019)
and instructional scaffolding (Van de Pol, Volman, & Beishuizen, 2010).
High values may indicate overreliance on external tools and diminished
metacognitive regulation (Azevedo, Moos, Johnson, & Chauncey, 2010;
Zimmerman,  2002),  whereas  moderate  values  may  reflect  strategic
support-seeking during task execution.

This  metric  is  a  composite  measure  derived  from  five  weighted
subcomponents:  text  similarity  (40%),  direct  copy-pasting  (10%),
structural  similarity  (20%),  prompt–response  consistency  (20%),  and
query-to-submission linkage (10%). In the results, the inverse value was
reported so that higher scores represent less reliance.

2.10. Consent and investigation approval

Procedures followed established ethical guidelines for minimal-risk

educational research:

• Informed Consent: During the first week, students signed consent
forms  confirming  voluntary  participation,  withdrawal  rights,  and
anonymization  of  academic  work.  AI  interaction  logging  was  dis-
closed,  but  specific  prompt-response  tracking  was  omitted  to  pre-
serve  natural  behavior,  an  accepted  practice  in  minimal-risk
educational research (Cohen, Manion, & Morrison, 2018).

• Anonymized  Identification:  Each  student  received  a  random  ID
used for AI logins and assignment labeling. This allowed matching
logs and assignments without exposing personal data.

• CGPA Data Use: Separate consent to access cumulative GPA (CGPA)
was  obtained  post-semester  to  avoid  influencing  student  behavior
during the study.

• Ethics  Approval:  The  study  was  approved  by  the  General  Direc-
torate  of  Research,  Universidad  de  los  Llanos  (Protocol  009,
September 13, 2023).

In  accordance  with  the  approved  ethics  protocol,  CGPA  and  raw
textual data (student prompts, AI responses, and written assignments)
were treated as confidential educational records and were not shared
publicly.  Only  a  derived,  anonymized  dataset,  containing  assignment
scores,  computed  AI  interaction  and  writing-quality  metrics,  and
contextual variables (course, week, attendance, AI access condition, and
activity type), was released for reproducibility purposes, consistent with
institutional  data  protection  policies  and  participant  consent  (Isaza
Domínguez, 2025a).

Ethical safeguards addressed potential risks such as overreliance on
AI,  reduced  autonomy,  and  diminished  critical  thinking.  These  were
mitigated by  restricting  AI  use to  classroom  sessions  under instructor
supervision,  alternating  AI  and  non-AI  conditions  weekly,  and  incor-
porating  reflection-based  tasks  designed  to  promote  independent
reasoning and metacognitive awareness.

2.12. Bias avoidance

To preserve the validity of the experiment, multiple sources of po-
tential bias were addressed through procedural, instructional, and sta-
tistical controls.

1.  Hardware and Software Controls: All students in the AI group used
university-issued  laptops  preconfigured  with  the  custom  Python
interface limited to ChatGPT-4o. Browser access was blocked, per-
sonal  devices  were  prohibited,  and  mobile  phones  were  collected
before  each  session  to  prevent  unauthorized  AI  use.  This  setup
ensured uniform access to AI tools and aligns with equity-centered
in
design  principles  that  promote  fairness  and  consistency
technology-supported learning (Reich & Ito, 2017).

2.  Ethical Logging and Consent: AI interactions were automatically
logged with anonymized student IDs and timestamps, saved in .txt
format,  and  securely  emailed  to  the  research  team.  Although  stu-
dents consented to their data being used, they were not informed that
individual  queries  would  be  recorded—an  ethically  permissible
practice in behavioral research when necessary to preserve natural
behavior, provided participants are debriefed (Cohen et al., 2018).
3.  Task and Instructional Equivalence: Both AI and non-AI students
completed  identical  assignments  under  identical  conditions.  Uni-
formity in task type, workload, and instruction supports valid com-
parison and is grounded in constructivist learning theory (Jonassen,
1999).  AI  access  was  assigned  using  stratified  randomization
(Torgerson & Torgerson, 2003), yielding an equal number of AI and
non-AI sessions per student across the semester.

4.  Exposure Symmetry: Session logs were monitored throughout the
semester  to  ensure  balanced  AI  exposure  across  students.  When
minor  imbalances  in  the  number  of  AI-access  sessions  occurred,
excess  entries  were  randomly  removed  post  hoc  to  maintain  sym-
metrical  exposure.  This  approach  mirrors  random  under-sampling

5. Grading  Anonymity  and  Calibration:  Submissions  were  anony-
mized,  and  teaching  assistants  used  uniform  rubrics  without
knowing  condition  assignment.  Inter-rater  reliability  was  checked
mid-semester  by  double-scoring  a  subset  of  60  assignments,  with
discrepancies  reviewed  and  resolved  collaboratively.  Inter-rater
consistency  was  high  (Intraclass  Correlation  Coefficient,  ICC  =
0.82), indicating reliable scoring across graders.

6.  Training and Attendance Controls: All students participated in two
standardized AI training workshops during the first two sessions of
the  semester,  providing  scaffolding  for  prompt  construction  and
responsible use (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978; Hammond, 2001). Students
with  <80%  attendance  were  excluded  as  a  proxy  for  academic
engagement. Surveys on prior AI use and motivation were omitted
due  to  reliability  concerns  in  high-integrity  settings,  where  self-
report is often inaccurate (Tossell, Tenhundfeld, Momen, Cooley, &
de  Visser,  2024).  However,  during  these  training  sessions,  it  was
evident  that  all  students  had  prior  experience  using  ChatGPT  or
similar  large-language-model  tools,  reducing  the  likelihood  that
differences in AI familiarity acted as a confounding factor.

7.  Academic  Covariate  Control:  CGPA  was  included  as  a  post  hoc
covariate,  retrieved  from  institutional  records  after  the  study
concluded. CGPA served as a stable proxy for prior academic per-
formance  and  enabled  checks  for  baseline  group  differences,
consistent with educational measurement theory (Brookhart, 2011).

To further assess potential sources of bias, baseline equivalence was
tested between AI and non-AI groups for CGPA, attendance, and course
distribution  (all  p  > .10).  Multicollinearity  diagnostics  indicated  low
variance  inflation (Variance  Inflation Factor < 2.0) across  predictors,
indicating low multicollinearity among covariates. All model estimates
were re-run with bootstrapped standard errors, which yielded consistent
significance  patterns  (standard  deviation  <0.02),  demonstrating
robustness  to  sampling  variation  and  supporting  the  stability  of  the
observed patterns.

2.13. Data aggregation and matching process

Following the procedures implemented to minimize bias and ensure
data  integrity,  all  experimental  outputs  were  systematically  consoli-
dated  through  a  multistep  data  processing  pipeline  that  aligned  AI
prompts, responses, written assignments, and grading records on a per-
session  basis.  This  process  enabled  the  computation  of  all  writing-
quality  and  behavioral  interaction  metrics  across  936  AI  logs  and
1866  assignments.  Fig.  2 summarizes  the  overall  aggregation  and
metric-computation  workflow,  while  detailed  preprocessing,  normali-
zation,  and  dataset-merging  procedures  are  provided  in  Appendix  C.
Repository  access,  reproducibility  resources,  and  data-sharing  details
are described in the Data Availability Statement.

3. Results

3.1. Comparison of AI and non-AI student performance

As shown in Table 3, students with access to ChatGPT consistently
received  higher  scores  across  all  evaluated  metrics.  On  average,  AI-
assisted  students  showed  assignment  scores  that  were  roughly  seven
points higher than those without access.

Differences  extended  beyond  overall  grades,  with  AI-assisted  ses-
sions also showing higher Structural Complexity and Content Richness
scores.

3.2. Relationships among all AI interaction metrics and assignment
performance

This  section  evaluates  relationships  across  the  complete  set  of  AI-
interaction metrics to identify overall associations between prompting
behaviors,  AI-response  integration  patterns,  and  assignment  perfor-
mance during  AI-assisted sessions. It also  examines whether these  re-
lationships remained stable after controlling for prior academic ability
through CGPA-adjusted analyses.

3.2.1. Correlational relationships across all AI interaction metrics

Analysis of 936 AI-assisted sessions showed consistent associations
among AI-interaction metrics and assignment performance. Spearman
rank correlations showed that AI-Driven Problem-Solving Score had the
strongest  positive  association  with  assignment  scores  (ρ  = 0.77,  p  <
.001), followed by AI Query Efficiency Score and Prompt Refinement
Depth Score. In contrast, AI Query Count was negatively correlated with
assignment performance.

3.2.2. Correlation structure across AI interaction metrics

To further examine relationships among the evaluated AI-interaction
metrics, Fig. 3 presents a Spearman correlation heatmap summarizing
interconnections  between  prompting  behaviors  and  assignment
performance.

The  heatmap  showed  two  dominant  relationship  patterns.  Prompt
Refinement  Depth  Score,  AI  Response  Complexity  Score,  and  AI
Response Utility Score exhibited strong mutual correlations, whereas AI
Query  Efficiency  Score  and  AI-Driven  Problem-Solving  Score  showed
closer relationships with assignment performance. These patterns sug-
gest  distinct  clustering  structures  across  the  evaluated  AI-interaction
metrics.

A  Random  Forest  Regressor  incorporating  all  eight  AI-interaction

3.5. Thematic analysis of prompting behaviors

strongest  associations  with  academic  performance  in  the  previous

task  completion.  As  one  student  explained,  “I  usually  start  by  asking
ChatGPT to list different ways to solve the problem, then I go through
each one to see which fits best with what the question is really asking.”
Quantitatively, these students showed higher AI-Driven Problem-Solv-
ing Score values and appeared in the cluster associated with higher AI-
Driven Problem-Solving Score values in the PCA biplot (Fig. 4).

structured  prompts  during  task  completion.  Students  reported  that

would  type  long  questions  and  get  vague  answers.  Now  I  make  my

4.3. Behavioral interpretation of AI interaction metrics

The following subsections discuss the observed prompting behaviors
in  relation  to  prior  research  on  AI-assisted  learning  and  engineering
education.  The  discussion  is  organized  according  to  the  student-AI
behavioral metric dimensions developed in this study.

4.3.1. Strategic and reasoning metrics

Further analysis of prompting behaviors showed that AI Query Ef-
ficiency Score demonstrated a strong positive association with assign-
ment performance across the correlation, Random Forest, and mixed-
effects  analyses,  suggesting  that  students  who  submitted  fewer  but
more structured prompts tended to achieve stronger task outcomes. This
trend aligns with Güner and Er (2025), who found that prompt refine-
ment  was  associated  with  more  strategic  engagement,  and  with  Liao
et al. (2024), who reported that scaffolded AI use enhanced creativity
and algorithmic reasoning.

Students in the top quartile of assignment grades exhibited stronger
iterative prompting strategies as measured by the AI-Driven Problem-
Solving Score and Prompt Refinement Depth Score metrics. Prior studies
have  linked  similar  behaviors  to  greater  cognitive  and  metacognitive
engagement during AI-assisted tasks. Tiwari, Bhat, Khan, Subramaniam,
and Khan (2023) found that students' perceptions of AI's usefulness in-
fluence  productive  engagement,  while  Singh,  Guan,  and  Rieh  (2025)
showed that using metacognitive scaffolds during prompting was asso-
ciated  with  deeper  understanding.  This  suggests  that  iterative  and
reflective prompting behaviors may be associated with more deliberate
cognitive engagement when interacting with AI systems.

Among the evaluated prompting metrics, AI-Driven Problem-Solving
Score showed the strongest association with academic performance (ρ =
0.77). This relationship remained significant after controlling for CGPA,
suggesting  that  structured  reasoning  with  AI  remained  independently
associated with assignment performance outcomes. The Random Forest
analyses ranked AI-Driven Problem-Solving Score as the variable with
the highest predictive importance after controlling for CGPA, exceeding
the predictive contribution of prior academic ability within the evalu-
ated  models.  In  the  Linear  Mixed-Effects  Model,  AI-Driven  Problem-
Solving Score again held the largest fixed effect (β  = 3.48, p < .001),
followed by AI Query Efficiency Score, CGPA, Prompt Refinement Depth
Score, and AI Response Complexity Score.

Prior research has shown similar patterns, suggesting that GPA alone
may not fully capture patterns of effective AI use (Haindl & Weinberger,
2024;  Lo  et  al.,  2024;  Nikolic  et  al.,  2023;  Pabreja  &  Pabreja,  2024;
Sajawal & Kittur, 2024). Together with these studies, the present find-
ings  suggest  that  prompting  behaviors  involving  structured  reasoning
and purposeful integration of AI output may represent complementary
dimensions  of  AI-assisted  academic  performance  beyond  traditional

14

indicators of prior academic achievement.

However, although these findings suggest that structured prompting
and  AI-supported  reasoning  were  associated  with  higher  assignment
performance, alternative explanations should also be considered. Some
of  the  advantages  observed  may  reflect  greater  efficiency  in  task
execution, improved navigation of assignment requirements, or stronger
ability  to  formulate  precise  and  goal-oriented  prompts  during  AI-
assisted  activities.  In  addition,  students  with  greater  familiarity  with
AI-supported  workflows  or  stronger  procedural  problem-solving  skills
may have been better positioned to use these systems effectively during
complex engineering tasks.

4.3.2. Linguistic and iterative metrics

Prompt  Refinement  Depth  Score  (ρ  = 0.53)  and  AI  Response
Complexity  Score  (ρ  = 0.50)  were  moderately  predictive  of  achieve-
ment,  suggesting  that  students  who  refined  prompts  iteratively  and
generated more cognitively complex and meaningful responses tended
to  show  higher  assignment  performance.  AI  Response  Utility  Score,
though somewhat lower (ρ = 0.39), reflected a complementary cognitive
process. This behavior aligns with depth-of-processing theory (Craik &
Lockhart,  1972),  which  proposes  that  reworking  information  rather
than  reproducing  it  may  be  associated  with  more  elaborate  forms  of
cognitive processing during academic tasks.

As  shown  in  the  heatmap,  AI  Response  Utility  Score  correlated
strongly  with  Prompt  Refinement  Depth  Score  (ρ  = 0.74)  and  AI
Response Complexity Score (ρ  = 0.84), suggesting that content trans-
formation, prompt refinement, and indicators of cognitive engagement
frequently  co-occur  within  broader  patterns  of  metacognitive  interac-
tion with AI tools. These findings are consistent with Sun, Boudouaia,
Yang,  and  Xu  (2024),  who  found  that  prompt-based  learners  out-
performed passive users, and Bai, Gonda, and Hew (2024), who reported
positive associations between iterative evaluation and refinement of AI-
generated material and improved learning outcomes.

This  progression  toward  deliberate,  strategic  prompting  parallels
what  Kock  et  al.  (2025) describe  as  an  instrumental  genesis  process,
wherein  learners  may  gradually  engage  with  AI  as  an  integrated
cognitive  support  tool.  Güner  and  Er  (2025) similarly  observed  that
students  who  engaged  in  iterative,  analytical  interactions  achieved
higher performance, supporting the interpretation that Prompt Refine-
ment  Depth  Score  and  AI-Driven  Problem-Solving  Score  may  reflect
complementary dimensions of prompting behavior.

PCA  revealed  two  distinct  behavioral  dimensions:  PC1  (68.78%

(18.63%), defined primarily by AI-Driven Problem-Solving Score. The
strong  PC1  loadings  of  Prompt  Refinement  Depth  Score,  AI  Response

prompting,  response  transformation, and  syntactic elaboration during
AI-assisted tasks. In contrast, AI-Driven Problem-Solving Score loaded
primarily on PC2, indicating a separate reasoning-oriented dimension
associated  with  procedural  decomposition  and  strategic  AI-supported
problem-solving  behaviors.  Students  spanned  the  full  PC1  range
regardless  of  PC2  scores,  suggesting  that  linguistic  sophistication  and
strategic  reasoning  represented  partially  separable  prompting  di-
mensions within the evaluated interaction patterns. The opposing vector
orientations observed across PC1 further indicate contrasting prompting
behaviors between concise procedural interaction styles and more syn-

Chen, Bai, Tairas, and Huang (2024), who showed that deeper engage-
ment  with  AI-generated  content  was  associated  with  stronger  perfor-
mance than surface-level reuse, and with Onal (2023), who emphasized
that  critical  thinking  and  analytical  engagement  are  important  for
meaningful AI-supported learning.

4.3.3. Regulation and over-prompting metrics

Conversely, students who produced a high volume of prompts tended
to show lower performance, as indicated by the strong negative corre-
lation with AI Query Count. Similar concerns were raised by Bernabei,
Colabianchi,  Falegnami,  and  Costantino  (2023) and  Baturalp  and
Bozkurt (2025), who observed that overreliance on AI can undermine
learning. Isiaku, Muhammad, Kefas, and Ukaegbu (2024) also cautioned
that uncritical AI use may weaken critical thinking, a pattern partially
reflected in the negative association observed between AI Query Count
and assignment performance in this study. Relatedly, the AI Response
Reliance Score showed moderate associations with both AI Query Count
and AI Query Depth and Structure Score, suggesting that students who
interacted more extensively with AI systems and produced more struc-
tured  prompts  also  tended  to  incorporate  greater  amounts  of  AI-
generated  material  into  their  assignments.  Unlike  perspectives  that
frame AI reliance primarily as passive dependence, these patterns sug-
gest that integration of AI-generated content may also occur alongside
cognitively  sophisticated  prompting  behaviors.  This  suggests  that  AI-
supported academic work may involve a more complex interaction be-
tween  strategic  engagement  and  dependence  on  generated  outputs,
rather than a simple distinction between effective and ineffective AI use.
While these findings highlight the potential educational relevance of
deliberate  prompting  behaviors,  they  also  underscore  potential  risks
associated with unequal prompting proficiency and overreliance on AI-
generated  reasoning.  Not  all  students  may  possess  the  linguistic  or
cognitive  skills  required  to  formulate  effective  prompts,  which  could
widen performance gaps between students who adapt quickly and those
who struggle to use AI systems effectively during academic tasks.

Excessive dependence on AI feedback may be associated with fewer
opportunities for independent reasoning, problem decomposition, and
creative exploration, which are core competencies in engineering edu-
cation. These patterns suggest that strategic prompting may be associ-
ated  with improved  learning experiences, but its  benefits  may not  be
universally  accessible  without  guided  instruction.  Addressing  these
disparities  may  require  pedagogical  frameworks  that  explicitly  teach
students how to engage in AI critically, responsibly, and equitably.

At  the  same  time,  high  prompting  frequency  may  not  necessarily
reflect ineffective learning-related behavior in all cases. Some students
may have used repeated prompting as a compensatory strategy while
learning  unfamiliar  material,  clarifying  technical  concepts,  or  exper-
imenting  with  different  prompting  approaches  during  complex  tasks.
Consequently, elevated AI Query Count may partly reflect differences in
confidence,  prior  knowledge,  or  exploratory  learning  behaviors  in
addition to potential overreliance on AI systems.

6. Limitations, future work, and conclusion

It  is  necessary  to  acknowledge  the  methodological  and  contextual
limitations  that  shape  interpretation  of  this  study's  findings.  Due  to
ethical and institutional restrictions associated with student privacy and
academic records, the raw textual datasets used in this study, including
student  prompts,  AI  responses,  and  written  submissions,  cannot  be
publicly released. Although this limits exact reproduction of the original
textual  dataset  and  generated  linguistic  outputs,  the  study  provides
extensive  methodological  documentation  to  support  approximate
replication.  The  complete  computational  pipeline,  metric  definitions,
preprocessing  procedures,  statistical  workflows,  validation  protocols,
and  analysis  scripts  are  openly  available  through  the  referenced  re-
positories and appendices. Future researchers may therefore reproduce
the overall study design and analytical framework using institutionally
approved  educational  datasets  collected  under  similar  experimental
conditions.

Although all students had prior experience using ChatGPT, individ-
ual differences in familiarity, prompting proficiency, and prior exposure

stratified  randomization,  and  CGPA-adjusted  analyses  to  help  reduce

A further limitation concerns the Python-based AI interface used for

not formally validated for user experience within this cohort. However,

Although all course tasks followed standardized rubrics and rotation
schedules,  minor  variations  in  task  complexity  may  have  introduced
uncontrolled  difficulty  effects.  Grading  bias  was  mitigated  through
anonymized  submissions  and  inter-rater  reliability  checks,  yet  some
degree of subjectivity inherent to human assessment cannot be elimi-
nated.  These  potential  confounds,  AI  familiarity,  task  variability,  and
evaluator  subjectivity,  should  be  considered  when  interpreting  the
findings.  In  addition,  motivational  differences,  self-regulation,  task
engagement, and individual adaptation to prompting strategies may also
have contributed to variation in AI-supported learning behaviors despite
the  randomized  AI-access  design  and  standardized  instructional
conditions.

Some  observed  performance  improvements  may  partly  reflect  AI-
assisted support for writing quality, task completion, or content orga-
nization  rather  than  learning  alone.  Consequently,  assignment  scores
and writing-quality metrics should not be interpreted as direct measures
of deeper conceptual understanding, long-term knowledge retention, or
durable learning outcomes. Although mixed-effects modeling, balanced
AI/non-AI exposure, and CGPA-adjusted analyses were implemented to
reduce potential bias, the findings should still be interpreted within the
broader  complexity  of  educational  environments,  where  behavioral,
contextual,  and  instructional  factors  may  interact  in  ways  not  fully
captured by the evaluated variables.

Moreover, as this investigation was conducted within a single insti-
tution and disciplinary context, the findings should be interpreted with
caution regarding their broader applicability. Replication across diverse
universities, academic disciplines, and cultural settings is strongly rec-
ommended  to  determine  whether  similar  prompting  behaviors  and
performance associations emerge in other educational contexts.

Despite these constraints, the findings suggest that variation in stu-
dents' prompting behaviors was associated with differences in academic
performance across AI-assisted tasks. This study's focus on fourth-year
engineering  students  ensured  internal  consistency  but  also  limits
external  generalizability.  Including  broader  student  populations  and
additional  task  types,  such  as  creative  writing,  group  work,  or  oral
presentations, could reveal additional prompting patterns. Additionally,
because  the  study  utilized  ChatGPT-4o,  future  work  should  consider
potential  model  drift  and  newer  model  versions  that  may  influence
prompting effectiveness and metric reliability as generative AI systems
continue to evolve.

While  post-semester  qualitative  analysis  provided  insight  into  stu-
dents'  prompting  strategies  and  intentionality,  further  research  is
needed to explore their attitudes, motivations, and perceived learning
outcomes.  Richer  qualitative  approaches,  such  as  interviews,  think-
aloud protocols, or reflective journals, could deepen understanding of
how students cognitively and emotionally engage with AI across tasks.
A  promising avenue  for future  research lies  in testing  targeted in-
terventions  to  strengthen  students'  prompting  skills.  Studies  could

A.2. Structural Complexity Score (SC)

The Structural Complexity Score (SC) captures syntactic depth and fluency in student writing. It was computed for each written assignment using

two linguistic subcomponents:

• Sentence Length (SL): Calculated as the average number of words per sentence using nltk.sent_tokenize() and nltk.word_tokenize(). SL was linearly
scaled to a 0–100 range using fixed thresholds: 5 words per sentence mapped to 0, and 50 words per sentence mapped to 100, based on the
observed dataset distribution.

• Grammatical Complexity (GC): Derived using syntactic dependency parsing with spaCy. Following Strübbe, Grünwald, Sidorenko, and Lampe

(2025), the GC score was calculated as the average number of advanced grammatical structures per sentence, including:
• Subordinate clauses (mark)
• Relative clauses (relcl)
• Adjectival clauses (acl)
• Coordination structures (cc)

GC was normalized by the total number of sentences in the assignment and scaled to a 0–100 range using an empirical upper bound of 4 target

structures per sentence.

The final SC score for each assignment was calculated as a weighted combination of both components:

SC = 0.40⋅SL + 0.60⋅GC

This approach balances fluency and syntactic variety, normalizing structural complexity across diverse writing styles while preventing inflation

from unusually long or highly fragmented sentences.

A.3. Content richness and information density score (CR)

The Content Richness and Information Density Score (CR) was computed for each written assignment to assess lexical variety and semantic density.

It consists of two balanced subcomponents:

• Type-Token Ratio (TTR): Calculated using nltk.word_tokenize() following Kettunen (2014). To mitigate TTR's sensitivity to text length, a fixed-size
sliding window of 50 words was applied. If a text was shorter than 50 words, the entire excerpt was used. TTR was computed as the ratio of unique
word types to total tokens.

• Lexical Density (LD): Computed via part-of-speech tagging using spaCy, following the method of Spring and Johnson (2022). Content-bearing
words—nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs—were divided by the total number of alphabetic tokens to produce a normalized LD value be-
tween 0 and 1.

The CR score was computed as the average of TTR and LD, scaled to a 0–100 range by multiplication. If the result exceeded 100, it was capped. To
prevent artificially inflated TTR values in short responses, CR was only computed for texts longer than 100 words (Kettunen, 2014). Unlike metrics
with unbounded syntactic complexity (e.g., SC), CR relies on inherently bounded ratios, so no additional normalization was needed.
CR = 0.50⋅TTR + 0.50⋅LD

A.4. AI Query Count (QC)

The AI Query Count (QC) metric was computed at the session level to capture the number of distinct prompts submitted by each student during an

AI-access session.

Prompt  logs were  parsed  using the  pandas  library  in  Python.  Each  prompt  was  extracted  from the  session-level  interaction  log  (one file  per
assignment). To avoid overcounting slight rephrasings or repeated queries, a Levenshtein similarity filter was applied: any pair of prompts with a
similarity score of ≥85% was grouped and counted as a single unique query. This prompt grouping technique follows the method described by
Gaikwad and Bogiri (2015).

QC was only computed for students assigned to the AI condition. No normalization by time or task complexity was applied, as QC is treated as a raw
engagement indicator reflecting the quantity of student–AI interaction. Later sections examine QC alongside Prompt Refinement Depth (PRD) to
explore both the volume and quality of prompting behavior.

A.5. AI response reliance score (ARR)

Each AI response block was compared to the corresponding full written assignment from the same session. All ARR subcomponents were computed
at the block level (i.e., full AI output vs. full student submission), rather than at the sentence alignment level, to reflect holistic reliance patterns rather
than granular reuse.

• Text  Similarity  (TS,  40%):  Computed  as  the  mean  of  Cosine  Similarity  (using  sentence-transformers  with  model  all-MiniLM-L6-v2),  Jaccard

Similarity, and normalized Levenshtein distance. This follows hybrid similarity detection methods from Korade et al. (2024).

• Direct Copy-Pasting (CP, 10%): Flagged near-verbatim reuse using sentence-wise Levenshtein ratios ≥90%, prioritizing full-sentence structural

matches over token overlap (cf. Anbananthen, Kannan, Busst, Muthaiyah, & Lurudusamy, 2022).

• Structural Similarity (SS, 20%): Captured syntactic overlap via dependency parsing with spaCy, comparing distributions of grammatical relations

(e.g., nsubj, aux, prep). Adapted from Yamamoto, Matsumoto, and Watanabe (2022).

• Prompt–Response Consistency (PRC, 20%): Measured conceptual alignment between student prompt and AI response using cosine similarity on

sentence embeddings (cf. Abdalgader & Matroud, 2024).

• Query-to-Submission Link Score (QLS, 10%): Used spaCy's Named Entity Recognition to compute overlap between entities in student prompts and

their final submissions, adapted from Hu et al. (2023).

All five components were normalized to a 0–1 scale before applying the weighted average. The formula below was used to compute the final score:

ARR = 0.40⋅TS + 0.10⋅CP + 0.20⋅SS + 0.20⋅PRC + 0.10⋅QLS

Because higher similarity between AI responses and student writing indicates greater reliance on AI, the raw ARR score was inverted so that higher
values  reflect  greater independence and  cognitive transformation. After  computing the  weighted average of  the five  components, the  score was
subtracted from 1 and multiplied by 100. This ensured that a student who reused large portions of AI output received a lower ARR score, while a
student who meaningfully transformed or diverged from the AI responses received a higher score. The final ARR score thus reflects reduced reliance on
AI content, consistent with the intended construct definition.

A.6. AI query depth & structure score (QD)

For each session, all student prompts were compiled into a single chronological text block. The AI Query Depth &  Structure Score (QD) was
computed over this block, treating all prompts in the session as a unified document. This approach enabled holistic analysis of structural, procedural,
and clarity-related features rather than isolated prompt-level scoring.

• Lexical and Syntactic Structure (LS, 35%): Calculated using the Flesch-Kincaid readability score, Gunning Fog index, and lexical diversity metrics.
These were computed using NLTK and spaCy, based on the methods described in Lee and Lee (2024). Prompts exhibiting greater syntactic variety,
richer vocabulary, and deeper grammatical constructions received higher scores.

• Multi-Step Problem-Solving Depth (MSD, 35%): Assessed using spaCy's dependency parser, following Altinok (2021). This subcomponent iden-
tified procedural and logical operators (e.g., “calculate,” “if,” “therefore”) as well as subordinate clause structures to capture evidence of multi-
stage reasoning and task decomposition.

• Query Focus and Clarity (FC, 30%): Measured using two complementary analyses: (1) lexical repetition, quantified via n-gram overlap using NLTK,
and (2) syntactic compactness, evaluated through part-of-speech patterning and dependency tree depth via spaCy, following the approach of Shaib
et al. (2024). Prompts that were concise, well-scaffolded, and non-redundant received higher scores.

All three subcomponents were normalized to a 0–100 scale using a combination of z-score and max-range scaling across the full prompt dataset.

A.8. AI response utility score (RU)

This metric evaluates the extent to which students meaningfully transformed or built upon AI-generated content. It operates on aligned pairs of text

segments between the AI response and the final student-written assignment.

• Segment Matching Strategy: Both the AI response and student assignment were split into paragraph-level segments using double line breaks and
sentence boundary heuristics. Sentence embeddings were generated using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 from sentence-transformers, and cosine similarity was
used to compute the semantic alignment between each AI segment and student paragraph. For each AI segment, the single most similar student
paragraph was retained, provided the similarity was ≥0.70. Segments under 100 words were excluded to ensure substantive comparisons.

• Semantic Novelty (SN, 45%): Calculated as (1 (cid:0) cosine similarity) × 100(1 - \text{cosine similarity}) \times 100(1 (cid:0) cosine similarity) × 100,
rewarding divergence from AI content. Higher values indicate greater novelty in the student's expression and conceptual framing. This adaptation
of Ormerod, Martínez del Rinc´on, and Devereux (2021) minimizes false inflation from short or repetitive texts and ensures originality is rewarded.

• Conceptual Transformation (CT, 40%): Measured the degree of lexical and structural transformation using:

• Jaccard Similarity (to assess word-level overlap), and
• Levenshtein Distance (to capture structural edits and rephrasings).

These measures were averaged per aligned segment pair, adapting the transformation framework proposed by Ormerod et al. (2021).

• Contribution to Final Assignment (CF, 15%): Quantified the reuse of core terminology and ideas from AI segments in the student assignment using
cosine similarity and Named Entity Recognition (NER) via spaCy. This follows the reuse-tracking approach outlined in Woolever and Wettstein
(2024).

Each score (SN, CT, CF) was computed at the segment pair level, then averaged across all valid segment matches within the session to yield a

session-level RU score.

The final RU score was computed as a weighted average:

RU = 0.45⋅SN + 0.40⋅CT + 0.15⋅CF

A.9. AI-driven problem-solving score (PS)

This metric evaluates how students adapted AI-generated solutions when completing structured tasks such as case analysis, quantitative reasoning,

or design activities. It emphasizes alignment in logic, application of concepts, and evidence of independent problem-solving expansion.

• Stepwise  Alignment  (SA,  40%):  Both  AI  responses  and  student  assignments  were  segmented  into  sentence-level  blocks.  Using  sentence-
transformers, cosine similarity was computed between all sentence pairs to identify logical alignment and structural overlap. For each sentence
in the AI output, the top-matching sentence from the student submission was retained if similarity exceeded 0.70. All valid sentence-level similarity
scores were averaged and scaled to a 0–100 range. This procedure follows logic-model alignment approaches in Ormerod et al. (2021).

• Conceptual Application (CA, 40%): Key domain-specific terms and named entities (e.g., technical vocabulary, process names, variables) were
extracted from each AI response using spaCy's Named Entity Recognition (NER). These terms were then searched for in the student's assignment
using both exact string matching and cosine similarity at the sentence level. This component reflects domain-sensitive concept transfer, based on
the entity-matching strategies of Dychka, Potapova, Vovk, Meliukh, and Vedenieieva (2024). Results were normalized and scaled to 0–100.

• Independent Expansion (IE, 20%): This subcomponent captured the extent to which students expanded beyond AI content.

• Semantic Expansion: Cosine similarity was computed between the AI response block and the student submission. Scores were inverted (1 (cid:0)

similarity) to reflect divergence.

• Syntactic Complexity: spaCy's dependency parser was used to count features associated with complex reasoning, such as subordinate clauses,
embedded phrases, and flexible word order (e.g., pre-verbal elaboration), following McNamara, Crossley, and McCarthy (2010) and Okhapkin,
Okhapkina, Iskhakova, and Iskhakov (2021).

The final IE score was the average of the semantic and syntactic measures, scaled to 0–100. All three subcomponent scores were combined using the

following weighted formula:
PS = 0.40⋅SA + 0.40⋅CA + 0.20⋅IE

Appendix C. Data aggregation pipeline

To compute the complete set of AI interaction and writing metrics used in this study, a multistep data processing pipeline was developed to align

each student's prompts, AI responses, written assignments, and grades on a per-session basis.

During the semester, each AI access session was logged as a raw TXT file labeled with the student's anonymized ID number, session number, and
course name. Each file contained a timestamped sequence of prompt–response pairs. These logs were parsed to separate individual prompts from their
corresponding responses, then cleaned to remove duplicate or near-duplicate prompts—defined as those with ≥95% textual similarity—using Lev-
enshtein distance thresholds implemented in Python.

The remaining prompts and responses were concatenated into two separate chronological text blocks (one for prompts and one for responses), with
a delimiter inserted between each entry to preserve separation for later parsing. The compiled AI output was then matched to the corresponding
written  assignment  (in  DOCX  format)  using  the  student's  anonymized  ID  and  converted  to  plain  text.  Assignment  scores  were  retrieved  from  a
structured grading spreadsheet and merged by student ID.

All files were combined into a single CSV dataset, producing one unified record per student per session. Each row included the written assignment

text, cleaned prompt block, compiled AI response block, assignment score, and contextual metadata (e.g., course, week, AI access status).

From this merged dataset:

• Prompt-based metrics were calculated using only the prompt logs.
• Response-to-writing metrics were computed by comparing the full AI output block to the student submission.
• Prompt–response relationship metrics were derived from their respective paired text blocks.
• Writing quality metrics were extracted directly from the final assignments.

This unified structure enabled systematic computation of all AI interaction and writing metrics across 936 AI access logs and 1866 written as-
signments. After metric calculation, data from both AI-access and non-AI sessions were compiled into a single dataset. Each row represented a unique
combination of student ID, course, and week. Columns included anonymized ID, course type, week, AI access status, activity type, attendance, final
assignment score, and all computed metrics. For AI-access sessions, metric fields contained calculated values; for non-AI sessions, AI-related fields
were set to zero. The final dataset was exported as an .xlsx file and deposited in the public data repository available via OSF (Isaza Domínguez, 2025a).
