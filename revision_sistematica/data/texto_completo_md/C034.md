Effects of ChatGPT and argument map(AM)‑supported
online argumentation on college students’ critical thinking
skills and perceptions

Abstract
Although online argumentation provides students with sufficient time to think, they
often lack a clear argument structure and timely guidance during the process. This
may negatively affect students’ abilities to support or rebut arguments and to develop
their critical thinking skills. In this study, 64 sophomores were evenly assigned to
either a control or an experimental group. Each group was further divided into four
teams, with each team comprising both a pro and a con side; the former needed to
argue for a given position and the latter against that position. Using the collabora-
tive online platform “ShiMo,” the control group engaged in textual argumentation,
whereas  the  experimental  group  conducted  their  argumentation  by  drawing  argu-
ment  maps  (AMs).  Furthermore,  students  could  also  seek  assistance  from  Chat-
GPT  3.5  when  encountering  difficulties  in  creating  AMs.  Both  questionnaires  and
content analysis were used to evaluate students’ critical thinking skills and percep-
tions. The findings indicated that ChatGPT and AM-supported online argumentation
effectively promoted students’ critical thinking skills, encouraging them to engage
in more rebuttal activities through analysis, evaluation, and creation. Students per-
ceived  that  AMs  clearly  visualized  argument  structure,  albeit  providing  limited
assistance in facilitating information gathering. ChatGPT provided detailed informa-
tion and fostered a positive attitude toward argumentation among students; however,
the  accuracy  and  appropriateness  of  the  provided  information  remained  doubtful.
These  findings  have  several  implications.  College  students’  critical  thinking  skills
can be improved by integrating teacher guidance into ChatGPT and AM-supported
online  argumentation,  being  skeptical  of  ChatGPT’s  outputs,  and  improving  infor-
mation literacy to efficiently utilize ChatGPT.

1  Introduction

Critical  thinking  is  a  sophisticated  cognitive  ability  that  encourages  individuals
to engage in profound reflection and formulate independent opinions, ultimately
resulting  in  well-informed  and  rational  decisions  (Jiménez-Aleixandre  &  Puig,
2012;  Zulkifli  et  al.,  2020).  Skilled  critical  thinkers  possess  specific  competen-
cies,  such  as  accurately  assessing  arguments  and  identifying  flawed  reasoning
(Ennis  &  Norris,  1989).  Weiler  (2004)  emphasized  the  significance  of  critical
thinking  skills  in  facilitating  students’  learning  processes;  such  skills  not  only
improve  their  capacity  to  retrieve  information  but  also  foster  cognitive  develop-
ment. Enhancing critical thinking skills among college students has emerged as a
priority in higher education institutions (Zhang et al., 2023a, 2023b). Employers
value critical thinking skills as a factor that contributes to both graduates’ profes-
sional success and active citizenship (Penkauskiene et al., 2019). Therefore, pro-
moting  college  students’  critical  thinking  skills  has  become  increasingly  urgent
for enhancing their employability in fast-changing workplaces.

Critical thinking skills can be enhanced through academic courses that incor-
porate  strategies  tailored  to  cultivate  these  skills.  Along  with  methods  such  as
problem-based (Narmaditya et al., 2018) and flipped (Smith et al., 2018) learning,
argumentation has proven to be an effective means for students to exercise their
critical  thinking  skills  (Rodriguez-Dono  &  Hernandez-Fernandez, 2021).  Tradi-
tional face-to-face argumentation often encounters problems, such as limited time
and  unclear  structures.  To  address  these  challenges,  integrating  an  online  argu-
ment map (AM) into argumentative practices can provide students with sufficient
time to think and structure their arguments clearly and coherently. Research has
demonstrated that this approach effectively fosters college students’ critical think-
ing skills (Chen et al., 2022a).

Although students’ argumentation and critical thinking skills are facilitated by
utilizing online AM as a scaffolding tool, they still face challenges in providing
adequate evidence to support their claims, a crucial aspect of articulating and jus-
tifying  assertions  (Omarchevska  et  al.,  2021).  The  primary  reasons  that  impede
students  from  formulating  and  rebutting  reasonable  viewpoints  stem  from  their
limited prior knowledge or experience, coupled with inadequate resources (Kuhn,
1991; Sonmez, 2021). Timely feedback can effectively guide students’ argumen-
tation  by  offering  valuable  opportunities  for  self-evaluation  and  reflection  (Lee
et al., 2021). According to Kaeppel (2021), teacher feedback is highly influential
in  encouraging  students’  excellence  in  critical  thinking-intensive  argumentative
tasks. However, teachers often encounter difficulties in providing comprehensive
feedback due to the time-consuming nature of the process (Guo et al., 2022; Xu
&  Zhang,  2022).  Chat  Generative  Pre-trained  Transformer  3.5  (hereafter  Chat-
GPT), a language model developed by OpenAI and belonging to the Generative
Pre-trained Transformer series, is capable of generating human-like text based on
inputs and has the potential to facilitate argumentation.

2   Literature review

2.1   Critical thinking skills and argumentation

Critical thinking denotes “a person’s thinking to decide what to believe” (Ennis
&  Norris,  1989).  It  can  be  traced  back  to  John  Dewey’s  concept  of  “reflective
thinking,”  which  involves  two  key  stages:  an  initial  state  characterized  by  per-
plexity,  hesitation,  and  doubt,  followed  by  the  phase  of  searching  for  evidence,
facts, and information (Dewey, 1910; Hitchcock, 2022). Consequently, reflection
is a crucial aspect for individuals to think critically. Piaget (2001) introduced the
term  “reflecting  abstraction”  to  elucidate  critical  thinking  in  the  context  of  psy-
chological  mechanisms  underlying  knowledge  construction.  Reflecting  abstrac-
tion  represents  a  cognitive  development  process,  in  which  individuals  reflect
upon their physical experiences, sometimes challenging, reinterpreting, or negat-
ing them, ultimately fostering critical thinking. From this perspective, argumen-
tation, defined as “a process of making claims and providing justification for the
claims using evidence” (Cho & Jonassen, 2002), is supposed to have advantages
in  enhancing  critical  thinking  by  encouraging  students  to  reflect  on  and  criti-
cally  evaluate  the  quality  of  established  arguments  (Ellerton, 2022;  Hasnunidah
et al., 2020; Sonmez et al., 2020). In the field of argumentation theory, Toulmin’s
(1958) model, which consists of six element: claims, grounds, warrants, backing,
modifiers, and rebuttals, has been widely used for analyzing arguments.

Ennis (1987) and Glaser (1941) clarified critical thinking into two dimensions:
critical thinking skills and dispositions. Critical thinking skills primarily include
cognitive skills, such as analysis, evaluation, and inference. Critical thinking dis-
positions  refer  to  individuals’  tendency  to  apply  these  cognitive  skills,  usually
incorporating  ethical,  moral,  and  value  considerations.  This  study  focuses  on
critical thinking skills, with proficient individuals demonstrating abilities in ques-
tioning,  problem  solving,  evidence  evaluation,  assumption  analysis,  information
synthesis, inference making, argument construction, and decision making (Egan,
2023; Ennis, 1987). Students’ critical thinking skills can be affected by many per-
sonal  and  contextual  factors,  including  their  grade  point  average  (GPA;  Lajthia
et al., 2024) and classroom environment (Almulla, 2023). As noted by Ab Kadir
(2017) and Mihail (2023), a lack of analyzing, processing, and critically reflect-
ing on materials, coupled with teachers’ limited strategies for cultivating critical
thinking  skills,  may  lead  to  students’  poor  critical  thinking  skill-levels.  Forawi
(2020)  proposed  that  critical  thinking  skills  could  be  “taught”  similar  to  other
skills, either integrated into subject curricula or as standalone modules.

Argumentation  has  been  adopted  as  an  effective  instructional  strategy  to
improve  college  students’  critical  thinking  skills  (Demircioglu  et  al.,  2023).
Recent  studies  have  found  evidence  to  confirm  the  utility  of  this  approach.  For
example,  Giri  and  Paily  (2020)  concluded  that  students’  critical  thinking  skills
could be improved when they argued face-to-face in class. The students were ran-
domly assigned to either a pro or con side, with each having the chance to express
their  own  evidence-based  viewpoints  or  rebut  the  opposite  side’s  arguments.

However,  there  are  some  problems  in  traditional  classes  that  incorporate  argu-

class  interactions  (Chen  et  al.,  2022b);  they  also  may  experience  difficulties  in
understanding  the  argument  structure.  To  address  this  challenge,  computer-sup-
ported asynchronous and visual tools could be integrated as a scaffold to facilitate
high-quality argumentation.

2.2   Use of computer‑aided AM in argumentation

AMs are box-and-line diagrams that visually depict reasoning and evidence for and
against a statement or claim (Dwyer, 2011). The theoretical foundation of AM stems
from  the  dual-coding  theory  within  the  field  of  cognitive  science  (Davies,  2021).
This theory posits that the utilization of multiple sensory modalities enhances human
information processing. Incorporating diagrams alongside words enables better cog-
nitive processing of complex information than words alone (Clark & Paivio, 1991).
In AMs, the propositions are organized in colored boxes, interconnected by arrows
that signify their relationships. This visual arrangement of concepts around a cen-
tral issue enhances students’ comprehension of complex argumentation (Chen et al.,
2023), serving as aids for “systematic reflection” and “thinking structuring” (Gelder,
2002). Recently, computer-aided AM has emerged as a scaffolding tool designed to
facilitate argumentation and improve students’ critical thinking skills (Darmawansah
et al., 2022; Yilmaz-Na & Sonmez, 2023a). In addition to visualizing the argument
structure, the online environment also offers students sufficient time to fully engage
in argumentation and practice their critical thinking skills.

When constructing AMs online using software, group members either collaborate
to support a particular viewpoint or engage in conflicting debates, with proponents
defending  their  position  and  opponents  arguing  against  it.  Both  forms  of  engage-
ment  elicit  students’  positive  perceptions  of  AM-supported  online  argumentation
(Chen  et  al.,  2022a).  Ngajie  et  al.  (2020)  divided  70  college  students  into  experi-
mental (N = 39) and control (N = 31) groups. Each group included several teams, all
of which participated in seven argumentations throughout a semester. In the exper-
imental  group,  the  team  members  collaboratively  argued  on  a  specific  topic  from
diverse perspectives by creating AMs on the Rational platform, whereas the teams
in  the  control  group  orally  argued  on  the  same  topic  by  answering  the  teacher’s
questions.  Questionnaire  results  indicated  that  creating  AMs  online  promoted  stu-
dents’ critical thinking skills. The study also interviewed six students to understand
their  perceptions  of  the  computer-aided  AMs.  The  interviewees  reported  that  this
tool helped them to clearly express their thoughts. Similarly, Chen et al. (2024) con-
ducted a study in which 17 college students engaged in five AM-supported online
group  debate  activities.  For  each  debate,  participants  were  able  to  select  many
debate topics derived from an academic article and freely choose their side, either
in  support  of  or  opposition  to  the  topic.  Both  sides  argued  by  drawing  AMs  on  a
visual  platform  called  “ZJUYuQue.”  The  results  of  content  analysis  of  the  debate
indicated that students’ critical thinking skills were enhanced. Additionally, all eight
interviewees positively perceived the integration of online AM into argumentation,

citing the provision of sufficient time and a clear structure as the key factors contrib-

Constructing  AMs  is  a  challenging  task,  given  the  need  to  coherently  visual-
ize  inferential  relationships  among  diverse  arguments  (Uçar  &  Demiraslan  Çevik,
2021).  Archila  et  al.  (2020)  proposed  that  students’  repertoires  of  logical  connec-
tors  are  limited.  Such  issues  can  be  mitigated  by  using  various  instructional  scaf-
folds.  Timely  feedback  is  a  regularly  used  scaffold  that  provides  opportunities  for
individuals  to  understand  their  current  performance  and  improve  the  quality  of
argumentation  (Lee  et  al.,  2019),  which  also  contributes  to  the  cultivation  of  stu-
dents’ critical thinking skills. In the context of AM-supported online argumentative
activities,  students  can  receive  feedback  from  teachers  or  peers  once  the  AMs  are
completed (Archila et al., 2022a, 2022b). However, in classes comprising many stu-
dents,  teachers  often  struggle  to  provide  adequate  procedural  feedback,  and  there
remains a notable absence of feedback during the AM construction process (Chen
et al., 2022a; Tsai et al., 2015). From this perspective, ChatGPT, capable of provid-
ing instant feedback for complex tasks (Cooper, 2023), could be utilized to facilitate
students’ reevaluation of arguments and improve their critical thinking skills.

2.3   Use of ChatGPT in argumentation

2.3.1   ChatGPT‑supported argumentation and critical thinking skills

Rebuttals  play  a  significant  role  in  facilitating  argumentative  discourse  (Osborne
et  al.,  2004).  To  construct  persuasive  rebuttals,  students  must  critically  analyze
complex problems and collaboratively generate solutions by comparing, evaluating,
and  synthesizing  different  opinions  based  on  evidence  and  rationale.  This  process
has been found to promote high-level and complex thinking skills, including criti-
cal  thinking  (Han  et  al.,  2021;  Puente  et  al.,  2024).  However,  students  often  hold
negative  attitudes  toward  rebuttals  in  argumentation;  they  find  it  unpleasant  to  be
challenged,  and  may  experience  anxiety  when  confronted  with  conflicting  view-
points  (Foy  et  al.,  2023).  As  a  result,  it  is  quite  common  for  students  to  prioritize
acceptance of their position over providing convincing counterargument (Widodo &
Riandi, 2023).

ChatGPT,  a  user-friendly  AI  tool  designed  to  generate  diverse  responses  tai-
lored  to  user-input  prompts  (Rospigliosi,  2023),  has  been  effectively  integrated
into students’ argumentation practice, providing alternative claims and potential
counterarguments  (Kasneci  et  al.,  2023).  When  scaffolded  by  ChatGPT,  learn-
ers  are  more  inclined  to  reflect  on  their  learning  experiences,  critically  analyze
diverse viewpoints, and articulate their ideas (Deng & Yu, 2023; Kim & Adlof,
2024),  thereby  facilitating  the  formulation  of  effective  rebuttals.  Specifically,
ChatGPT  usually  functions  as  a  learning  partner  by  rebutting  students’  argu-
ments.  For  example,  Urhan  et  al.  (2024)  encouraged  college  students  to  debate
with ChatGPT regarding the concepts of calculus. Despite occasional issues with
ChatGPT’s responses from a professional standpoint, it generally demonstrated a
capacity  to  reasonably  rebut  students’  viewpoints.  Additionally,  ChatGPT  could

also  serve  as  a  guide  collaborating  with  arguers  to  foster  rational  discourse  and

writing  classes,  it  collaborated  with  students  as  a  virtual  peer  engaged  in  con-

paragraphs, and a conclusion. Each section was explained in detail with various
examples to support or rebut viewpoints, which facilitated students’ understand-
ing of the discussed topics.

Based on the literature review, it is reasonable to hypothesize that ChatGPT and
AM-supported  online  argumentation  can  promote  students’  critical  thinking  skills
by encouraging them to produce more persuasive rebuttals. However, there is dearth
of empirical research investigating the impact of ChatGPT and AM-supported online
argumentation on critical thinking skills, especially in the context of higher educa-
tion.  To  fill  this  gap,  this  study  integrates  ChatGPT  and  computer-supported  AM
as tools to facilitate college students’ online argumentation, aiding them in making
effective rebuttals and ultimately enhance their critical thinking skills.

2.3.2   Students’ perceptions of ChatGPT in argumentation

From a constructivist perspective, which emphasizes that learners construct knowl-
edge  through  collaborative  activities  and  reflection  on  what  they  have  learned
through  conversation  with  others  (Jonassen  et  al.,  1995),  ChatGPT  can  facilitate
students’  construction  of  new  knowledge  by  functioning  as  a  virtual  collaborator
in  group  discussions  (Rasul  et  al.,  2023).  This  may  result  in  positive  perceptions
among students when engaged in argumentative activities (Wang et al., 2023).

Although  ChatGPT  has  improved  students’  argumentation  quality  by  offering
prompts, proposing related concepts, or suggesting alternative approaches, the dis-
crepancy between the potential capabilities and limited current implementation, as
evidenced by inconsistencies and inaccuracies in responses to educational inquiries
from  specific  AI  tools,  has  led  students  to  be  apathetic  toward  its  practical  appli-
cation (Kairu, 2020). Guo and Wang (2024) compared the performances of teach-
ers  and  ChatGPT  in  providing  feedback  on  EFL  students’  argumentative  writing.
The instructor collected 50 English argumentative essays, with lengths ranging from
314 to 455 words (average = 375 words). Although ChatGPT generated significantly
more  feedback  than  the  teachers  did,  covering  both  content  and  language-related
issues,  EFL  teachers  reported  negative  perceptions  of  using  ChatGPT-based  feed-
back, citing concerns that the lengthy feedback may increase students’ anxiety and
reduce  their  writing  motivation.  According  to  Hwang  et  al.  (2021),  students’  con-
ceptions have been shown to influence their learning motivation, attitudes, and effec-
tiveness. Therefore, when integrating ChatGPT into AM-supported online argumen-
tation, it is crucial to explore students’ perceptions of this type of learning activity.

3   Purpose of the study and research questions

This study explored the effects of ChatGPT and AM-supported online argumen-
tation on college students’ critical thinking skills, as well as their impact on stu-
dents’  perceptions.  All  participants  were  organized  into  teams,  each  consisting
of a side that was required to argue for a position (pro) and one that was required
to  argue  against  that  position  (con).  By  utilizing  an  online  collaborative  plat-
form,  the  teams  in  the  experimental  group  were  able  to  support  or  rebut  view-
points by drawing AMs with the help of ChatGPT. The pros and cons teams of
the control group were encouraged to argue textually online. The research ques-
tions guiding this study are as follows:

Q1: How does ChatGPT and AM-supported online argumentation affect stu-
dents’ critical thinking skills?
Q2: How do students perceive ChatGPT and AM-supported online argumen-
tation?

4   Methodology

4.1   Participants

The  participants  were  64  sophomores  (mean  age:  19  years)  enrolled  at  Henan
University  in  China.  None  of  the  students  had  prior  experience  in  debating  or
formal  training  in  utilizing  computer-supported  AM  or  ChatGPT  within  the
context  of  argumentative  activities.  They  all  took  the  compulsory  course  titled
“Application  of  Modern  Educational  Technology.”  The  participants  were  ran-
domly  assigned  to  either  the  control  (N = 32)  or  experimental  (N = 32)  group.
To exclude the possible influence of age and gender, Independent samples t-test
was  performed  and  it  was  confirmed  that  the  distribution  of  age  (t(62) = 0.00,
p = 1.00) and gender (t(62) = −0.99,  p = 0.32) did not differ between the experi-
mental  and  control  groups.  Additionally,  California  Critical  Thinking  Skills
questionnaire was adopted to evaluate students’ critical thinking skills (Facione
& Facione, 2002), and the result also indicated no significant difference of criti-
cal thinking skills between the two groups (t(60) = −0.32, p = 0.75).

Each  group  was  divided  into  four  teams,  with  eight  students  per  team.  In
each  team,  there  were  two  sides  (pro  and  con)  and  each  side  consisted  of  four
students. Each student in the team was randomly assigned to either the pro-  or
con-side.  At  the  end  of  the  study,  two  students  from  each  team  (one  per  side)
in  the  experimental  group  were  randomly  selected  for  a  semi-structured  inter-
view. Each interviewee was interviewed for fifteen minutes. The interviews were
recorded in audio using a recording pen.

4.2   Instructional design

The course was conducted over 18 weeks, with students engaging in two consecu-
tive 45-min classes per week. Considering that systematic basic knowledge related
to argumentation, AMs, and ChatGPT are needed to inform students, the instructor’s
lectures  featured  these  aspects  in  the  first  two  weeks  of  the  course.  The  instructor
provided students with a comprehensive introduction to course arrangement, argu-
mentation,  and  educational  technology,  including  course  goals,  content  outlines,
course  schedules,  key  points,  and  typical  cases  for  argumentation,  AM,  and  Chat-
GPT,  as  well  as  assessment  criteria  for  students’  performance.  To  ensure  that  stu-
dents could better understand argumentation, several guidelines were listed as fol-
lows:  (1)  all  statements  should  be  expressed  in  a  respectful  way;  (2)  information
should  be  clear,  accurate,  and  comprehensive;  (3)  facts,  statistical  data,  or  related
literature are needed when supporting or rebutting opinions; (4) content should be
organized logically and professionally; and (5) arguments should be presented using
the  same  concepts.  Students’  critical  thinking  skills  were  evaluated  online  using  a
questionnaire  at  the  end  of  the  second  week.  From  the  3rd  to  the  17th  week,  five
debates were integrated into the course in both the control and experimental groups,
each of which centered on a specific topic and lasted for three weeks (Table 1).

Each debate topic was lectured twice during the first two weeks of the three-week
cycle, and the instructor assigned a topic to students immediately after the first week
of the lecture. Both the control and experimental groups were given two weeks to
engage  in  the  debate.  In  the  final  week  of  each  three-week  cycle,  all  groups  were
required to present the key structure and content of their debates (Table 2).

“ShiMo” was used as the collaborative platform to conduct argumentation. It not
only enables students to engage in textual debates but also facilitates visual debates
using AMs. All of the students were asked to register on the platform using their real
names. In the experimental group, students filled in rectangles with debate content,
and each side was represented by a specific color. A red dot indicated the con side,
and a blue dot indicated the pro side. The students were required to label their names
after  completing  each  rectangle.  Additionally,  students  were  encouraged  to  seek
timely  guidance  from  ChatGPT  when  they  did  not  know  how  to  rebut  (opposite)
or support (same-side) viewpoints. ChatGPT can interact with individuals, answer
questions, and collaborate on creative work. When ChatGPT was referred to during
argumentation, it was marked as “name + ChatGPT” within the relevant rectangle.
Students were not permitted to copy content directly from ChatGPT and were asked
to select relevant materials for their argumentation. In the control group, participants
from both the pro- and con-sides engaged in textual argumentation using a word pro-
cessing tool. This tool automatically identifies and marks students’ names. Consid-
ering the third argumentation regarding whether the application of media improves
or does not improve learning efficiency, Fig. 1 illustrates the argumentation process
for the experimental and control groups.

During  the  18th  week,  students  conducted  a  comprehensive  review  of  their
semester-long learning and engaged in discussions utilizing the acquired knowledge.

4.3   Instrument

This  study  adopted  Murphy’s  (2004)  framework  for  evaluating  students’  critical
thinking skills. It has been widely utilized to evaluate college students’ critical think-
ing skills through coding their online asynchronous discussions (Zhang et al., 2024,
2023a, 2023b). This framework integrates four critical thinking models: Brookfield
(1987), Norris and Ennis (1989), Bullen (1998), Garrison et al. (2001). These mod-
els categorize critical thinking skills into various phases, such as the five identified
by Brookfield (1987): trigger event, appraisal, exploration, development of alterna-
tive perspectives, and integration. Specifically, Murphy’s framework consists of five
phases, organized in ascending order according to the level of required critical think-
ing skills (Table 3). For example, students who analyze viewpoints perform better

4.4   Data collection and analysis

The data were collected from four sources. One source was the California Critical
Thinking  Skills  questionnaire,  utilized  as  both  a  pre-  and  post-test  instrument  to
evaluate students’ critical thinking skills. The questionnaire was distributed through
the  “Questionnaire  Star”  platform,  and  a  total  of  64  questionnaires  were  collected
in each test. An independent samples t-test was used to assess differences in critical
thinking  skills  between  the  control  and  experimental  groups  for  the  questionnaire
data.

The second source was students’ arguments presented on the “ShiMo” platform
during  five  debates.  Two  undergraduate  students  majoring  in  educational  technol-
ogy independently coded the collected content. Students’ arguments were analyzed
based on the analysis unit. Each sentence was considered an analysis unit; all units
were  analyzed  using  Murphy’s  (2004)  framework,  and  the  revised  argumentative
behavior framework based on Gan and Hill (2014). The experimental group gener-
ated 753 analysis units while the number was 389 in the control group.

To ensure the reliability of the encoded data, two coders participated in two two-
hour  training  sessions  on  analyzing  students’  argumentation  utilizing  the  coding
framework.  The  instructor  and  two  coders  discussed  the  meaning  of  each  critical
thinking skill phase, methods for dividing units, and the appropriate critical think-
ing  skill  phases  for  the  units,  along  with  the  rationale  supporting  these  decisions.
Subsequently, the two coders independently encoded approximately 10% of the units
(O’Connor & Joffe, 2020), achieving a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.88, indicat-
ing strong inter-coder agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). The remaining data were
then independently coded. During the coding process, the two coders negotiated to

resolve  any  inconsistencies.  Furthermore,  the  instructor  reviewed  and  refined  the

To investigate the impact of ChatGPT and AM-supported online argumentation on
students’  critical  thinking  skills,  first,  descriptive  statistics  were  used  to  illustrate
the proportion of different critical thinking skill phases within each argumentation
behavior.  The  results  were  visually  depicted  using  a  Sankey  diagram,  where  the
width  of  each  branch  extension  corresponds  directly  to  the  volume  of  data  traffic,
indicating its proportion in the study.

Table 5 presents samples of students’ arguments in the debate activity. The debate
topic was “The gap in primary education could be narrowed/widened by using infor-
mation technology.” The pro side supported the opinion “The gap in primary edu-
cation  could  be  narrowed  by  using  information  technology.”  Conversely,  the  con
side asserted the opinion “The gap in primary education could be widened by using
information  technology.”  DA,  DB,  and  DD  signify  arguments  in  favor  of  the  pro
side. DC represents arguments supporting the con side. ChatGPT denotes arguments
proposed by referring to ChatGPT, which belongs to the con side.

Second,  epistemic  network  analysis  (ENA)  was  utilized  to  explore  the  relation-
ship between critical thinking skill phases and argumentative behaviors. ENA is an
analytical technique that characterizes individual or group cognitive traits by analyz-
ing log data and other records related to individual and collaborative learning, result-
ing in a dynamic network analysis model. In the current study, the ENA Web Tool
(http:// www. epist emicn etwork. org/)  was  adopted  to  process  all  of  the  codes.  ENA
determines the relationships among codes (i.e., the critical thinking skill phase) in
what  is  termed  a  stanza;  a  moving-window  method  is  usually  used  to  divide  con-
tent-related messages into stanzas. This method assesses and analyzes coordination
among individuals within groups and groups as a whole, providing insights into dis-
course interconnectivity (Siebert-Evenstone et al., 2017). During the coding process,
we identified the preceding conversational turn to which each utterance responded
within the entire dataset and then used a moving stanza window with a size of seven
codes. Subsequently, the ENA web tool illustrated the relationships among the codes
within each stanza by analyzing the units. This study selected both group types (i.e.,
control vs. experimental) and argumentative behaviors (initiate, response, feedback,
and deep) as the units, aiming to identify distinctions between the control and exper-
imental groups across different argumentative behaviors.

For  each  stanza,  the  ENA  generates  an  adjacency  matrix  to  calculate  the  co-
occurrence  associations  of  each  code.  The  adjacency  matrix  assigns  a  value  of
“1”  when  two  codes  co-occur  within  a  stanza  and  a  value  of  “0”  when  they  do
not.  Consequently,  each  stanza  has  an  adjacency  matrix,  and  the  aggregation
of  these  matrices  produces  a  cumulative  matrix,  revealing  the  underlying  epis-
temic  networks  of  students’  activities.  To  present  the  data  visually,  the  cumula-
tive adjacency matrix was transformed into position nodes as individual locations
for each code. Additionally, the ENA space was visualized using two dimensions
that explained the majority of the data variance (Shafer et al., 2016). Specifically,
the  X-axis  represents  the  dimension  with  the  highest  variance  percentage  and
the  Y-axis  denotes  the  dimension  with  the  second-highest  variance  percentage.

5.1.1   Distribution of critical thinking skill phases in different argumentative

Figure  3  illustrates  the  distribution  of  critical  thinking  skill  phases  across  various

mental groups. In the control group, the distribution of critical thinking skill phases
remained  largely  consistent  across  the  range  of  argumentative  behaviors.  Among
the four types of argumentative behaviors, recognition (R), understanding (U), and
analysis (A) occupied a significant proportion, whereas evaluation (E) and creation
(C)  proportions  were  relatively  low,  indicating  limited  improvement  in  students’
critical  thinking  skills  with  increasing  complexity  of  argumentative  behaviors.  By
contrast, the experimental group exhibited significant variations in the proportion of
critical thinking skill phases among different argumentative behaviors. Specifically,
recognition (R) and understanding (U) were highly prevalent in initial and response
argumentative behaviors, whereas evaluation (E) and analysis (A) were predominant
in  feedback  argumentative  behavior.  Creation  (C)  and  evaluation  (E)  constituted  a
significant  proportion  of  deep  argumentative  behavior.  These  results  demonstrate
improved  critical  thinking  skills  among  students  as  their  argumentative  behaviors
deepened.  Therefore,  when  argumentative  behaviors  became  more  complex,  stu-
dents  in  the  experimental  group  engaged  in  more  evaluation  (E)  and  creation  (C),
indicating a higher level of critical thinking skills than those in the control group.

6   Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the influence of ChatGPT and AM-
supported online argumentation on college students’ critical thinking skills and per-

AM-supported  online  argumentation  can  enhance  students’  critical  thinking  skills
by eliciting more high-quality rebuttals.

First,  AM  clearly  presented  the  structure  of  argumentation  and  encouraged  stu-
dents  to  actively  engage  in  rebuttals,  addressing  the  challenges  that  students  usu-
ally encounter in constructing valid arguments within complex and non-linear argu-
mentation (Noroozi et al., 2020). This finding aligns with Alsmari (2022) and Liu
et  al.,  (2024a,  2024b),  indicating  that  students  find  it  more  flexible  and  easier  to
rebut viewpoints on the opposite side by utilizing AM online. AM enables students
to clearly rebut opposing arguments within complex scenarios (Rodger & Stewart-
Lord, 2020). In the current study, the debate topics were derived from real-life sce-
narios and contemporary issues, such as “The application of media improves/does
not  improve  learning  efficiency.”  Yilmaz-Na  and  Sonmez  (2023b)  suggested  that
authentic  cases  motivate  students  to  actively  engage  in  argumentation  because  of
their  inherent  complexity  and  lack  of  a  single,  definitive  answer.  By  constructing
AMs,  students  were  able  to  demonstrate  complex  solutions  in  a  structured  man-
ner, which helped them convince others that their claims were more reasonable and
valid.  This  process  has  been  proven  to  improve  the  quality  of  argumentation  and
foster students’ critical thinking skills (Britt, 2015; Tawfik et al., 2018). The results
of  the  perception  questionnaire  and  interview  further  confirmed  this  finding,  with
all  students  (100%)  agreeing  or  strongly  agreeing  that  AM  provided  a  clear  argu-
mentation structure, including supporting evidence, rebuttals, and the relationships
among them. Additionally, by visually presenting both weak and strong arguments,
AM  simplifies  the  process  by  which  teachers  identify  students’  shortcomings  and
provide immediate feedback (Fan & Chen, 2021). The timely identification and cor-
rection  of  inappropriate  arguments  are  crucial  for  enhancing  the  overall  quality  of
argumentation.

However,  in  contrast  to  the  findings  reported  by  Kaya  (2024),  which  indicated
that computer-supported AM could negatively affect critical thinking skills for the
overuse may lead to students’ decreased engagement, students in the present study
demonstrated  greater  curiosity  towards  computer-supported  AM  due  to  their  lack
of prior experience with it. Additionally, coursework knowledge is essential for stu-
dents  to  engage  effectively  in  argumentation  and  formulate  well-formed  rebuttals
(McGee et al., 2020). In the current study, the teacher lectured before each argumen-
tation session, aiming to facilitate students’ mastery of professional knowledge and
immerse them in the concrete and realistic scenarios created by AM (Archila et al.,
2022a, 2022b). To maintain students’ continuous engagement, diverse pedagogical
strategies, such as gamification, should be incorporated when using computer-sup-
ported AM to promote critical thinking skills.

Second, AI tools, such as ChatGPT, can guide students to analyze, evaluate, or
create  through  multiple  rebuttals  by  providing  detailed  feedback,  thereby  promot-
ing  critical  thinking  skills.  This  result  is  consistent  with  Puente  et  al.  (2024)  and
Darmawansah et al. (2024), suggesting that ChatGPT assists students in generating
counterarguments and improving critical thinking skills by providing prompt-based
feedback in argumentation. According to Karataş et al. (2024), ChatGPT feedback
always serves as a scaffold, promoting deep engagement in analysis, evaluation, and
creation  among  students  in  complex  scenarios,  such  as  rebuttals,  which  has  been

17649

proven to enhance their critical thinking skills (Yilmaz & Yilmaz, 2023). The result
was further supported by the perception questionnaire and interview, with 93.33% of
the students believing that ChatGPT provided detailed feedback, including concrete
examples, empirical data, and a variety of ideas.

Additionally, the feedback provided by ChatGPT could also positively impacted
students’ affective domains, such as attracting their interest in rebutting arguments.
These affective factors of attitudes and beliefs not only play a crucial role in deter-
mining  ChatGPT’s  practical  application  efficiency  (Yifan  et  al.,  2023),  but  also
represent important factors for enhancing critical thinking skills (Simonovic et al.,
2023).  A  similar  result  was  reported  by  Guo  and  Lee  (2023),  who  found  that  stu-
dents expressed an increased usage of ChatGPT to promote critical thinking skills
and a willingness to recommend it to their peers. According to the results of the per-
ception questionnaire, 66.67% of the students agreed or strongly agreed that Chat-
GPT  enhanced  their  positive  attitudes  toward  argumentation.  Among  the  numer-
ous AI tools, ChatGPT is often perceived as a virtual peer engaged in discussions
that  provides  suggestions  to  promote  expression  skills  during  the  learning  process
(Hartwell  &  Aull,  2022).  It  has  been  praised  for  its  immediate  feedback,  natural
interactions, and capacity to alleviate anxiety and isolation, which are essential for
enhancing  student  confidence  and  satisfaction  in  argumentation.  Moreover,  rebut-
ting others’ opinions is sometimes perceived as impolite, leading students to hesitate
in presenting rebuttals due to concerns about damaging their interpersonal relation-
ships (Aoyagi et al., 2010). In the current study, students considered ChatGPT as a
virtual peer and rebutted its output without experiencing any psychological stress.

However,  the  finding  that  ChatGPT’s  feedback  facilitated  high-quality  rebuttals
was inconsistent with Guo et al. (2023), who found no significant enhancement in
students’  ability  to  construct  reasonable  rebuttals  using  AI  tools  such  as  chatbots
during  argumentative  tasks.  A  possible  explanation  for  this  discrepancy  is  that
retrieval-based chatbots have limitations in evaluating the quality of user input and
thus struggle to provide meaningful feedback. In the current study, ChatGPT, a new
chatbot  model  fine-tuned  from  GPT-3,  exhibited  an  improved  capability  to  com-
prehend  natural  language  and  generate  personalized  feedback  in  response  to  que-
ries (Rudolph et al., 2023). However, Wang et al. (2023) argued that ChatGPT was
limited in providing affective feedback for argumentation compared to the teacher.
Therefore, incorporating ChatGPT as a complementary resource in conjunction with
teachers and achieving a balanced integration of AI and human guidance are crucial
for students to effectively engage in argumentation. Another possible explanation is
that in Guo et al. (2023), students were asked to formulate arguments individually
without a chatbot or peer interaction during the post-test; they were still challenged
to propose rebuttals by themselves. From this perspective, teacher scaffolds such as
summarizing strategies or providing concrete rebuttal templates are needed to facili-
tate independent rebuttal practices among students in argumentative tasks.

The  result  that  ChatGPT  encouraged  students  to  rebut  arguments  by  analyzing,
evaluating,  or  creating  do  not  align  with  Essien  et  al.  (2024),  who  proposed  that
ChatGPT significantly facilitates the development lower-level cognitive skills, such
as  understanding.  A  possible  explanation  for  the  observed  discrepancy  is  that  the
students  in  our  study,  having  received  adequate  instruction  on  utilizing  ChatGPT

during the argumentation activities, were able to provide relevant inputs and elicit
feedback, which improved their ability to utilize ChatGPT for analytical, evaluative,
or  creative  purposes.  Therefore,  restructuring  the  course  to  integrate  core  profes-
sional knowledge with supplementary activities designed to promote students’ infor-
mation literacy, such as using ChatGPT, is essential for the effective utilization of
AI tools to enhance learning experiences (Pham et al., 2023). Another explanation
might be that the socio-scientific debate topics utilized in the study were relatively
easy to rebut (Demircioglu et al., 2023), which provide more chances for ChatGPT
to  deeply  process  the  information  and  offer  feedback  demonstrating  higher-level
cognitive skills, such as analysis and evaluation. These serve as references for stu-
dents to analyze, evaluate, or create arguments.

Finally, the students’ critical attitudes toward ChatGPT’s output encouraged them
to rebut with analysis, evaluation or creation, thereby cultivating their critical think-
ing skills. According to the perception questionnaire and interview, 46.66% of the
students disagreed or strongly disagreed that ChatGPT provided them with appropri-
ate guidance, as the feedback was occasionally irrelevant, inaccurate, or lacking in
value. The result was consistent with Berg and Plessis (2023), which demonstrated
that  ChatGPT  may  lack  a  deep  understanding  of  common  sense  reasoning,  result-
ing in answers that sound plausible but are fundamentally incorrect or nonsensical.
Similarly, Kiryakova and Angelova (2023) highlighted the limitations of ChatGPT
in handling complex tasks because of a lack of background knowledge. Contrary to
previous study that have demonstrated the inappropriate output of ChatGPT misled
students  and  weaken  their  critical  thinking  skills  (Mohammad  et  al.,  2023),  argu-
mentation, in which arguers aim to convince opponents of their viewpoints to win
arguments  (Hennessey  et  al.,  2015),  encourages  students  to  critically  analyze  or
evaluate the output of ChatGPT before incorporating it into their arguments, thereby
ensuring that their arguments are well-reasoned and resilient to rebuttal. Addition-
ally,  those  rebutting  ChatGPT-based  content  must  create  novel  ideas  because  the
content is always comprehensive. This alleviates concerns that students may overly
rely on ChatGPT without self-reflection and ultimately promote their critical think-
ing  skills  (Hadi  et  al.,  2024).  However,  in  non-argumentative  instructional  activi-
ties, it is still common for students to over-rely on AI systems, utilizing ChatGPT to
complete assignments without contributing to their original thoughts (Javaid et al.,
2023).  Therefore,  specific  guidelines  for  the  utilization  of  ChatGPT  in  education
should be developed to mitigate potential negative consequences.

7   Conclusions, suggestions and limitations

In  conclusion,  this  study  demonstrates  that  the  integration  of  ChatGPT  with  com-
puter-supported  AM  is  an  effective  teaching  strategy  for  enhancing  critical  think-
ing  skills  among  college  students.  Specifically,  AMs  clearly  presented  the  argu-
ment structure, whereas ChatGPT provided detailed and clear feedback, motivating
students  to  engage  in  multiple  rebuttals  through  analyzing,  evaluating,  or  creating
viewpoints.  Additionally,  students  expressed  skepticism  regarding  the  appropriate-
ness,  rationality,  and  accuracy  of  the  ChatGPT  output.  While  our  study  provides

Education and Information Technologies (2025) 30:17623–17658

indicative  insights  into  how  ChatGPT  and  AM-supported  online  argumentation
might  impact  the  enhancement  of  critical  thinking  skills,  it  is  crucial  to  recognize
that critical thinking is a sophisticated and multifaceted skill influenced by numer-
ous  factors  extending  beyond  the  integration  of  information  tools  in  education.
These include students’ information literacy and the types of tasks assigned. In sum-
mary, our findings indicate that integrating ChatGPT and computer-supported AM
with argumentation can promote students’ critical thinking skills. It encourages edu-
cators to explore these possibilities and reconsider their teaching strategies to foster
a holistic learning environment.

7.1   Theoretical implications

Our  study  has  two  major  theoretical  implications.  Firstly,  it  enhances  the  exist-
ing  understanding  of  the  development  of  critical  thinking  skills  in  argumentative
activities. Previous studies have primarily  focused on  AM-supported or ChatGPT-
supported  online  argumentation,  with  limited  detailed  analysis  of  how  these  tools
impact critical thinking skills. By investigating the effect and perceptions of Chat-
GPT and AM-supported online argumentation on critical thinking skills, our study
provides deeper insights and extends upon prior research in this area. Secondly, our
study contributes to the dialogue around the practical applications of ChatGPT and
computer-supported  AMs  in  academic  settings.  It  identifies  methods  for  integrat-
ing  ChatGPT  and  computer-supported  AMs  into  educational  strategies  to  improve
students’ engagement and critical thinking skills. Additionally, it provides detailed
analyses  of  the  tools’  utility,  particularly  in  the  context  of  argumentation,  empha-
sizing their potential to revolutionize the teaching and learning of critical thinking
skills in higher education.

7.2   Practical implications

The  integration  of  ChatGPT  and  computer-supported  AM  in  the  educational  set-
tings,  especially  in  argumentation  activities,  significantly  enhances  the  develop-
ment of critical thinking skills, which have become increasingly crucial in today’s
information-saturated environment. This offers teachers, students, schools, and poli-
cymakers a strong incentive to incorporate these computer-driven tools into educa-
tional systems to facilitate students’ critical thinking skills. Teachers must possess
professional knowledge and skills necessary to utilize ChatGPT and computer-sup-
ported  AM.  Meanwhile,  it  is  crucial  to  implement  strategies  to  mitigate  the  over-
use  by  students,  such  as  encouraging  them  to  share  how  these  tools  enhance  their
argumentation  processes.  For  students,  mastering  these  tools  and  advanced  cogni-
tive skills, such as evaluation and analysis, is essential for effective utilization and to
avoid over-reliance on computer-supported AM and ChatGPT. For schools, integrat-
ing AI-related training into course content or scheduling specific training sessions is
recommended to enhance students’ information literacy when integrating ChatGPT
or computer-supported AM into the classroom. Policymakers must develop a com-
prehensive policy framework that includes both the educational benefits and ethical

considerations  in  AI  interaction,  ensuring  the  equitable  access  to  enhanced  learn-
ing experiences facilitated by tools like ChatGPT, regardless of students’ socio-eco-
nomic backgrounds.

This  study  has  three  main  limitations.  First,  the  study  duration  was  relatively
short,  as  only  five  debate  activities  were  implemented;  it  has  been  suggested  that
cultivating critical thinking skills may take longer than one semester. Future studies
should enable students to experience ChatGPT and AM-supported online argumen-
tation  for  a  one-year  period.  Second,  the  sample  size  was  relatively  small;  hence,
caution should be applied when generalizing these findings to a wider population.
Larger samples should be included in future studies that replicate our design. Third,
although  the  results  demonstrated  how  ChatGPT  and  AM-supported  online  argu-
mentation impact students’ critical thinking skills, it remains unclear how students
with  different  levels  of  critical  thinking  skills  (low  or  high)  participated;  future
research exploring the participation patterns of different types of students in argu-
mentation is needed.
