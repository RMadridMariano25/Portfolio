The rapid integration of Generative AI (GenAI) into higher education presents both opportunities and systemic
challenges, particularly in domains where feedback is central to learning. This study investigates the capacity of a
large language model to generate formative feedback for student-created UML diagrams in a university software
engineering course. Across two cohorts (N = 262), AI-generated, teacher-generated, and no-feedback conditions
were  compared,  analyzing  student  perceptions,  learning  outcomes,  and  grading  reliability.  Results  show  that
while students rated GenAI feedback as beneficial and often comparable to human comments, teacher feedback
remained  more  effective  in  supporting  performance  gains,  especially  in  complex  modeling  tasks.  Linguistic
analysis  further  revealed  that  GenAI  feedback  was  more  repetitive  and  less  pedagogically  rich  than  human
feedback.  Beyond  these  course-level  findings,  the  study  highlights  broader  implications  for  higher  education
systems.  GenAI  feedback  represents  not  just  a  pedagogical  tool  but  a  cognitive  partner  that  can  reshape
assessment models, curriculum design, and faculty roles. Its scalable nature offers potential to democratize access
to high-quality formative feedback, while also raising equity, accountability, and policy challenges at institu-
tional and sectoral levels. By situating empirical results into this broader frame, the study argues that GenAI is
catalyzing a paradigm shift toward new systems of learning, where feedback becomes systemic, scalable, and
embedded in the very structure of higher education.

1. Introduction

The  introduction  of  ChatGPT  has  opened  possibilities  to  integrate
accessible Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) and Large Language
Models  (LLMs) into  education. These models have  proven their capa-
bilities  across  tasks  such  as  code  generation  (Liu  et  al.,  2023),  test
automation  (Zimmermann  &  Koziolek,  2023),  and  assessment  (Gao
et al., 2024; Henkel et al., 2024). When prompted accordingly, GenAI
can  perform  a  wide  range  of  functions,  presenting  opportunities  to
support educators in time-intensive tasks and enhance student learning
experience.  One  domain  where  this  potential  is  especially  relevant  is
diagrammatic modeling, particularly the design and evaluation of Uni-
fied Modeling Language (UML) diagrams. UML diagrams are considered
a fundamental part of software engineering, with their ability to visually
describe  the  structural  aspects  of  software  systems  (Ozkaya  &  Erata,
2020). Designing a UML diagram requires the students to translate ab-
stract system requirements into structured visual representations, which
makes  these  tasks  high  in  cognitive  load  and  involves  trade-offs  in
design, structure, and semantics. Diagrammatic modeling tasks, such as

Existing approaches to automated UML assessment have primarily
focused on rule-based systems or machine learning techniques aimed at
grading  accuracy  rather  than  learning  support  (Beck  et  al.,  2015;
Bergstr¨om et al., 2022; Correia et al., 2017). Although these systems can
detect  syntactic  or  structural  errors,  they  often  struggle  to  deliver
explanatory,  design-oriented,  or  reflective  feedback  that  supports
higher-order  learning  processes.  As  a  result,  a  persistent  gap  remains
between scalable automated assessment and pedagogically meaningful
formative feedback in diagrammatic modeling contexts.

GenAI  tools  are  increasingly integrated  into  educational practices,
like designing inquiry-based learning lesson plans (Moundridou et al.,
2024),  generating  codes  (Liu  et  al.,  2023),  or  automating  feedback
across various domains (Gao et al., 2024; Henkel et al., 2024; Pecuchova
& Drlik, 2025). GenAI can improve student motivation and the learning
process by offering immediate feedback (Kasneci et al., 2023). Against
this backdrop, GenAI has emerged as a potential response to the scal-
ability challenges of formative feedback, rather than merely as a tool for
automation or efficiency. Unlike earlier rule-based or task-specific sys-
tems,  LLMs  offer  the  ability  to  generate  context-sensitive,  natural--
language  feedback  that  may  address  not  only  correctness  but  also
reasoning,  design  choices,  and  reflection.  In  programming  education,
GenAI  automated  assessment  helps  move  beyond  traditional  systems
that provided only limited feedback or relied on predefined templates
(Zhang et al., 2024). They have been used to offer detailed explanations
for student mistakes or provide single-sentence hints in a conversational
manner (Heickal & Lan, 2024). These models have led to the develop-
ment of various programming feedback tools that are being deployed in
classrooms  and  offer  quality  feedback  through  iterative  improvement
(Prather et al., 2024). Automatization of feedback can be beneficial for
high-enrollment courses, where it can adjust the learning experience to
each  student's  progress  and learning  style (Y. Dai et  al., 2023; Zhang
et  al.,  2024).  These  benefits  extend  beyond  programming,  where,  for
example, in writing instruction, GenAI has produced feedback rated as
more readable and consistent than human-generated feedback, though
often less precise (Biber et al., 2011; Fleckenstein et al., 2023). Dai et al.
(2024) showed that GenAI-generated feedback outperforms human in-
structors because they often struggle to deliver quality feedback, mainly
in  large  classes  consistently.  However,  studies  highlight  GenAI's  limi-
tations  in  handling  domain-specific  language,  producing  hallucinated
content, and failing to capture conceptual misunderstandings without
careful  prompt  design.  These  shortcomings  underscore  the  need  for
hybrid systems (Azaria et al., 2024; Phung et al., 2024), where human
supervision  supports  GenAI  feedback  generation  (Tuomisto  &  Harju-
maa, 2025).

More recently, GenAI has begun to move from standalone applica-
tions toward infrastructure integration in education. This implies a more
integrated  and  scalable  role  within  existing  systems,  moving  beyond
human interaction (Kinder et al., 2025). Feedback generation is being
embedded into educational platforms and learning management systems
(LMS)  through  APIs,  transforming  GenAI  into  a  background  service
capable of producing on-demand, context-aware responses to requests
(Pereira  Cipriano  et  al.,  2024).  This  shift  supports  modular  system
design,  allowing  for  scalable  feedback  that  is  maintainable,  config-
urable, and extensible across courses and existing educational platforms,
and reusability (Amiri & Islam, 2025). One of the most significant ways
GenAI becomes an infrastructural element is by overcoming the tradi-
tional  challenges  of  providing  personalized  and  adaptive  feedback  at
scale (Kinder et al., 2025). When combined with structured input data,
such as student-submitted UML diagrams, GenAI models may function
not only  as evaluators but as  feedback systems capable of supporting
large cohorts with minimal teacher involvement. This changes the ca-
pacity of feedback delivery, making it a systemic, pervasive capability
rather than a bottleneck dependent on human availability (Kinder et al.,
2025). This transformation reflects a broader paradigm shift in educa-
tion systems, where GenAI is positioned not as a supplementary tool but
as  an  instructional  component  capable  of  reshaping  assessment,

feedback,  and  instructional  design  (Sandu  et  al.,  2024;  Wang  et  al.,
2024).  Understanding  the  suggestions  of  this  shift  requires  empirical
studies that evaluate GenAI's educational value in complex, structured
tasks, such as UML modeling.

Despite growing interest in the use of GenAI for automated assess-
ment and grading in software engineering education, empirical evidence
on its capacity to provide pedagogically meaningful formative feedback
for complex, diagrammatic modeling tasks remains limited. In partic-
ular, little is known about how GenAI-generated feedback compares to
human  instructor  feedback  in  terms  of  feedback  types,  instructional
richness, and its impact on student learning outcomes beyond surface-
level  performance.  Addressing  this  gap  is  essential  for  understanding
whether GenAI can function as a learning-oriented feedback mechanism
rather than merely an evaluative or grading tool. The results achieved in
evaluation of GenAI feedback to programming tasks (Koutcheme et al.,
2025; Lai & Lin, 2025; Sandu et al., 2024) show a promising direction
and offer potential to address in this paper.

This  study  investigates  the  quality  and  usefulness  of  GenAI-
generated  feedback  for  UML  design  assignments  in  a  university  soft-
ware engineering course. Specifically, the aim is to evaluate how well
GenAI  can  identify  structural  or  semantic  issues  in  student-submitted
UML  diagrams,  and  how  students  interpret  and  value  this  feedback.
Unlike prior work focused primarily on grading, the goal of this study is
to assess GenAI's capacity to support learning through formative feed-
back in structurally complex, diagrammatic modeling tasks.

This study contributes a perspective by demonstrating how GenAI,
when  deployed  as  an  embedded  feedback  infrastructure,  can  extend
beyond isolated tutoring scenarios and function as a scalable cognitive
partner in educational ecosystems. What makes this approach distinc-
tive is the integration of GenAI into diagrammatic modeling, a domain
traditionally  reliant  on  expert  interpretation,  and  also  the  systemic
configuration of feedback as a service-level educational component. The
study explores how prompt design, input structure, and task complexity
interact with GenAI's behavior, offering insights into the stability and
robustness  of  AI-generated  feedback  across  varied  instructional  con-
texts.  This  has  direct  implications  for  building  hybrid  teacher–AI
assessment models, where educators focus on mentoring, nuance, and
higher-order guidance, while GenAI supports high-frequency formative
feedback loops.

By  embedding  GenAI  into  the  feedback  architecture  of  complex,
open-ended  tasks,  the  research  moves  toward  a  model  of  feedback-
abundant learning that may help overcome the long-standing trade-off
between cognitive load and instructional scalability. The findings sup-
port a shift in educational systems from viewing AI as an auxiliary tool
toward suggesting how feedback may increasingly be conceptualized as
an infrastructural capability within hybrid human–AI educational sys-
tems, a perspective aligned with broader calls to rethink curriculum and
pedagogy in the age of cognitive technologies. In doing so, this work
offers both empirical grounding and forward-looking extrapolation into
how  GenAI  can  support  scalable,  personalized,  and  pedagogically
meaningful assessment systems in higher education.

The paper is organized as follows. The related work on integrating
GenAI into education is summarized in Section 2. Section 3 details the
dataset  used  and  the  methods  to  achieve  the  aim  of  the  experiment.
Section 4 presents the results of producing feedback using GenAI and
evaluating the quality of the generated feedback. Section 5 provides a
discussion of the results and implications for education. Subsequently,
the conclusions and ideas for future research are presented.

2. Related work

GenAI  offers  new  opportunities  to  enhance  feedback  delivery  in
computing education. Across various tasks, its ability to generate timely,
context-sensitive feedback has been examined to help students identify
errors, understand concepts, and develop self-directed learning strate-
gies. Frankford et al. (2024) examined the GPT-3.5-powered AI-Tutor

designed to give feedback to students’ coding assignments. The model
was set to guide students to solve the coding issues without providing
direct solutions. They identified two usage patterns by students that can
help  in  designing  AI-powered  educational  systems.  The  first  pattern
involved students using continuous prompting from the AI-Tutor. While
this  was  beneficial  for  students  seeking  guidance,  there  was  a  rising
concern  about over-reliance  on  the  AI-Tutor. The  second pattern  was
oriented  towards  an  iterative  learning  approach,  where  the  students
relied heavily on the test feedback to guide their solution. While stu-
dents  appreciated  the  timely  and  scalable  feedback,  the  system  also
produced  occasional  generic  responses  and  required  careful  instruc-
tional  framing.  Messer  et  al.  (2024) noted  in  a  systematic  review  of
automatic feedback systems that most studies lack human evaluation of
AI-generated  feedback,  limiting  the  understanding  of  its  instructional
quality.

Other studies focused on more specific tasks. Ahmed et al. (2019)
used targeted examples for compilation errors and compared the time
taken to repair these errors with and without feedback. On average, the
feedback improved the time necessary to repair the bugs. P˘adurean et al.
(2025) argue  that  students  mainly  use  the  trial-and-error  methods  to
resolve coding issues, while the ability to identify and hypothesize the
cause of errors is crucial. They used GenAI to generate buggy assign-
ments from a problem description and verify them using a test suite. The
results showed that GenAI generates comparable assignments to teach-
ers, and both GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 were able to generate diverse sets of
exercises  with  varying  difficulty.  This  indicates  that  for  the  GenAI
models to produce quality feedback, they have to be able to create faulty
assignments that teach students to identify issues and improve critical
thinking.  Koutcheme  et  al.  (2025) compared  open-source  and  pro-
prietary LLMs, evaluating their ability to generate and assess feedback.
Proprietary  models  showed  the  highest  performance  across  all  the
criteria  examined.  The  authors  identified  quality  gaps  by  identifying
irrelevant  issues.  However,  most  of  the  time,  they  produced  under-
standable feedback. The results showed potential in the integration of
open-source LLMs into feedback tools. However, some models require a
lot of computational power, which can make integration difficult. The
results also showed that smaller models could, with careful fine-tuning,
be useable for feedback generation. Sandu et al. (2024) and Lai and Lin
(2025) extended  these  findings  to  curriculum  level  developments,
showing  how  GenAI  could  support  high-enrollment  courses  and  offer
dynamic,  personalized  feedback.  However,  concerns  remain  about
over-reliance,  hallucinations,  and  insufficient  conceptual  depth  in
GenAI-generated responses.

Providing personalized feedback is a challenging task for teachers.
Despite  the  advancements,  studies  show  that  students  often  find  AI-
generated  feedback  less  helpful  than  instructor  feedback.  Er  et  al.
(2025) found  that GenAI  models lack  contextual  sensitivity  and  need
context-specific  training  to  produce  helpful  feedback.  The  students
perceived  the  instructors’  feedback  as  more  useful,  and  students
receiving AI feedback showed lower improvement in learning outcomes.
Similarly,  Li  et  al.  (2025) observed  that  while  GenAI  can  enhance
learning outcomes in the short term, it may negatively affect long-term
knowledge transfer. This creates the risk of over-reliance on GenAI, as
the  students  trust  the  feedback without  critical thinking.  The authors
recommended  using  GenAI  to  support  student  engagement,  while
encouraging  other  problem-solving  assignments  without  external  re-
sources.  This  way,  students  can  improve  their  academic  performance
while  maximizing  learning  benefits.  Similar  concerns  are  raised  by
Sheese  et  al.  (2024),  who  noted  that  students  often  submit  minimal
prompts,  missing  deeper  conceptual  understanding.  This  raises  the
requirement of human supervision to maintain feedback quality.

The  UML  diagrams  are  used  in  the  analysis  and  construction  of
software systems. The quality of the layout is essential to share knowl-
edge  about  the  system  between  developers.  Automated  assessment  of
the  designing  tasks  has  been  a  research  goal,  particularly  for  use  in
online learning settings. Bergstr¨om et al. (2022) used machine learning

techniques to build regression models based on features extracted using
image processing. The models were trained on human-labeled diagrams
using a 5-point Likert scale. Despite limitations of a small dataset, their
approach achieved strong alignment with human evaluators, showing
the  potential  for  scalable  grading.  Beck  et  al.  (2015) integrated
rule-based feedback for UML diagrams through LMS Moodle. However,
their approach was limited by the specification of the hand-coded rules,
which struggled with anticipating diverse student errors. Correia et al.
(2017) addressed this by proposing an automated diagram assessment
tool  with  enhanced  feedback.  The  proposed  tool  can  distinguish  be-
tween syntactic and semantic errors and provide specialized feedback.
The authors argued the need for conceptual, formative feedback, not just
error marking, which would help students understand the logic behind
designing UML diagrams.

More  recently,  GenAI  has  been  explored  as  an  evaluator  in  visual
design contexts. Wang et al. (2024) evaluated the quality of UML use
case,  class,  and  sequence  diagrams.  They  created  a  set  of  evaluation
criteria  for  GenAI  to  assess  the  quality  of  examined  UML  diagrams
quantitatively. ChatGPT showed the capability to grade UML diagrams
with similar performance to teachers. Wang et al. (2024) suggested new
directions  in  integrating  GenAI  to  software  engineering  education,
where GenAI can offer objectivity of the grading process and fairness of
scores to some degree. Another point provided was that GenAI can offer
valuable  insights  into  students'  learning  situations  and  help  identify
potential  weaknesses.  Similar  results  were  obtained  by  Bouali  et  al.
(2025) who compared three GenAI models’ capabilities to evaluate UML
class  diagrams.  The  authors  highlighted  that  the  assessment  of  UML
diagrams is a subjective challenge due to the absence of unique, specific
answers. However, a well-designed and configured prompt can help the
GenAI  model  to  effectively  grade  these  subjective  tasks  with  similar
performance  to  human  evaluators.  These  studies  confirmed  the
assumption  about  the  capability  of  GenAI  in  structured  visual  tasks.
However,  it  was  focused  primarily  on  grading  rather  than  formative,
student-facing feedback.

The previous research (Pecuchova & Drlik, 2025) evaluated GenAI
models for assessing responses to open-ended questions. This research
provided one point of view on the possibility of using GenAI models to
work  with  textual  data  that  lacks  objective  reference  answers.  UML
design  tasks  share  this  challenge  of  subjectivity  and  structural  uncer-
tainty,  where  multiple  correct  solutions  exist.  While  existing  studies
tested  GenAI's  ability  to  generate  or  grade  UML  diagrams,  this  study
focuses  on  the  quality  and  perceived  usefulness  of  GenAI-generated
feedback  for  student-created  UML  diagrams.  By  comparing  GenAI
feedback  with  teacher-produced  feedback,  randomly  combined  and
evaluated by students, the aim is to introduce a novel perspective on
how GenAI can be used not just to automate assessment grading, but also
to  support  learning  through  formative  feedback  in  visual  modeling
tasks.

3. Materials & methods

Complex and semantically rich schemas, maps, and diagrams are an
essential and irreplaceable part of learning in higher education settings.
Especially  in  engineering,  business,  or  computer  science  study  pro-
grams, complex visual modeling tasks such as UML and BPMN diagrams,
mind maps, database schemas, object-oriented or architectural design
patterns are integral components of learning, which can prove the stu-
dent's correct understanding of the topic. These tasks engage students in
abstraction,  system  thinking,  and  semantic  reasoning.  However,  they
are simultaneously time-consuming to assess and provide feedback on.
Especially in high-enrollment courses, the formative evaluation of such
diagrammatic  assignments  often  exceeds  the  available  instructional
capacity. As such, missing or superficial feedback frequently causes a
system-level issue, reducing the opportunity for iterative improvement
and self-regulated learning.

This  study  builds  on  the  assumption  that  GenAI  can  serve  as  an

infrastructural solution to the challenge of scalable formative feedback.
Moving beyond its traditional role in natural language or code genera-
tion, GenAI was considered as an autonomous feedback agent embedded
into  a  visual  modeling  workflow.  Student-designed  UML  diagrams,
created using Enterprise Architect, were exported in XML format and
submitted  to  a  large  language  model  (GPTo1).  The  model  returned
natural-language  feedback  in  Slovak.  Unlike  template-based  systems
(Beck et al., 2015; Correia et al., 2017), the feedback was generated in a
free-text form, with no constraints regarding tone, style, or pedagogical
intention.  As  a  result,  these  settings  allow  post-hoc  analysis  of  the
model's communicative behavior.

This study employed a mixed-methods, quasi-experimental research
design  conducted  across  two  academic  years in  a  bachelor-level  Soft-
ware Engineering course. A total of 262 students were divided into three
groups: one receiving feedback generated by a generative AI model, one
receiving teacher-provided feedback, and a control group receiving no
feedback. The design combined quantitative analyses (non-parametric
statistical tests, regression models, inter-grader reliability metrics, and
linguistic/sentiment  analyses)  with  qualitative  categorization  of  feed-
back into diagnostic, design-oriented, motivational, and metacognitive
types. This multi-cohort, multi-modal approach allowed us to evaluate
both the pedagogical quality and systemic implications of AI-generated
feedback, capturing not only its impact on student learning outcomes
but also its communicative characteristics and alignment with human-
provided  feedback.  The  research  design  aimed  to  address  multiple
layers  of  pedagogical  evaluation,  from  affective  tone  to  technical
alignment.  It  was  analyzed  how  GenAI  feedback  is  perceived  by  stu-
dents, what types of comments it tends to generate, and whether it can
match  the  evaluative  reliability  of  human  experts.  Additionally,  the
research investigated the downstream effects of receiving GenAI feed-
back  on  student  learning  outcomes,  comparing  them  to  those  who
received  instructor  feedback  or  no  feedback  at  all.  To  systematically
explore these aspects, the following research questions were formulated:

RQ1:  How  can  the  student-perceived  helpfulness  and  pedagogical
value of GenAI-generated feedback be compared to the instructor's
feedback across UML modeling tasks?
RQ2:  What  types  of  feedback  does  GenAI  produce  spontaneously
without explicit prompt conditioning, and how does this distribution
compare to that of teacher-generated feedback?
RQ3: Does receiving GenAI feedback influence students' modelling
skills and improve their performance on subsequent modeling tasks
and  final  exams,  relative  to  students  receiving  teacher  or  no
feedback?
RQ4:  Can  GenAI  accurately  grade  visual  UML  diagrams  based  on
XML  representations  with  agreement  levels  comparable  to  expert
human graders, and how do different prompt strategies influence this
grading performance?

The following steps were applied to answer the research questions:

1.  Data Acquisition

a.  Gathering  data  from  student  assignments  focused  on  creating

UML diagrams.

b.  Creating feedback (GenAI and teacher) to students and obtaining
their evaluation of the feedback using a five-point Likert scale.

b.  Post-hoc sentiment analysis of teacher and GenAI feedback.
c.  GenAI assessment of UML diagrams evaluation.

a.  Formulation  of  conclusions,  benefits,  and  limitations  of  GenAI

The forthcoming subsections describe the dataset structure, GenAI

3.1. Dataset

The  study  was  conducted  over  two  consecutive  academic  years
(2023/2024 and 2024/2025) at Constantine the Philosopher University
in Nitra, as part of the bachelor's course Introduction to Software Engi-
neering. The identical instructional content, assignments, and assessment
protocols  were  used  in  both  years,  allowing  data  to  be  combined  for
analysis. A total of 262 students participated in the experiment across
both years. Over a 13-week semester, students completed four primary
assignments, each targeting a distinct phase of software system design:
(1)  requirements  specification,  (2)  use  case  diagram  design,  (3)  class
diagram  development,  and  (4)  construction  of  the  activity  diagram.
Each  assignment  increased  in  complexity  and  abstraction.  All  sub-
missions were exported from Enterprise Architect in XMI/XML format.
This format allows for preserving semantics and relationships between
modelled elements of the diagram. It ensured consistent, structured, and
machine-readable input suitable for both human and AI evaluation. The
submission and grading process was managed via the university's LMS
(Moodle).  Students  were  divided  into  three  experimental  groups  to
evaluate the effectiveness of different feedback strategies:

• 72 students received feedback generated by GenAI (GPTo1),
• 39 students received feedback from a human instructor,
• 151 students did not receive any feedback (control group).

In addition to the four modeling tasks, students completed six auto-
graded  theoretical  quizzes  and  a  final  exam  consisting  of  three  UML
diagramming tasks (use case, class, and activity). Two human evaluators
independently graded these final diagrams and were also assessed by the
GenAI  model  using  multiple  prompt  configurations.  All  AI-generated
feedback  was  returned  in  Slovak.  This  structure  allowed  for  a  multi-
dimensional analysis of both performance and perception.

3.2. Methods

The  key  objective  of  the  study  was  to  explore  whether  LLMs  can
generate formative feedback on complex visual modeling tasks, specif-
ically  UML  diagrams,  in  a  manner  that  is  pedagogically  relevant  and

3.2.2. Feedback annotation

To  better  understand  the  communicative  role  of  feedback  in  the
instructional  process,  a  post-hoc  qualitative  annotation  process  was
conducted for each feedback, whether authored by a human instructor
or generated by GenAI. This process aimed to categorize each feedback
comment into functional pedagogical types, allowing us to evaluate not
only the correctness of feedback but also its intended instructional role
in  the  learning  process.  The  categorization  scheme  was  inspired  by
recent  pedagogical  literature  emphasizing  the  formative  and  dialogic
role  of  feedback  in  higher  education  (Boud  &  Dawson,  2023;  N.  E.
Winstone &  Boud, 2022). In addition to its corrective functions, feed-
back  is  increasingly  understood  as  a  means  of  promoting  reflection,
engagement, and design awareness, particularly in complex tasks such
as  visual  modeling  (N.  Winstone  &  Carless,  2019).  Drawing  from
established pedagogical frameworks, and in alignment with the cogni-
tive goals of the study, a four-category scheme, adapted to the context of
diagram modeling tasks, was adopted:

• (1)  Diagnostic  —  Feedback  that  identifies  errors,  incomplete  ele-
ments,  or  violations  of  formal  diagram  syntax  or  semantics.  This
includes both surface-level and conceptual mistakes, and it is foun-
dational for student awareness of misunderstandings.

• (2) Design-Oriented — Feedback that comments on modeling strat-
egy, structure, or design decisions without labeling them as errors.
For  example,  advice  on  class  cohesion,  diagram  readability,  or
abstraction.  This  feedback  supports  deeper  engagement  with
modeling principles.

• (3)  Motivational  —  Feedback  that  encourages  students  through

• (4) Metacognitive —  Feedback that prompts students to reflect on

dominant communicative intent. Annotation was performed manually
by one evaluator familiar with the course content and the educational

analytical  clarity  in  downstream  comparisons.  This  categorization
enabled a structured comparison of how feedback types varied across
assignments and feedback sources. Of particular interest was the degree
to which GenAI-generated comments exhibited higher-order pedagog-
ical characteristics, such as metacognitive prompting or design-oriented
guidance, even though the model was not explicitly instructed to adopt
these styles.

4. Results

The dataset obtained has offered various insights into the feedback
quality  analysis.  All  analyzed  data  variables  did  not  meet  the  re-
quirements for normal distribution (p < 0.05) and were analyzed using
non-parametric  tests.  The  results  section  is  organized  by  research

4.1. Student-perceived helpfulness and pedagogical value of feedback
(RQ1)

First, the students’ evaluation of feedback was examined, where the
students  assigned  each  feedback  score  using  a  five-point  Likert  scale
without the knowledge whether the feedback was produced by GenAI or
teacher.  For  each  assignment,  a  Mann-Whitney  U  test  was  used  to

determine  whether  students  rated  teacher-generated  and  GenAI-
generated feedback differently. The results showed that in the case of
S0U1  (requirements  specification)  and  S0U4  (activity  diagram),  the
students  did  not  find  any  significant  qualitative  differences  between
GenAI and the teacher (p > 0.05). On the other hand, statistically sig-
nificant  differences  were  identified  between  feedback  sources  in  the
assignment S0U2 (p < 0.01) and S0U3 (p < 0.05). A closer look at the
student scores of feedback showed that in the case of S0U2 (use case
diagram) and S0U3 (class diagram), they considered the teacher feed-
back of higher quality based on the mean score (S0U2 teacher = 4.59;
S0U2 GenAI = 4.14; S0U3 teacher = 4.46; S0U3 GenAI = 4.03). Across
all  assignments,  the  students  considered  both  feedback  sources  with
high scores (GenAI mean = 4.09; Teacher mean = 4.38), which shows
that they evaluated the feedback as beneficial regardless of the source.
The lowest scores were given only to GenAI-generated feedback. Using
the four feedback categories defined in RQ2 (diagnostic, design, meta-
cognitive, motivational), boxplots of the feedback categories distribu-
tions  based  on  student  evaluations  using  the  Likert  scale  for  each
assignment  were  created  to  confirm  the  observed  patterns  visually
(Fig. 1). Assignment S0U4 did not contain any metacognitive or moti-
vational feedback, as the activity diagram student solutions contained
multiple  errors.  Therefore,  the  teachers  focused  more  on  correcting
them,  and  the  feedback  was  diagnostic  and  design-oriented.  Interest-
ingly, GenAI was not prompted to target specific feedback category, and
as can be seen, it also focused only on diagnostic and design feedback in
this assignment.

To  examine  the  relationship  between  student  feedback  ratings,
feedback source (GenAI or teacher), and feedback category, a two-way
ANOVA  was  performed  for  each  assignment  (Table  1).  The  results
confirmed a significant main effect of feedback source in S0U1-S0U3 (p
< 0.05),  where  teacher  feedback  was  consistently  rated  higher  than
GenAI feedback. Additionally, the main effect of feedback category was
significant in all three assignments, indicating some feedback categories
were more favorably received. However, interaction effects (Source x
Category) were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that
the  difference  between  GenAI  and  teacher  feedback  ratings  was
consistent across feedback categories. In the case of the first assignment,
the  students  valued  the  highest  diagnostic  feedback  (mean  score  =
4.67), while in the case of S0U2 and S0U3, students gave the highest
scores to motivational feedback (mean score = 4.83; 4.89).

4.2. Spontaneous feedback characteristics and type distribution (RQ2)

The GenAI model was instructed to provide formative feedback, but
the prompt did not request any particular feedback type, tone, or cate-
gory; thus, category diversity reflects spontaneous output. In addition to
student perception, teacher-generated categorizations of feedback were
analyzed. The feedback was categorized into four categories (diagnostic,
design,  metacognitive,  and  motivational)  by  a  human  evaluator.  The
goal was to look at the capabilities of GenAI to generate various feed-
back styles without specifying the prompt or requirements. The teacher
and GenAI feedback were comprised mainly of diagnostic feedback type
(GenAI: 66%; teacher: 48%). The other categories were evenly distrib-
uted in the case of teacher feedback (design: 19%; metacognitive: 18%;
motivational: 15%). However, in the case of GenAI feedback, motiva-
tional category was the least frequent (4%) together with metacognitive
(10%).  GenAI  was  more  focused  on  producing  diagnostic  (66%)  and
design (20%) feedback categories. The Chi-square test of independence
showed that from the evaluator's perspective, both GenAI and teacher
feedback were classified similarly in terms of their category (p > 0.05)
despite  descriptive  differences  indicating  GenAI  skewed  more  toward
diagnostic feedback.

To assess the tone and character of feedback, a post-hoc sentiment
analysis was conducted (Fig. 2). Traditional models such as TextBlob or
VADER proved insufficient due to the use of formal Slovak and domain-
specific terminology. Therefore, a custom heuristic method was adop-
ted.  This  involved  mapping  frequent  phrases  to  sentiment  categories
using a curated Slovak lexicon of pedagogically relevant terms. Senti-
ment  was  categorized  as  positive  (e.g.,  “výborne”  (perfect),  “spr´avne
rieˇsenie”  (correct),  “kompletn´e  rieˇsenie”  (complete)),  neutral  (e.g.,
“zadanie bolo splnen´e” (assignment satisfied), “rieˇsenie je v poriadku”
(OK)),  or  negative  (e.g.,  “chýba”  (missing),  “nespr´avne”  (wrong),
“nedostatoˇcn´e”  (insufficient)). This enabled quantification of how the
feedback tone varied between AI and instructor sources and across as-
signments  of  increasing  difficulty.  The  goal  of  this  analysis  was  to
observe how feedback tone evolves across task types and sources. Fig. 2
illustrates  the  distribution  of  sentiment  in  feedback  generated  by  a
GenAI model and a human instructor across four student assignments of
increasing complexity. This analysis served two purposes (a) to detect
differences  in  affective  expressiveness  between  teacher  and  GenAI-
generated  feedback,  and  (b)  to  quantify  how  sentiment  profiles
evolved across assignments of increasing difficulty and abstraction.

GenAI  feedback  was  predominantly  positive  or  neutral,  with  very
limited critical sentiment in the first assignment focused on functional
requirements.  This  suggests  that  the  model  tended  to  affirm  student

teacher maintained a more balanced sentiment distribution even in this
lower-complexity  task,  incorporating  both  encouragement  and  light
critical cues. This pattern indicates early evidence of greater pedagog-
ical depth in human-generated feedback. A significant shift appears in
the second assignment, where students were tasked with designing use
case  diagrams.  GenAI  became  notably  more  critical,  with  negative
sentiment far outweighing neutral or positive tones. This change likely

offering  less  critique  despite  the  increased  cognitive  demands  of
modeling object-oriented systems. One interpretation is that the model
may  have  encountered  semantic  limitations  in  evaluating  inheritance
structures, associations, or cohesion principles from the diagram exports
(provided  in  XML  format).  In  contrast,  the  teacher  provided  more
negatively-coded feedback, pointing to detailed structural or conceptual
issues likely overlooked by the model. This disparity reinforces the idea
that the teacher was engaged in deeper evaluation aligned with peda-
gogical  goals,  whereas  GenAI's  assessments  may  have  become  more
superficial under rising semantic complexity.

negative sentiment in the final and most complex assignment, focused
on modeling system behavior through activity diagrams. This observa-

task  was  classified  as  diagnostic.  It  means  it  was  aimed  primarily  at
identifying errors and deficiencies (see Fig. 1). The sharp rise in critical

deviations from expected flow or misuses of UML constructs. However,

also  included  neutral  and  occasionally  positive  commentary.  This
divergence  suggests  that  while  GenAI  enforces  formalism  rigidly,  the

4.3. Influence of GenAI feedback on modelling skills and performance
(RQ3)

Subsequently,  the  impact  of  feedback  on  learning  outcomes  was
examined. Since the final exam consisted of three UML diagram design
tasks and the students had four assignments during the semester, score
gain was computed as the difference between the average assignment
scores and the final exam tasks. Spearman correlation showed no sig-
nificant relationship between student-rated feedback quality and score
gain (p > 0.05). Although feedback was perceived as beneficial, it did
not directly impact their final score. However, a statistically significant
difference in score gain was identified between feedback sources (p <
0.05). The students receiving teacher feedback gained on average 0.87
points  more  than those receiving  GenAI  feedback.  This  indicates  that
teacher  feedback,  although  sometimes  similarly  rated  to  GenAI,  was
more effective in supporting student learning.

Additionally,  students  completed  six  automatically  evaluated
quizzes  during  the  term,  focused  on  theoretical  knowledge.  Teacher
feedback  recipients  scored  significantly  higher  on  average  in  these
quizzes  (p  < 0.01)  than  students  with  GenAI  feedback  (Fig.  3).  This
suggests that students were not only engaged with the feedback but also
applied it to enhance their theoretical understanding during the term.

On the other hand, feedback source did not significantly influence (p
> 0.05) final theoretical exam scores (Fig. 4a). This may be influenced
by  the  overall  highly  rated  quality  of  GenAI  feedback  or  students’

classification of the feedback into the four categories was to examine
whether GenAI can, without a specific prompt, generate not only diag-
nostic but also other variants of feedback. This was confirmed as GenAI
created 11% metacognitive and 5% motivational feedback. Specifically,
GenAI-generated diagnostic feedback significantly reduced score gains
by 0.47 points compared to teacher-generated diagnostic feedback (p <
0.05).

A  mixed  linear  regression  model  was  used  to  analyze  progression
across assignments (S0U1 - S0U4) and whether feedback source (GenAI
or  teacher)  influenced  learning  advancement.  The  results  (Fig.  5)
include  a  control  group  of  students  who  received  no  feedback.  The
experiment  was  done  during  two  terms,  where  on  the  first  year  the
students had the assignments only assessed using a score, and another
year, the  students also  obtained textual feedback  from the  teacher or
GenAI. The analysis (Fig. 5) confirmed that feedback had a significant
positive  effect  on  learning,  with  teacher  feedback  leading  to  greater
improvement than GenAI feedback. The first assignment (S0U1), which
focused on functional and non-functional requirements, had the highest
average score, while subsequent assignments required visual modeling
and  posed  greater  challenges.  Although  average  scores  declined  over
time, feedback helped mitigate this decline, particularly for the teacher
feedback group. The results suggest that while GenAI feedback supports
learning  to  some  extent,  it  is  less  effective  than  teacher  feedback.  A
hybrid approach, where the teacher would post-edit the GenAI feedback,
may help balance effectiveness with reduced workload and offer more
insights for students on their assignments in a more timely manner. On
the other hand, the results showed that GenAI models still lag behind
humans and cannot produce complex and beneficial formative feedback.
Nevertheless, any type of feedback improves the learning performance
and helps students better understand the abstract diagrammatic visual
tasks.  The  high  student  ratings  confirmed  the  beneficial  influence  of
feedback.

5. Discussion

This discussion interprets the findings in relation to the four research
questions, focusing on student perceptions of feedback, the pedagogical
characteristics  of  feedback,  learning  outcomes,  and  the  reliability  of
GenAI-supported  grading.  Rather  than  reiterating  empirical  results
presented in Section 4, the discussion aims to contextualize the observed
patterns within existing research on formative feedback, diagrammatic
modeling, and hybrid human–AI educational systems.

RQ1:  How  can  the  student-perceived  helpfulness  and  pedagogical
value of GenAI-generated feedback be compared to the instructor's
feedback across UML modeling tasks?

With  respect  to  RQ1,  the  findings  indicate  that  students  generally
perceived  both  teacher-generated  and  GenAI-generated  feedback  as
helpful, confirming that formative feedback is valued in complex UML
modeling tasks regardless of its source. However, statistically significant
differences  emerged  in  assignments  involving  higher  semantic  and
structural  complexity,  where  teacher  feedback  was  consistently  rated
higher.  This  suggests  that  while  GenAI  feedback  can  satisfy  baseline
expectations  for  usefulness,  its  perceived  value  decreases  as  tasks  de-
mand  deeper  interpretive  judgment,  design  rationale,  and  contextual
understanding.  The  absence  of  significant  differences  in  some  assign-
ments further indicates that task characteristics mediate how feedback
sources are perceived. In less abstract or more procedurally constrained
tasks, GenAI feedback may be experienced as equally valid, whereas in
conceptually  rich  modeling  tasks,  students  appear  more  sensitive  to
qualitative depth and pedagogical nuance.

RQ2:  What  types  of  feedback  does  GenAI  produce  spontaneously
without explicit prompt conditioning, and how does this distribution
compare to that of teacher-generated feedback?

Regarding  RQ2,  the  analysis  of  feedback  categories  revealed  that
both  GenAI  and  teacher  feedback  addressed  diagnostic  and  design-

​
J. Pecuchova et al.

Computers and Education: Arti(cid:999)cial Intelligence 10 (2026) 100574

oriented aspects of student work, while motivational and metacognitive
feedback  appeared  less  frequently  and  predominantly  in  teacher-
generated  responses.  The  perceived  value  of  feedback  categories  var-
ied  across  assignments,  with  diagnostic  feedback  most  valued  in
specification-focused tasks and motivational feedback becoming more
prominent in complex UML design assignments. These patterns suggest
that  students’  feedback  preferences  shift  alongside  increasing  task
complexity and cognitive demand. Importantly, the limited and unsys-
tematic  emergence  of  motivational  and  metacognitive  feedback  in
GenAI outputs highlights a key limitation of unprompted AI-generated
feedback. Without explicit instructional guidance embedded in prompt
design, GenAI tends to prioritize evaluative commentary over dialogic
or reflective engagement, reinforcing earlier concerns that automated
feedback  systems  may  struggle  to  support  higher-order  learning  pro-
cesses unless pedagogically framed.

RQ3: Does receiving GenAI feedback influence students' modelling
skills and improve their performance on subsequent modeling tasks
and  final  exams,  relative  to  students  receiving  teacher  or  no
feedback?

In  relation  to  RQ3,  the  results  demonstrate  that  feedback  source
significantly influenced learning outcomes in applied visual modeling
tasks. Students receiving teacher feedback showed greater score gains
across assignments, higher quiz performance, and stronger performance
in final exam modeling tasks compared to those receiving GenAI feed-
back.  Mediation  analysis  further  indicated  that  perceived  feedback
quality played a central role in explaining these differences, suggesting
that  how  students  interpret  and  trust  feedback  is  more  consequential
than  the  feedback  source  itself.  Notably,  GenAI-generated  diagnostic
feedback was associated with reduced score gains compared to teacher
diagnostic feedback. This pattern is consistent with the linguistic anal-
ysis, which showed that GenAI feedback exhibited higher repetition and
lower lexical diversity, while teacher feedback contained more specific
reformulations, examples, and task-oriented guidance—features that are
more likely to support actionable uptake and learning transfer. While
the magnitude of this effect may appear modest, its cumulative impact
becomes  relevant  in  scenarios  where  GenAI  feedback  is  deployed
intensively or substitutes human input. Longitudinal analysis confirmed
that both teacher and GenAI feedback supported learning progression
relative  to  no-feedback  conditions,  yet  teacher  feedback  consistently
produced  stronger  gains.  Together,  these  findings  suggest  that  GenAI
feedback,  in  its  current  form,  functions  more  effectively  as  a  supple-
mentary  support  rather  than  a  replacement  for  human  instructional
feedback in complex modeling contexts.

RQ4:  Can  GenAI  accurately  grade  visual  UML  diagrams  based  on
XML  representations  with  agreement  levels  comparable  to  expert
human graders, and how do different prompt strategies influence this
grading performance?

Finally,  addressing  RQ4,  the  findings  show  that  GenAI  achieved
substantial  to  almost  perfect  agreement  with  human  graders  when
evaluating  UML  diagrams,  particularly  under  optimized  prompting
conditions.  This  level  of  agreement  suggests  that  GenAI  can  reliably
approximate  human  grading  judgments  in  structured  modeling  tasks,
supporting its potential role in scalable assessment workflows. However,
agreement  varied  across  diagram  types  and  prompt  formulations,
underscoring the sensitivity of GenAI evaluation to input structure and
rubric  specificity.  Importantly,  high  grading  agreement  does  not
necessarily imply pedagogical effectiveness. While GenAI may perform
well as an evaluator, this does not guarantee that its feedback supports
conceptual  understanding  or  learning  progression,  reinforcing  the
distinction  between  evaluative  accuracy  and  instructional  value.
Although  prompt  design  was  not  treated  as  an  independent  experi-
mental  variable,  the  observed  variability  across  prompt  strategies

indicates  that  prompting  constitutes  a  critical  design  dimension  that
warrants  systematic  treatment  in  future  methodological  research.
Prompt  strategy  served  as  a  design  factor  in  this  study  which  condi-
tioned  grading  agreement  and  feedback  tone and  because  of  that  the
results are interpreted under these specific prompt conditions.

5.1. Research implications

The  following  implications  distinguish  between  findings  directly
supported  by  the  empirical  results  of  this  study  and  forward-looking
design  propositions  intended  to  inform  future  research  and  educa-
tional practice. This study suggests that, based on the observed patterns,
GenAI  has  the  potential  to  support  a  shift  toward  feedback  being
conceptualized from a scarce teacher resource into a scalable pedagog-
ical infrastructure, enabling more frequent and personalized formative
assessment.  However,  AI  feedback  could  be  embedded  in  hybrid
teacher-AI  models  to  maximize  learning,  where  educators  motivate
students,  add  nuance,  context,  and  critical  thinking  prompts  that  AI
alone cannot provide.

Although this study focused on UML diagramming in software en-
gineering,  the  findings  may  be  cautiously  transferable  to  other  disci-
plines that involve structured, rule-based, and semantically rich tasks.
However,  such  transferability  remains  hypothetical  and  requires
empirical validation in domain-specific contexts, where this paper may
serve as a starting point. The demonstrated strengths of GenAI feedback
and its potential to become a cognitive partner of teachers are directly
relevant to other study fields such as architecture, biology, business and
economics, computer science, and mathematics, where students create
formal representations that require iterative refinement. In this context,
GenAI  functions  as  a  cognitive  partner  in  the  sense  of  distributed
cognition, supporting diagnostic reasoning and iterative feedback cycles
while  pedagogical  judgment  and  accountability  remain  with  the
instructor, consistent with the framing of AI as a cognitive partner in
learning set-ups (Huang et al., 2022).” As in UML modeling, these tasks
can overwhelm teachers' capacity to provide detailed and timely feed-
back  at  scale,  making  them  potential  candidates  for  AI-supported
augmentation.  However,  if  the  observed  limitations  like  GenAI's  ten-
dency toward repetition, lack of nuance, and reduced effectiveness in
complex or abstract assignments are considered, these findings suggest
that  hybrid  AI–teacher  feedback  models  may  be  necessary  across  do-
mains rather than fully automated approaches. More broadly, the study
indicates a potential shift toward feedback-abundant learning environ-
ments  in  disciplines  grounded  in  structured  symbolic  or  visual  repre-
sentations. Such extrapolation should therefore be considered plausible
primarily for domains characterized by formal syntax, constrained so-
lution spaces, and evaluable visual or symbolic representations.

If GenAI can reliably provide formative feedback in visual modelling
tasks, curriculum design can move away from the traditional constraint
of  limited  teachers’  feedback  toward  models  that  are  feedback-rich,
iterative, and scalable. This may support instructional designs empha-
sizing  iterative  cycles  of  modelling,  feedback,  and  revision,  without
being limited by teacher capacity. Such approaches could enable more
personalized learning pathways and support the use of authentic, open-
ended assessments that would otherwise be difficult to sustain at scale.
Deploying GenAI feedback may also lead to creation of new layer of
learning  analytics  evidence  in  form  of  interaction  logs  that  contain
timing, frequency and type of feedback. Another evidence may present
recurring error patterns in diagram design structure that can timely alert
the educators to deal with these errors. When responsibly overseen, this
layer  can  support  instructors  to  detect  systematic  misunderstandings,
monitor  feedback  consistency  across  prompt  versions  and  evaluate
whether students act based on the obtained feedback. This may lead to
reframing GenAI not only as a feedback generator but also as a tool that
supports course improvement and scalable formative assessment.

Moreover, if GenAI feedback can be deployed at scale, assessment in
engineering  programs  could  gradually  support  a  reorientation  within

J. Pecuchova et al.

the  constraints  identified  in  this  study.  Traditionally,  assessments  in
engineering have been constrained by the limited capacity of teachers to
provide timely, formative feedback for complex assignments or projects,
resulting  in  heavy reliance  on summative  exams  to validate  learning.
Implementing GenAI feedback, even with some limitations identified in
this study, allows students to receive iterative guidance throughout the
design and problem-solving process. This may support a gradual shift
toward  competency-based  and  mastery-oriented  assessment  models,
where  progression  depends  on  demonstrated  skill  development  over

stable  and  robust  across  different  contexts.  Based  on  the  results,  the
structure and phrasing of prompts can substantially influence both the
tone and accuracy of GenAI feedback. Although three prompt versions
were tested for each diagram type to simulate diverse evaluative per-
spectives, it was observed that the model's grading behavior and style of
response  varied  depending  on  the  formulation  and  specificity  of  the
prompt. This highlights that prompt engineering should be treated as an
integral component of educational design rather than a purely technical
concern.  Prompts  must  be  continually  validated  to  ensure  reliability
across variations in input quality, student background, and assignment
type. Without such validation, the risk of inconsistent or biased evalu-
ation  increases,  potentially  undermining  trust  in  GenAI-supported
feedback systems.

Simultaneously, agreement is also expressed with other researchers
(Correia et al., 2017; Sandu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024) that study
programs  should  adopt  authentic  assessments  that  mirror  real-world
practice,  since  the  burden  of  detailed  feedback  can  be  divided  be-
tween the teachers and their potential cognitive partner (Er et al., 2025).
However,  this  shift  requires  careful  consideration  of  the  results  of
studies similar to this study and understanding the limitations of GenAI
feedback  in  particular  settings  and  environments.  In  this  sense,
AI-driven feedback may support a transition from episodic evaluation
toward  more  continuous  learning  and  skill  refinement,  rather  than
replacing established assessment practices outright.

Considering  the  pedagogical  models  and  learning  theories,  the
integration of AI-generated feedback has the potential to both foster and
undermine  models of self-regulated learning (SRL). On one hand, the
immediacy  and  abundance  of  feedback  can  promote  SRL  by  giving
students  the  resources  to  monitor  their  progress,  set  goals,  and  itera-
tively refine their work without being constrained by instructor avail-
ability.  In  this  role,  AI  may  function  as  a  scaffold  for  metacognitive
monitoring  and  strategic  planning.  However,  considering  the  imper-
fections  and  limitations  of  the  GenAI  feedback  confirmed  also  in  this
study, an over-reliance on AI feedback may risk eroding students' au-
tonomy and reflective capacity if students passively accept suggestions
without critically evaluating their validity or integrating them into their
reasoning processes. This dependency could weaken the students’ abil-
ity to interpret, question, and act on feedback independently. To align
AI-supported feedback with SRL models, curricula should incorporate
structured opportunities for reflection and comparison between AI and
teacher feedback.

The outcomes of this research can carry significant implications for
multiple stakeholders in higher education. From the teachers’ perspec-
tive,  hybrid  teacher–AI  models  may  help  reduce  repetitive  diagnostic
feedback  while  preserving  human  contributions  in  areas  requiring
judgment, motivation, and contextualization. This redistribution of roles
may allow instructors to manage larger and more diverse cohorts while
maintaining instructional quality. Students may benefit from immediate
and  iterative  feedback  that  supports  autonomy,  yet  they  must  also
develop skills to critically interpret and evaluate AI-generated feedback.
At the institutional level, GenAI feedback systems represent a stra-
tegic opportunity to scale high-quality learning support while reducing
faculty  workload.  At  the  same  time,  they  raise  challenges  related  to

ethics, equity, governance, and accountability. For example, inconsis-
tent interpretation of non-canonical yet conceptually valid UML struc-
tures by GenAI systems may disproportionately disadvantage students
whose  modeling  approaches  diverge  from  standardized  patterns.
Another  challenge  is  different  prompt  formulation  by  students  or
teachers,  where  not  only  differences  may  be  caused  by  different  lan-
guage input or various phrasing. Building feedback and GenAI literacy of
all stakeholders should become a central component of learning in an AI-
augmented educational system. University leaders must therefore take a
proactive  role  in  developing  policies  and  investing  in  hybrid  in-
frastructures. However, firstly, they need to ensure the development of a
new  set  of  AI-related  professional  skills  among  faculty  staff.  Subse-
quently, together with systematic monitoring and continual transfer of
the  results  of  contemporary  educational  research,  the  academic  in-
stitutions can successfully adapt to the new reality. These implications
should  be  interpreted  in  light  of  the  study's  scope,  which  focuses  on
formative feedback in UML modeling, and should not be read as pre-
scriptive  claims  for  universal  adoption  without  further  empirical
validation.

5.2. Research limitations

While this study provides valuable empirical evidence on the role of
GenAI in formative feedback for UML modelling, its several limitations
should be acknowledged. The research was conducted within a single
institutional and disciplinary context (software engineering at one uni-
versity), which may limit the generalizability of findings to other do-
mains or educational settings. A quasi-experimental design was applied,
which is pragmatic, but relied on naturally occurring groups of unequal
size (GenAI feedback n = 72, teacher feedback n = 39, without feedback
n = 151). This decision may lead to group imbalance and uncontrolled
confounding  factors.  The  annotation  of  feedback  categories  was  per-
formed by a single human grader, raising concerns about subjectivity
even though GenAI was not prompted to create specific oriented feed-
back. Although the study relied on a single generative model (GPTo1),
which  limits  the  generalizability  of  the  findings  to  other  LLMs  with
different  architectures,  training  data,  or  fine-tuning  strategies,  this
model  is  one  of  the  most  used  LLMs.  Integration  of  multiple  GenAI
models  would  require  more  resources  and  assignments  and  could  be
considered as further step in future research. In addition, several limi-
tations are associated with the design and application of the prompts
used for GenAI evaluation. While three versions of prompts were created
to  capture  different  grading  perspectives,  they  were  developed  by  a
single  teacher  and  thus  may  reflect  implicit  assumptions  about  peda-
gogical  focus  and  evaluative  priorities.  Given  the  well-documented
sensitivity  of  LLMs  to  prompt  formulation,  variations  in  phrasing,
input structure, and rubric specificity could have influenced the model's
responses. Moreover, although the model generated feedback in Slovak,
the prompts themselves were written in English, potentially introducing
minor  interpretive  mismatches.  Importantly,  while  the  prompts  were
designed  to  encourage  thoughtful,  formative  responses,  they  did  not
include  explicit  directives  to  generate  motivational  or  metacognitive
feedback. As a result, the emergence of such feedback types was organic
rather than intentional. This limits the ability to fully assess the model's
capacity to produce higher-order pedagogical guidance. Finally, due to
ethical and publication constraints, full versions of the original student
tasks  or  prompt  inputs  could  not  be  included  in  the  paper,  instead,
representative  samples  were  provided  to  illustrate  the  structure  and
intent  of  the  prompt  strategy.  Despite  these  constraints,  the  prompt
design  enabled  a  structured  yet  flexible  evaluation  of  AI-generated
feedback under conditions that closely resemble authentic educational
use. The resulting analysis offers meaningful insight into how prompting
strategies influence the quality and character of AI-generated formative
assessment.

6. Conclusion

This conclusion focuses on synthesizing empirically supported find-
ings, acknowledged limitations, and clearly defined directions for future
research.  Presented  study  examined  the  usefulness  of  GenAI  for
providing  formative  feedback  on  UML  diagramming  tasks  in  higher
education.  The  findings  demonstrate  that  students  perceived  GenAI
feedback as beneficial and, in some cases, comparable to teacher com-
ments. However, teacher feedback proved more effective in improving
performance on complex modeling tasks, quiz outcomes, and final exam
results. Linguistic and sentiment analyses further revealed that GenAI
feedback,  while  scalable  and  consistent,  lacked  the  richness,  nuance,
and  adaptability  of  human-provided  guidance.  Nevertheless,  even
GenAI feedback offered improvement to performance in comparison to
students  who  did  not  receive  any  feedback.  Notably,  the  study  also
showed  that  GenAI  can  achieve  substantial  agreement  with  human
graders in assessing UML diagrams, particularly when prompt design is
optimized, highlighting its potential as a scalable evaluation tool and as
a valuable cognitive partner in education.

However,  considering  the  limitations  of  the  study,  caution  is
required before generalizing the findings. Addressing these limitations
in future research will be essential to understand the educational value
and risks of GenAI-driven feedback fully.

This  study  offers  a  contribution  to  the  evidence  that  GenAI  is
emerging not merely as a supplementary tool but may potentially serve
as an infrastructural and essential element of future higher education
systems,  capable  of  transforming  how  feedback,  assessment,  and
learning  are  organized.  The  challenge  ahead  lies  in  designing  hybrid
teacher-AI  ecosystems  that  combine  the  scalability  of  AI  with  the
pedagogical  depth  of  human  expertise.  This  work  contributes  to  an
emerging  vision  of  new  systems  of  learning,  where  AI  reshapes  the
feedback  landscape  from  a  teacher-limited  resource  into  a  scalable
infrastructural capability that can inform the design of hybrid feedback
systems in higher education.
