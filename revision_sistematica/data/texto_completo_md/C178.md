Artificial intelligence in higher education: The interplay of competence,
perception, and artificial intelligence influence on self-learning outcomes

The  quick  adoption  of  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  in  higher  education  has  changed  how  students  learn  and
cultivate their capacity for self-directed learning. Although previous research has looked at the adoption of AI,
few have investigated the ways in which perception, competency, and AI's behavioural effect, all work together
to shape self-learning outcomes in emerging nations. These dynamics were examined in this study among 509
students from two of Ghana's largest universities of teacher education with distance education programmes. The
study used a cross-sectional survey approach, and Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling to evaluate
the data. The results revealed three key insights. First, competence in using generative AI particularly skills in
prompt  creation,  error  detection,  and  ethical  awareness  were  significant  but  modestly  predicted  self-learning
outcomes. Second, perception of AI's usefulness and ease of use showed no significant direct effect, suggesting
that positive attitudes alone were insufficient to generate academic benefits. Collectively, the model explained
57.4% of the variance in self-learning outcomes and demonstrated strong predictive relevance. Practically, the
study underscored the need for higher education institutions to embed AI literacy into curricula for improved
learning outcomes.

1. Introduction

The  integration  of  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  in  higher  education
transformed  how  students  engaged  with  knowledge,  instruction,  and
learning  environments  (Bhatia  et  al.,  2024;  Walter,  2024).  Beyond
automating administrative functions, generative AI tools such as adap-
tive  learning  systems,  intelligent  tutoring  platforms,  and  generative
models  were  increasingly  employed  to  deliver  personalized  feedback,
tailor  content  to  learners'  needs,  and  support  self-directed  learning
(Kasneci et al., 2023; Kyambade et al., 2025). These tools were partic-
ularly significant in higher education, where competence in self-learning
encompassing autonomy, cognitive ability and digital literacy strongly
influence  academic  success  and  lifelong  learning  (Al-Hail,  2025;
Rodríguez et al., 2025). Recent research indicated that the effectiveness
of AI in education depended not only on what the technology could do,
but  also  on  students'  competence  in  navigating  these  tools  and  their
perceptions  of  the  technology  (Hidayat-ur-Rehman,  2024;  Mohebi,
2025; Sanusi et al., 2022). For instance, a large-scale European study

found that AI competence, including technical knowledge and ethical
awareness,  was  a  significant  predictor  of  students'  ability  to  use  AI
constructively in their studies (Al-Zahrani & Alasmari, 2024). Similarly,
a recent study in Ghana demonstrated that graduate students’  compe-
tence with generative AI determined the extent to which they were able
to  integrate the  technology  into academic  work  (Owusu  Tabiri et  al.,
2025). Perception, on the other hand, played a key role in adoption as
students with positive attitudes toward AI were more likely to explore its
potential,  while  those  expressing  skepticism  often  limited  their
engagement with the required tools (V´azquez-Parra et al., 2024).

It could also be argued that AI itself may exert a direct influence on
self-learning  outcomes  (Alqurni,  2026;  Boubker,  2024).  By  offering
real-time analytics, predictive insights, and adaptive feedback, AI sys-
tems  scaffold  independent  learning  behaviours  and  enhanced  perfor-
mance (Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Evidence from a higher
education institution in Kenya further showed that generative AI tools
increased  learner  motivation  and  fostered  autonomy,  but  concerns
about plagiarism, misinformation, and academic integrity among users

comes refer to the academic and cognitive gains that result from this
process, including improved understanding, critical thinking, problem-

2025; Saleh, 2025; Tram et al., 2024). While prior studies had investi-

perception, and AI influence collectively shaped self-learning outcomes
among  university  students  as  a  way  of  offering  useful  insights  for

research gap, the study aims to examine how competence, perception,

education. Specifically, the study sought to; (1) determine the effect of
competence  on  self-learning  outcomes;  (2)  examine  the  influence  of
perception  on  self-learning  outcomes;  and  (3)  assess  the  effect  of  AI
influence on self-learning outcomes. Through these objectives, the study
would provide an understanding of how these factors interact to influ-
ence self-learning outcomes in AI-supported learning environments.

2. Theoretical framework

The  self-determination  theory  (SDT)  and  self-regulated  learning
(SRL) theory were used to explain how competence, perception, and AI
influence  shaped  self-learning  outcomes  as  these  theories  provided
complementary  perspectives.  While  SDT  explained  the  essence  of
motivation  and  SRL  looked  at  how  learners  regulate  their  learning
processes.  Self-determination  theory,  developed  by  Deci  and  Ryan
(2012),  posits  that  human  motivation  is  driven  by  the  satisfaction  of
three  basic  psychological  needs:  autonomy,  competence,  and  related-
ness.  In  the  context  of  AI  supported  learning,  these  dimensions  help
explain how students engage with digital learning environments.

The  first  dimension,  autonomy,  refers  to  learners'  ability  to  take
control  of  their  own  learning.  Generative  AI  tools  such  as  adaptive
learning  systems  and  generative  platforms  enable  students  to  decide
what, how, and when to learn, thereby supporting independent learning
and  strengthening  self-learning  outcomes  (Yambal  &  Waykar,  2025).
This  flexibility  is  particularly  important  in  higher  education,  where
self-directed learning is essential. The second dimension, competence,
reflects  learners' perception of their ability to effectively use learning
tools and achieve desired outcomes. AI systems support competence by
providing personalized feedback, automated assessments, and targeted
recommendations  that  enhance  learners’  sense  of  mastery  (Jegede,
2024; Vashishth et al., 2024). The third dimension, relatedness, refers
to  the  sense  of  connection  learners  have  with  others  in  the  learning
environment.  AI  enabled  platforms  can  facilitate  collaboration  and
interaction by connecting students with peers, instructors, and learning
communities (Obaje, 2025). Together, these three dimensions explain
how motivation is shaped in AI-supported learning contexts.

Self-regulated  learning  theory,  developed  by  Zimmerman  (2013),
emphasized  learners’  ability  to  plan,  monitor,  and  evaluate  their
learning  activities.  In  AI-supported  environments,  these  processes  are
critical. During the planning phase, students may use generative AI tools
to set up goals and structure their learning activities (Banihashem et al.,
2025;  Mazari,  2025).  In  the  monitoring  phase,  AI  systems  provide
real-time feedback that help learners track progress and adjust strategies

(Radovi´c & Seidel, 2025; Zhang & Chen, 2026). The evaluation phase

feedbacks (Mohanraj, 2024; Oates & Johnson, 2025). Without sufficient

weaken  the  self-regulation  process  (Henry,  2024).  The  integration  of
SDT  and  SRL  theories  provide  a  comprehensive  explanation  of
self-learning outcomes. Together, these perspectives threw some high-
lights on the fact that self-learning outcomes are shaped by both moti-
vational and self-regulatory mechanisms and also formed the theoretical
foundation for the conduct of the current study.

2.1. Model development and theoretical alignment

The  theory-to-model  connection,  SDT  and  SRL  were  used  as
explanatory lenses rather than as a requirement that every theoretical
dimension  should  appear  as  a  separate  latent  variable.  The  SDT  di-
mensions of autonomy and relatedness informed the interpretation of AI
influence, while competence informed the competence construct. The
SRL  processes  of  planning,  monitoring,  and  evaluation  informed  the
operational definition of competence and self-learning outcomes. These
theoretical dimensions therefore shaped construct definition and item
development, rather than being modelled as additional paths. This de-
cision also kept the model aligned with the stated objectives and direct
hypotheses.

2.2. Literature review and hypotheses development

2.2.1. Competence and self-learning outcomes

Competence  is  a  fundamental  factor  in  technology-enhanced
learning,  particularly  in  the  context  of  AI  use.  In  this  study,  compe-
tence refers to students’ digital literacy, AI literacy, and their ability to
engage in self-regulated learning processes (Chiu, 2025). These elements
are  treated  as  an  integrated  capability  rather  than  isolated  skills.
Effective  engagement  with  generative  AI  tools  requires  students  to
simultaneously  navigate  digital  platforms,  critically
interpret
AI-generated outputs, and regulate their own learning activities (Kotsis,
2025; Mohebbi, 2025; Yadav, 2024). This integrated perspective reflects
the  reality  of  AI-supported  learning,  where  technical,  cognitive,  and
self-regulatory skills are closely intertwined (Güner &  Er, 2025). In a
study conducted by Yeh et al. (2019), it was found that self-regulated
learning  strategies  such  as  goal  setting,  time  management,  and
self-monitoring  were  strong  predictors  of  academic  success  in  online
learning  environments.  Similarly,  recent  studies  have  shown  that
competence in using generative AI tools enabled students to effectively
integrate  such  tools  into  their  academic  work,  thereby  improving
learning  outcomes  (Al-Zahrani  &  Alasmari,  2024;  Chen  et  al.,  2020;
Owusu  Tabiri  et  al.,  2025;  Wan  et  al.,  2025).  In  addition,  studies  by
Yusuf et al. (2024), El Samaty (2025) and Nasr et al. (2025) revealed
that competence allowed students to engage critically with AI-generated
outputs rather than relying on them passively. The implication is that
students with higher levels of competence are more likely to evaluate,
adapt, and apply AI-generated information in meaningful ways, which
enhances their self-learning outcomes (Khalil et al., 2024; Zhou et al.,
2025).  Beyond  technical  ability,  competence  also  increases  the  confi-
dence of a student in navigating digital learning environments (Deng &
Liu, 2025). Learners who feel capable are more likely to experiment with
new  tools,  persist  through  challenges,  and  take  ownership  of  their
learning  process  (Chen  et  al.,  2020;  Deng  &  Liu,  2025).  Thus,  in
AI-supported settings, such adaptability becomes especially important,
as students must continuously interpret and refine AI-generated outputs
(Khlaif et al., 2025; Oubibi, 2025; Wiemer, 2019). Based on foregoing
discussion the study hypothesized that.

2.2.2. H1: Competence positively influences self-learning outcomes

2.2.2.1. Perception  and  self-learning  outcomes. Perception  plays  a  sig-
nificant role in shaping students' engagement with learning tools. Ac-
cording to Kashive et al. (2020) perception may include evaluation of
the usefulness, ease of use, and trustworthiness of AI technologies by a
user.  This  construct  reflects  an  evaluative  orientation  rather  than  a
motivational  state,  focusing  on  students'  assessment  of  generative  AI
tools  in  terms  of  usefulness,  ease  of  use,  and  trustworthiness  within
digital learning environments (Qambar &  Samo, 2026). Students' per-
ceptions influence their willingness to explore and adopt generative AI
tools in their learning processes (Zhang, 2025; Seraj & Sundram, 2025).
Empirical  studies  suggest  that  students  with  positive  perceptions  of
artificial  intelligence  are  more  likely  to  engage  with  these  tools.  For
instance,  V´azquez-Parra  et  al.  (2024) and  Mohebi  (2025) found  that
students who perceived AI as useful and easy to use are more likely to
integrate  it  into  their  academic  activities.  Similarly,  Shi  and  Zhang
(2025) observed  that  perceptions  shaped  by  trust,  transparency,  and
institutional support influenced students' adoption of AI in educational
settings. However, existing research also indicates that perception alone
may  not  be  sufficient  to  produce  meaningful  learning  outcomes
(Alwaqdani,  2025;  Rafiq-uz-Zaman  &  Nadeem,  2025).  While  positive
perceptions  encourage  engagement,  they  do  not  necessarily  lead  to
improved  academic  performance  unless  accompanied  by  active  and
effective  use  of  the  technology  (M´a˜nez  et  al.,  2025;  Uppal  &  Hajian,
2025).  This  suggests  that  perception  serves  as  an  enabling  condition
that  facilitates  engagement  but  does  not  independently  guarantee
improved outcomes. Furthermore, students’ perceptions are shaped by
prior experiences, peer influence, and institutional context (Uras Eren &
Atay, 2025). In support of this assertion, Alshayban (2026) and Yeung
et  al.  (2026) also  found  that  learners  who  have  had  positive  in-
teractions with generative AI tools are more likely to trust and continue
using them, while others who had concerns about its accuracy or over-
dependence might influence their willingness to engage fully with these
tools. These mixed perceptions highlighted the complexity of this phe-
nomenon  and  reinforced  the  importance  of  examining  perception
alongside  self-learning  outcomes.  Based  on  this  argument,  the  study
formulated and tested a hypothesis as.

3. Methods

This study adopted a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to
examine  how  competence,  perception,  and  AI  influence  shaped  stu-
dents’ self-learning outcomes in a higher education setting. The target
population comprised of 3588 undergraduate and postgraduate students
enrolled  in  two  large  public  universities  that  primarily  focused  on
teacher  education  and  were  on  distance  learning  programmes.  These
institutions offered a broad mix of disciplines, including education, so-
cial sciences, business, and selected STEM-related programmes. These
institutions were selected because they represented key hubs for training
students to use pedagogical innovation through an integration of tech-
nology into teaching and learning within flexible education systems. A
simple  random  sampling  technique  was  employed  to  ensure  that  all
students  had  an  equal  chance  of  selection.  From  target  population  a
sample  size  of  600  students  were  used  across  selected  study  centres.
However, a total of 509 valid responses were retained after screening for
missing  values  and  inconsistencies.  This  sample  size  is  considered
adequate  for  Partial  Least  Squares  Structural  Equation  Modeling,  as
prior  methodological  studies  indicate  that  samples  above  300  were
sufficient for complex models, while samples exceeding 500 provided
stronger  statistical  power  and  more  stable  parameter  estimates
(Chomeya  et  al.,  2024;  Demir  &  Us¸ak,  2025).  Comparable  studies  in
artificial intelligence and digital learning in higher education have also
relied on similar sample sizes, further supporting the adequacy of the
present study (Bailey, 2025; Tran, 2025). Data were collected using a
combination of online and paper-based survey methods to accommodate
the diverse accessibility levels of distance education students. The online
questionnaire  was  distributed  through  institutional  learning  manage-
ment systems and student communication platforms.

The  paper-based  survey  was  not  administered  by  post  but  was
instead  distributed  during  scheduled  face-to-face  tutorial  sessions  at
designated study centres, where distance learners periodically met for
academic support. This approach ensured that students with limited or
inconsistent  internet  access  were  not  excluded.  Out  of  the  total  re-
sponses, 109 were collected through the online method, while 400 were
obtained through the paper-based approach. The survey instrument was
organized into five sections. Section A captured demographic informa-
tion, including gender, university affiliation, programme of study, and
the  specific  artificial  intelligence  platforms  used  by  students  for  self-
learning.  The  inclusion  of  artificial  intelligence  tools  was  intentional,
as it provided direct insight into students’  engagement with technolo-
gies such as generative AI systems, which could not be inferred from
demographic characteristics alone. Age was not included in the instru-
ment as the settings used for current study have population of compa-
rable ages. Sections B to E measured the main constructs of the study,
namely  perception,  competence,  AI  influence  and  self-learning  out-
comes. All items were measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from
strongly disagree to strongly agree.

3.1. Variables and measurement

The study focused on four main constructs derived from the research
hypotheses:  perception,  competence,  ai  influence,  and  self-learning
outcomes.  These  constructs  were  measured  using  a  structured  ques-
tionnaire administered to students. Perception was measured using ten
items  adapted  from  prior  studies  on  digital  learning  and  educational
technology (Kanont et al., 2024). The items were designed to capture
how  students  view  artificial  intelligence  tools  in  terms  of  usefulness,
ease  of  use,  and  trustworthiness  in  supporting  their  learning.  In  this
study, perception reflects how students judge the value of generative AI
tools and the extent to which they believe these tools can support their
self-directed learning.

Competence  was  measured  using  ten  items  adapted  from  prior
research  on  digital  literacy,  artificial  intelligence  literacy,  and  self-
regulated learning (Khalil et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). These items
assessed students' ability to use generative AI tools effectively, interpret
the  outputs  they  generate,  and  manage  their  own  learning  processes.
Competence  is  therefore  treated  as  a  combination  of  technical  skills,
critical  thinking,  and  the  ability  to  plan,  monitor,  and  evaluate  one's
learning.

AI influence was measured using five items developed based on self-
determination  theory  and  existing  studies  on  AI-supported  learning
(Chan  et  al.,  2025;  Chiu  et  al.,  2024;  Nur  et  al.,  2024;  Shi  &  Zhang,
2025). The items  focused on how generative AI tools shape  students’
learning  behaviours,  particularly  in  terms  of  autonomy,  motivation,
efficiency, and active engagement. This construct reflects the extent to
which  students  feel  encouraged  by  AI  to  take  responsibility  for  their
learning and engage more actively in the learning process.

Self-learning outcomes were measured using five items adapted from
prior  studies  on  self-regulated  learning  and  academic  development
(Ogalo & Mtenzi, 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Yeh et al.,
2019).  These  items  captured  the  learning  gains  students  experience
when studying independently, including improvements in understand-
ing,  critical  thinking,  problem-solving,  and  overall  learning  effective-
ness. In this study, self-learning outcomes represent the extent to which
students are able to achieve meaningful progress through their own ef-
forts, supported by generative AI tools.

3.2. Data analysis approach

Data analysis was conducted using Partial Least Squares Structural

Equation Modeling, a variance-based technique suitable for predictive
research involving latent constructs and complex models. This approach
was appropriate for studies examining emerging phenomena such as AI
use in education because it emphasized prediction and explained vari-
ance while remaining robust to non-normal data (Cepeda et al., 2024;
Chinnaraju  et  al.,  2025).  The  measurement  model  in  this  study  was
specified as reflective, as all indicators were assumed to represent their
underlying latent constructs. This approach aligns with contemporary
methodological  recommendations  advocating  reflective  measurement
for  theory-driven  constructs  in  management  and  social  sciences
(Yingsoon & Rahman, 2025; Mtotywa & Mohapeloa, 2023).

The analysis followed a two-stage procedure consisting of measure-
ment model assessment and structural model evaluation, consistent with
established guidelines (Hair et al., 2021; Sarstedt et al., 2022). In the
first stage, the measurement model was evaluated using indicator reli-
ability,  internal  consistency  reliability,  convergent  validity  and
discriminant  validity.  Indicator  reliability  was  assessed  using  outer
loadings.  Loadings  of  0.70  and  above  were  considered  acceptable
(Fasanmi,  2025;  Kostanek  et  al.,  2024).  Indicators  with  loadings  be-
tween 0.40 and 0.70 were evaluated and retained only if their removal
did  not  improve  composite  reliability  or  average  variance  extracted,
while indicators with loadings below 0.40 were removed as they do not
adequately contribute to the construct (Hair et al., 2021).

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha
and  composite  reliability,  with  threshold  values  of  0.70  and  above
indicating  acceptable  reliability,  while  values  between  0.60  and  0.70
were considered acceptable in exploratory research (Hair et al., 2021;
Sarstedt et al., 2022). Convergent validity was evaluated using average
variance extracted, with a threshold of 0.50 indicating that the construct
explains at least half of the variance of its indicators. Discriminant val-
idity was assessed primarily using the Heterotrait Monotrait ratio, with
values below 0.85 indicating adequate discriminant validity. The For-
nell Larcker criterion was also examined as a supplementary assessment,
where  the  square  root  of  the  average  variance  extracted  for  each
construct was expected to exceed its correlations with other constructs
(Hair et al., 2021; Henseler et al., 2015).

In the second stage, the structural model was evaluated to test the
hypothesized  relationships  among  competence,  perception,  artificial
intelligence  influence,  and  self-learning  outcomes.  Collinearity  was
assessed using variance inflation factor values, with values below 3.0
indicating  no  multicollinearity  concerns  (Hair  et  al.,  2021;  Sarstedt
et al., 2022). The explanatory power of the model was evaluated using
the coefficient of determination, where values of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75
represent weak, moderate, and substantial levels of explanatory power

4.4. Structural model assessment

After  confirming  the  adequacy  of  the  measurement  model,  the
structural model was evaluated to examine the hypothesized relation-
ships  among  the  constructs.  Table  6 presented  the  assessment  of  the
structural  model,  including  path  coefficients,  means,  standard  de-
viations, t-values, p-values, effect size and confidence interval for the
hypothesized  relationships.  The  results  provided  evidence  of  the
strength,  significance,  and  multicollinearity  of  the  paths  within  the
model.

The assessment of the structural model highlighted the relationships
between competence (C), perceptions (P), and AI influence (I) on self-
learning  outcomes  (O),  as  presented  in  Table  6.  The  results  revealed
varying  levels  of  predictive  power  and  significance  across  the  three
hypothesized paths.

The path from competence to self-learning outcomes was statistically
significant (β = 0.278, t = 4.984, p = 0.000), indicating that learners’
technical abilities in navigating, prompting, and critically engaging with
generative AI tools positively contributed to self-learning outcomes. The
effect  size  (f2  = 0.091)  was  small  but  meaningful,  suggesting  that
competence  served  as  a  foundational  enabler  for  effective  AI-assisted
learning.  Recent studies confirmed that digital and  AI-related compe-
tence allowed students to integrate AI outputs meaningfully into their
academic  practices,  enhancing  efficiency and  knowledge construction
(Chee  et  al.,  2025).  This  relationship  underscored  the  importance  of
prioritizing  competence-building  towards  the  usage  of  generative  AI
tools in higher education curricula.

The direct effect of perceptions on self-learning outcomes was weak
and non-significant (β  = 0.050, t = 1.064, p = 0.287). The effect size
(f2  = 0.003) was negligible, indicating that learners’  attitudes toward
generative  AI  contributed  very  little  to  self-learning  outcomes.  While
learners might have held positive perceptions, these alone were insuf-
ficient  to  drive  learning  improvements.  Instead,  perceptions  likely
influenced outcomes indirectly through behaviors such as motivation or
engagement (Al-Dokhny et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025). Thus, favorable
perceptions  appeared  to  be  necessary  but  insufficient  conditions  for
improving learning outcomes.

The most substantial and statistically significant path was from AI
influence to self-learning outcomes (β = 0.526, t = 10.282, p = 0.000).
The  effect  size  was  large  (f2  = 0.404),  indicating  that  behavioural
engagement  with  AI,  such  as  promoting  learner  responsibility,  moti-
vating  active  engagement,  and  inspiring  exploration,  directly  drove
improvements in self-learning outcomes. Empirical evidence confirmed
that generative AI fostered self-directed learning, critical thinking, and
exploration  of  new  perspectives  when  actively  integrated  into  study
practices (Jayasinghe, 2025; Li et al., 2024). This result highlighted that
AI  influence  was  the  most  powerful  determinant  of  self-learning  out-
comes,  surpassing  the  contributions  of  both  competence  and

perceptions.

The  confidence  interval  (CI)  analysis  supported  these  results.  For
competence on self-learning outcomes, the 95% CI ranged from 0.173 to
0.390, indicating a consistently positive and statistically significant ef-
fect. For perceptions, the 95% CI ranged from (cid:0) 0.037 to 0.147, crossing
zero and confirming the non-significant effect. For AI Influence, the 95%
CI  ranged  from  0.420  to  0.621,  reflecting  a  highly  stable  and  robust
positive effect.

Overall,  the  findings  indicated  that  competence  and  AI  influence
were  statistically  reliable  predictors  of  self-learning  outcomes,  while
perceptions  were  not.  Competence  acted  as  a  foundational  enabler,
providing students with the skills to leverage on AI effectively, with a
small  but  meaningful  effect  size  (Yusuf  et  al.,  2024;  Zaman,  2024).
Perceptions  shaped  attitudes  but  had  a  negligible  effect  size  and
required translation into actionable engagement (Prentice et al., 2023;
Kruk &  Kału˙zna, 2025). AI influence, however, served as the primary
driver of learning outcomes, with a large effect size, demonstrating that
active  behavioral  interaction  with  generative  AI  tools  was  central  to
achieving  meaningful  academic  results  (Wu  et  al.,  2024;  Kim  et  al.,
2025; Perla &  Vinci, 2023). These findings emphasized that pedagog-
ical designs should move beyond technical skill training and attitudinal
promotion, focusing on integrating AI into authentic learning contexts to
maximize  its  transformative  potential.  Fig.  3 presented  the  PLS

4.5. Predictive relevance (Q2predict)

Table 7 presented the predictive relevance (Q2predict) of the struc-
tural model. The results indicated the ability of the model to accurately
predict  endogenous  constructs,  demonstrating  its  practical  relevance
and robustness in explaining self-learning outcomes.

The  predictive  relevance  of  the  structural  model  for  self-learning
outcome was evaluated using Q2predict, as shown in Table 7. The re-
sults indicated a Q2predict value of 0.563, demonstrating that the model
had substantial out-of-sample predictive power. According to Elnaqlah
and Abbas (2024), Q2 values greater than zero indicate that the exoge-
nous constructs meaningfully predicted the endogenous variable, con-
firming that the model did more than simply explain variance and also
provided reliable forecasts for new observations.

The PLS-SEM predictive metrics supported this conclusion. The Root
Mean  Square  Error  (RMSE)  was  0.666  and  the  Mean  Absolute  Error
(MAE) was 0.467, suggesting relatively low prediction errors. As noted
by Chinnaraju (2025), lower RMSE and MAE values indicate that the
model  outperformed  naive  benchmarks  and  confirmed  its  predictive
accuracy  beyond  explanatory  capacity.  Overall,  the  findings  demon-
strated  that  the  structural  model  reliably  predicted  self-learning
outcome,  underscoring  the  value  of  AI  Influence,  competence,  and
perception in shaping students' learning trajectories. The results high-
lighted the practical utility of integrating AI technologies in higher ed-
ucation, as they allowed institutions to anticipate and enhance students’
self-directed learning outcomes (Wu et al., 2024).

5. Discussion

This study examined how student competence, perception, and AI
influence shaped self-learning outcomes of participants in higher edu-
cation setting in a developing country. The results highlighted the in-
teractions among these factors and also provided new insights into how
the  use  of  generative  AI  tools  affected  self-learning  outcomes.  The
findings  for  hypothesis  one  confirmed  that  competence  significantly
influenced  self-learning  outcomes.  This  implied  that  students  with
higher  competence  in  using  generative  AI  tools  were  more  likely  to
demonstrate an improved ability to critically evaluate AI outputs, tailor
them  to  suit  their  learning  needs,  and  integrate  them  into  problem-
solving  tasks  (Kotsis,  2025;  Maphalala  et  al.,  2025;  Mohebbi,  2025;
Shikina, 2025). These results reinforced the argument that competence
acted as a foundation rather than a standalone driver of learning gains.
Without  sufficient  competence,  even  high-quality  AI  tools  may  not
translate  into  meaningful  academic  improvements  among  students
(Deng  &  Liu,  2025;  Chiu  et  al.,  2024;  Uren  &  Edwards,  2023).  This
assertion was aligned with self-determination theory which emphasized
autonomy  and  competence  as  central  drivers  of  sustained  motivation
and  achievement  among  students  (Khlaif  et  al.,  2025;  Oubibi,  2025;
Wiemer, 2019).

With regards to hypothesis two on perception, the hypothesis was
not supported. The implication is that perception toward the usefulness,

The third hypothesis which focused on AI influence, emerged as the
most powerful predictor of self-learning outcomes. This meant that the
capacity  of  AI  to  foster  student  autonomy,  encourage  responsible
engagement,  and  motivate  active  exploration  was  coherent  with  self-
learning  outcomes  (Ogalo  &  Mtenzi,  2025;  Saleh,  2025;  Xu  et  al.,
2025).  The  results  showed  that  generative  AI  tools,  when  integrated
thoughtfully  would  help  shape  students'  learning  behaviours  and
enhance  self-directed  learning  capacities  (Chiu  et  al.,  2024;  Li  et  al.,
2025; Shikina, 2025; Xueqing & Thomas, 2025). Importantly, the find-
ings suggested that the transformative potential of AI lies not only in
technical  mastery  or  initial  perception  but  in  its  ability  to  encourage
students to take ownership of learning and engage in active knowledge
construction (Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Yavich, 2025). This
also extended previous work by demonstrating that AI tools can act as an
agent of behavioural change in educational contexts (Uren & Edwards,
2023;  Maphalala  et  al.,  2025).  Taken  together,  these  findings  high-
lighted  a  dynamic  interplay  between  competence,  perception,  and  AI
influence. As competence might equip students with the foundational
skills  to  use  AI  effectively,  perception  was  likely  to  shape  initial
engagement, while AI influence drive meaningful behavioural changes
that result in improved self-learning outcomes. By examining these di-
mensions together, this study advanced an understanding of AI's role in
higher education settings.

The findings contribute to the literature in three main ways. First,
they show that perception does not directly translate into self-learning
outcomes.  Although  positive  views  about  the  usefulness,  ease  of  use,
and trustworthiness of AI tools may encourage students to engage with
them,  such  views  alone  are  not  enough  to  produce  meaningful  self-
learning  outcomes  (Mohebi,  2025;  Rafiq-uz-Zaman  &  Nadeem,  2025;
Uppal &  Hajian, 2025). Second, the study positions AI Influence as a
behavioural mechanism that links students' engagement with generative
AI  tools  to  autonomy,  active  exploration,  learning  responsibility,  and
improved self-learning outcomes (Ogalo &  Mtenzi, 2025; Saleh, 2025;
Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). Third, the study
extends Self-Determination Theory and Self-Regulated Learning theory
by  showing  how  competence,  perception,  and  AI  Influence  shape
self-learning  outcomes  in  a  developing-country  distance  education
context, where generative AI use is growing but institutional guidance
remains  uneven  (Deci  &  Ryan,  2012;  El  Samaty,  2025;  Kotsis,  2025;
Mohebbi, 2025; Sarfo et al., 2024; Shi &  Zhang, 2025; Wakunuma &

Eke, 2024; Zimmerman, 2013). This theoretical contribution refines AI
education  literature  by  showing  that  competence  provides  the  skills
needed  for  effective  generative  AI  use,  but  it  does  not  fully  explain
self-learning  outcomes.  Stronger  outcomes  depend  on  AI  Influence,
which captures how learners use AI for planning, monitoring, evalua-
tion, autonomy, and independent academic improvement (Banihashem
et  al.,  2025;  Chiu,  2025;  Oates  &  Johnson,  2025;  Radovi´c  &  Seidel,
2025; Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Zhang & Chen, 2026). The
non-significant perception path further shows that positive views of AI's
usefulness,  ease  of  use,  and  trustworthiness  should  be  treated  as  an
enabling  condition,  not  a  direct  predictor  of  self-learning  outcomes
(Mohebi,  2025;  Rafiq-uz-Zaman  &  Nadeem,  2025;  Uppal  &  Hajian,
2025).

5.1. Conclusion

The study provided compelling evidence that while competence was
important,  AI  influence  served  as  the  key  driver  of  self-learning  out-
comes  in  higher  education.  Competence  ensured  students  could  use
generative AI tools effectively, evaluate outputs critically, and integrate
AI  into  their  learning  process  responsibly.  Though  perception  may
initiate engagement with generative AI tools, it did not guarantee self-
learning  outcomes  as  portrayed  in  this  study.  The  study  confirmed
that,  AI  influence  fostered  meaningful  self-directed  learning  among
students. These insights had some implications. Firstly, for students, the
findings  highlighted  the  need  to  combine  technical  skills  with  active
engagement.  Simply  having  access  to  generative  AI  tools  or  positive
attitudes  toward  them  was  not  enough;  learners  must  interact  pur-
posefully  with  AI  tools  to  construct  knowledge  and  develop  critical
thinking. It is very worthy to note that students in a developing country
context showed portrayed some competence in the use of generative Al
tools and platforms towards improving their learning activities.

As future educators, these innovative skills are likely to have a ripple
effect on their teaching and professional activities. Secondly, for edu-
cators, the study wants to put emphasis on the need to redesign learning
experiences where generative AI tools would become more attractive to
encourage autonomy, responsibility and active exploration. A focus on
pedagogical strategies that would leverage on generative AI tools as a
supportive  tool  would  improve  on  students'  perception  towards  the
usefulness and trustworthiness of such tools. This assertion is deemed
very important because a student's perception about the usefulness of
generative AI tools would argument their efforts to use such tools and
subsequently  improve  on  their  competence  as  well.  Thirdly,  at  the
institutional level, managers need to be intentional about the incorpo-
ration of generative AI tools in training programs and monitoring sys-
tems  of  future  educators  to  ensure  empowerment,  creativity  and
responsible  learning.  Overall,  the  study  established  a  nuanced  frame-
work in which competence, perception, and AI influence were interde-
pendent.  The  study  concluded  that  competence  provided  the
foundation,  perception  may  initiate  engagement  while  AI  influence
drove  the  behavioural  transformation  necessary  for  successful  self-
learning outcomes. By focusing on all three dimensions, higher educa-
tion  institutions  could  harness  generative  AI  tools  to  cultivate  inde-
pendent,  responsible,  and  motivated  learners  equipped  to  thrive  in  a
technology-driven world (Shikina, 2025; Maphalala et al., 2025; Uren
& Edwards, 2023).

5.3. Practical and policy implications

From  a  practical  perspective,  universities  could  implement  struc-
tured AI literacy programs by combining technical training and critical
thinking development. These programs would help students to develop
the needed perceptions and also adopt generative AI tools responsibly to
foster meaningful engagement and improved academic pursuits (Kotsis,
2024; Shikina, 2025). This perspective is considered as very important
strategy based on the fact that right competencies and positive percep-
tions about the use of appropriate generative AI tools are relevant to the
extent to which generative AI tools could influence learning outcomes of
students in the settings as the ones used in the current study. The use of
innovative technological tools to improve teaching, learning and pro-
fessional  practices  are  important  skills  any  21st  century  educator  or
student cannot afford to miss. Administrators and policymakers there-
fore  need  to  develop  frameworks  that  strengthen  monitoring  systems
that incorporate AI strategies into national higher education plans. Ev-
idence  from  African  contexts  suggests  that  while  students  are  eager
adopters  of  generative  AI,  unequal  access  and  lack  of  regulatory
frameworks  could  compromise  long-term  benefits  (Sarfo  et  al.,  2024;
Wakunuma & Eke, 2024). Governance approaches must therefore bal-
ance innovation with regulation to ensure academic integrity, equitable
access and responsible use of generative AI tools. The practical recom-
mendation  is  for  universities  to  move  from  general  AI  awareness  to
structured AI-supported learning design. This requires short AI literacy
modules,  assessment tasks  that require  prompt evaluation  and  source
verification, and tutor guidance on responsible use. Policy units should
also  develop  institutional  AI  use  guidelines  that  address  access,  aca-
demic integrity, acceptable support, and data privacy. These steps give
students the skills and boundaries needed to turn AI engagement into
measurable learning gains.

5.4. Suggestions for future studies

Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs
to clarify causal relationships between AI use and learning outcomes.
Studies across diverse cultural and institutional contexts can test model
generalizability.  Further  investigation  should  explore  faculty  roles,
institutional  support,  and  ethical  factors  influencing  AI  integration.
Additional research into behavioural and psychological outcomes, such
as  creativity,  critical  thinking,  and  problem-solving,  will  deepen  un-
derstanding of AI's long-term impact on higher education (Chiu et al.,
2024; Uren & Edwards, 2023).

5.5. Limitation

cross-sectional  design  restricted  the  ability  to  draw  causal  inferences
between  competence,  perception,  AI  influence,  and  self-learning  out-
comes. The focus on two institutions for distance education limited the
generalizability of findings to other higher education contexts, partic-
ularly those with different levels of digital infrastructure and pedagog-
ical  integration  of  generative  AI  tools.  Moreover,  reliance  on  self-
reported  data  may  have  introduced  response  bias,  as  students  might
have overstated or understated their competencies, although the survey
items were adapted from prior validated studies and assessed through
reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity tests, the study
relied on self-reported responses. Finally, the study did not account for
institutional policies, faculty engagement, or infrastructural disparities,
which  could  have  moderated  how  generative  AI  shaped  self-learning
outcomes.

5.6. Ethical consideration

This study adhered to established ethical protocols for educational
research.  Participation  was  voluntary,  and  informed  consent  was  ob-
tained prior to data collection. Respondents were assured of confiden-
tiality  and  anonymity,  with  no  personally  identifiable  information
collected. The questionnaire items on ethical issues surrounding AI use
were carefully framed to minimize social desirability bias and encourage
honest responses. In line with institutional review standards, the study
emphasized  transparency,  responsible  handling  of  data,  and  accurate
reporting of results. These measures ensured that the research upheld
principles of integrity, respect, and accountability while examining the
emerging role of AI in higher education. Ethical approval was obtained
from the Internal Review Board prior to the commencement of the study.
