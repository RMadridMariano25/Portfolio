The emergence of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools, such as ChatGPT, is reshaping the landscape of
higher  education  by  introducing  new  opportunities  for  learner  autonomy,  flexibility,  and  engagement.  While
extensive  research  has  explored  GenAI’s  technical  capabilities  and  ethical  implications,  limited  attention  has
been paid to students’ subjective learning experiences with AI-supported instruction. This study investigates how
undergraduate  students  perceive  and  experience  two  instructional  modes  in  a  database  management  course:
traditional  lecturer-led  instruction  and  GenAI-supported  self-regulated  learning.  Sixty-eight  second-year  engi-
neering students participated in the study, providing qualitative insights through open-ended survey responses.
The findings reveal key cognitive, emotional, and strategic differences between the two approaches: traditional
instruction fostered structure, immediate feedback, and emotional reassurance, while GenAI-supported learning
promoted autonomy and exploration but raised concerns regarding reliability and critical thinking. Importantly,
students did not view these modes as mutually exclusive, but rather as complementary components of a broader
“learning puzzle,”  balancing the stability of face-to-face instruction with the adaptability of GenAI to support
diverse learning needs. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of hybrid learning environments and
underscores the importance of nuanced, learner-centered integration of AI technologies in higher education.

shift in higher education. Unlike earlier AI technologies designed pri-

virtual  teaching  assistants,  and  tools  that  support  creativity,  critical
thinking, and inquiry-based learning.

Research on this topic is essential for understanding how integrating
GenAI into educational settings influences student motivation, engage-
ment,  and  overall  learning  experiences.  From  a  practical  perspective,
such  insights  are  highly  relevant  for  educational  policy,  curriculum
design, and teaching practice. As institutions decide how to adopt and
regulate GenAI use, evidence on students’ lived experiences guides how
to  balance  innovation  with  reliability,  support  autonomy  without
undermining  critical  thinking,  and  design  curricula  that  responsibly
integrate human and technological strengths.

Accordingly,  this  study  investigates  how  students  experience  two
distinct  course  instruction  modes.  The  first  is  traditional  face-to-face
learning  led  by  a  lecturer,  and  the  second  is  GenAI-supported  self-
regulated  learning.  Our  goal  is  to  provide  nuanced  insights  into  how
these  instructional  methods  might  complement  or  contradict  one
another and to identify the conditions under which they best support
student engagement, self-regulation, and deep learning.

Comparing traditional lecturer-led instruction with GenAI-supported
learning holds significant implications for teaching practice, curriculum
design,  and  educational policy. Understanding  how  students  navigate
and  value  these  modes  can  inform  instructors  on  when  to  leverage
human interaction for scaffolding and emotional support, how to inte-
grate  GenAI  tools  to  promote  learner  autonomy  and  exploration, and
how institutions can craft policies that ensure responsible and equitable
AI integration across courses. This comparison thus provides a founda-
tion  for  evidence-based  strategies  that  align  technological  innovation
with pedagogical integrity.

Based on these aims, we formulated the following  study question.
What patterns emerge when comparing students’  experiences learning new
course content through traditional lecturer-led instruction versus learning the
same content through GenAI?

These dynamics position the study within two established theoretical
frameworks. First, the Self-Regulated Learning theory [9] highlights the
centrality  of  planning,  monitoring,  and  self-evaluation  in  learners’
ability to navigate instructional demands. Second, Cognitive Load The-
ory  [10]  emphasizes  how  different  learning  environments  influence
intrinsic,  extraneous,  and  germane  cognitive  load.  Together,  these
frameworks  clarify  why  comparing  lecturer-led  and  GenAI-supported
learning  is  theoretically  meaningful,  as  each  environment  activates
distinct self-regulation processes and cognitive load conditions.

Data were collected from 68 undergraduate students enrolled in a
computer  science  program  through  open-ended  survey  responses
following  engagement  with  both  instructional  methods.  The  research
methodology  chosen  for  this  study  was  an  interpretive  qualitative
approach. It analyzed these responses to explore the subjective mean-
ings students assign to their learning experiences, including elements of
complexity, ambiguity, and internal contradictions.

The findings reveal that traditional instruction was appreciated for
providing structured explanations, immediate feedback, and emotional
reassurance, reinforcing conclusions from previous research [11,12]. In
contrast,  GenAI-supported  learning  was  valued  for  promoting  auton-
omy, flexibility, and exploratory learning. At the same time, the data
echo concerns raised in prior studies regarding the uncertain reliability
of information, challenges in self-navigation, and a potential decline in
critical thinking in a GenAI-supported learning model [13,14]. As we
shall show, our findings suggest that rather than treating human-led and
AI-supported instruction as opposing models, we should consider them
as complementary components of hybrid learning.

Altogether, by qualitatively examining students’ subjective learning
experiences with both human-led and GenAI-supported instruction, this
study contributes to a deeper understanding of the evolving educational
landscape. It highlights the need to move beyond binary conceptions of
human versus technological instruction, advocating for a more nuanced
appreciation  of  hybrid,  learner-centered  educational  models  [15–17].
This  innovative  focus  on  subjective  student  experiences,  rather  than
only measuring attitudes or usage patterns, offers a novel contribution to
the growing literature on GenAI in education. It also provides actionable

insights  for  educators  and  policymakers  seeking  to  design  effective,
ethical, and sustainable hybrid learning environments.

In  line  with  these  perspectives,  this  paper  explores  how  students
experience and navigate the interplay of teaching, social, and cognitive
presences  in  technology-mediated  learning  environments.  While  the
Community  of  Inquiry  framework  provides  a  valuable  system-level
perspective, our analysis suggests the need to consider learners’  more
fluid, subjective movements across these presences. As will be shown
later in the paper, this exploration leads us to introduce a new concep-
tual model that emerges from our findings and aims to capture these
dynamics in greater depth.

2. Background and related works

2.1. Pedagogical and theoretical context

Higher  education  has  undergone  a  major  transformation  from
traditional,  instructor-centered  instruction  toward  more  flexible  and
technology-mediated  learning  models.  Traditional  learning  environ-
ments,  typically  synchronous  and  face-to-face,  emphasize  knowledge
transmission from  instructor to  student, consistent  with  the  objectivist
model in which knowledge is seen as fixed and measurable [6,18]. In
contrast, the situated learning theory perspective views learning as a so-
cial,  context-bound  process,  emphasizing  active  participation  and
community engagement [19]. Face-to-face instruction promotes moti-
vation, belonging, and immediate feedback [11,12], yet may constrain
flexibility for learners with diverse needs or circumstances [6].

The  rapid  digital  transformation  accelerated  by  the  COVID-19
pandemic  has  expanded  online  and  hybrid  learning  modalities,
combining  synchronous  interaction  and  asynchronous  flexibility  [20,
21]. While online learning enhances accessibility and autonomy, it can
also  diminish  motivation  and  social  connection  [22].  These  de-
velopments  have  intensified  pedagogical  interest  in  active  learning,
which  emphasizes  participation,  inquiry,  and  collaboration.  Research
consistently  supports  active  learning  for  improving  engagement,  un-
derstanding, and achievement [7,8,23]. Recent findings also show that
courses integrating active learning strategies receive significantly higher
evaluations and perceived instructional quality from students [24,25].
frameworks—Self-Regulated

Learning  (SRL)  [9]  and  Cognitive  Load  Theory  (CLT)  [10,26]—help
explain these changes. SRL emphasizes learners’ ability to plan, monitor,
and evaluate their progress, while CLT focuses on managing cognitive
resources efficiently. GenAI technologies affect both frameworks: they
may reduce cognitive effort through instant feedback and personalized
explanations, yet introduce new demands for evaluation, reflection, and
information verification.

2.2. Pedagogical tensions in technology-mediated learning

The rapid shift toward flexible and blended learning environments,
mainly  driven  by  digital  transformation  and  the  adoption  of  active
learning  strategies,  has  led  to  the  identification  of  three  interrelated
pedagogical tensions. The first is the structure–flexibility paradox [15,16,
30,31]. This paradox captures the challenge of balancing the need for
clear instructional frameworks with increasing demands for autonomy,
personalization,  and  learner  agency.  While  structured  learning  envi-
ronments provide necessary scaffolding and clear pathways, flexibility
enables personalized learning experiences that let learners control their
pace, path, and place of learning, aligning education more closely with
individual needs and circumstances. Flexible learning options have been

poses significant challenges. Excessive autonomy, particularly without
sufficient structure, may lead to decreased motivation, procrastination,
cognitive overload, and feelings of isolation [28,33,34]. For example,
Rasheed et al. [33] found that while students appreciate the autonomy
afforded by flexibility, many simultaneously express a strong need for
clearly defined structures, such as deadlines, guided learning paths, and
explicit instructor feedback, to manage their learning processes effec-
tively. Similarly, Boelens et al. [28] emphasize the importance of stra-
tegic  instructional  scaffolding  to  balance  flexibility  and  structure,
arguing  that  effective  blended  learning  environments  must  combine
autonomy-supportive elements with structured guidance to foster suc-
cessful self-regulated learning.

This paradox is further exacerbated by technological affordances and
constraints inherent in virtual learning platforms. As institutions seek to
balance structure and flexibility, digital tools and GenAI offer targeted,
adaptive experiences that complement instructional design. Yet while
these  technologies  offer  unprecedented  flexibility  and  personalized
learning experiences, they also require careful pedagogical framing and
explicit  instructional  design  strategies  to  prevent  learners  from
becoming overwhelmed or disoriented [35].

Recent  studies  advocate  a  hybrid  model  that  integrates  a  clear
pedagogical structure alongside personalized flexibility, proposing that
educators  explicitly  scaffold  learners’  autonomy  through  strategic  in-
terventions,  clear  instructions,  and  targeted  feedback  [29,32,36].
Consequently, navigating the structure–flexibility paradox requires ed-
ucators to thoughtfully design instructional experiences that integrate
pedagogical  structure  and  learner  autonomy.  This  includes  deliberate
attention to metacognitive support and the effective use of technological
tools.

The second central tension in contemporary pedagogy is the dialectic
between  instructor  authority  and  learner  autonomy.  Instructors  must
provide clear guidance and structured feedback, while also cultivating
learners’  self-regulated  learning  and  personal  agency  [37–40].  This
dialectic  parallels  the  structure–flexibility  paradox,  in  that  both
emphasize  integrating  external  support  with  internal  autonomy.

The  third  tension,  between  attention  and  cognitive  load,  is  closely
related  to  the  first  two.  This  paradox  refers  to  the  multiple  demands
placed on learners’  mental resources in technology-enhanced environ-
ments, particularly in hybrid and self-regulated learning contexts. On
the  one  hand,  digital  tools,  especially  GenAI  applications,  can  help
reduce extraneous cognitive load by simplifying complex information,
providing  instant feedback,  and  enabling learners  to control  the pace
and sequence of content presented [32,43]. On the other hand, the very
abundance  of  information,  choices,  and  open-ended  prompts  that
characterize  GenAI-supported  learning  may  increase  intrinsic  or
germane  cognitive  load,  requiring  learners  to  monitor,  evaluate,  and
self-regulate their learning processes continuously [44–46]. The poten-
tial for GenAI to produce incorrect or unreliable information presents
another  problem,  requiring  learners  to  be  alert  to  this  possibility.
Moreover, research shows that simply maintaining attention in online or
blended settings often requires greater executive control compared to
traditional classrooms, due to increased distractions and the absence of
external social cues [34,47].

2.3. Gen AI and students’ learning environment

Generative AI introduces new opportunities and challenges within
these  pedagogical  tensions.  Tools  such  as  ChatGPT  are  now  used  for
diverse  educational  purposes,  including  brainstorming,  writing,  and
programming support [1,2,48,49]. Studies show that GenAI can foster
autonomy,  metacognition,  and  engagement  when  meaningfully  inte-
grated [43], but also raise concerns about bias, plagiarism, overreliance,
and critical thinking [4,5,50]. Pedagogically, the growing use of GenAI
also necessitates rethinking assessment design, fostering AI literacy, and
developing  instructional  frameworks  that  enhance  learning  while
maintaining human-centered teaching values [4,51].

Some researchers have reframed the challenges of GenAI as oppor-
tunities to foster dialogue, reflection, and critical thinking in the class-
room.  For  example,  Roe  et  al.  [52]  proposed  metaphors  such  as  the
“funhouse  mirror”  and  “echo  chamber”  to  help  students  critically
engage with GenAI and its social implications, emphasizing experiential
learning that treats GenAI as both a tool and a concept for structured
reflection. Beyond these “top-down” approaches, students’ “bottom-up”
meaning-making  is  equally  important,  shaped  by  pedagogy,  content,
and learning environments. Traditional face-to-face instruction fosters
belonging  and  engagement,  while  asynchronous  online  learning  can
lead to isolation when poorly structured [53]. As GenAI use expands,
students  report  mixed  experiences—enhanced  confidence,  autonomy,
and  efficiency  [54]  alongside  concerns  about  authenticity,  critical
thinking, and overreliance [14]. Despite these insights, the subjective,
emotional, and identity-related dimensions of GenAI-supported learning
remain underexplored, underscoring the need for deeper understanding
to inform ethical and learner-centered integration [55].

To  address  this  need,  this  study  investigates  how  undergraduate
students perceive and experience two instructional modes in a database
management  course:  traditional  lecturer-led  instruction  and  GenAI-
supported  self-regulated  learning.  Using  an  open-ended  survey,  we
examined  cognitive,  emotional,  and  strategic  differences  between  the
two approaches.

4. Methodology

We adopted an interpretive qualitative research approach, analyzing
students’  responses to open-ended questions. This approach enables a
deep understanding of the subjective meanings that participants assign
to their learning experiences, allowing space for diverse interpretations,
complexity,  and  internal  contradictions  in  respondents’  perceptions.
Because the study employed an interpretive qualitative design focused
on meaning-making rather than hypothesis testing, inferential statistical
analyses  were  not  applied.  Descriptive  statistics  were  used  only  to
summarize participant demographics and baseline equivalence between
groups.

4.1. Participants and procedure

4.1.1. Overview of study participants and procedure

The  study  was  conducted  within  the  framework  of  a  mandatory
undergraduate course in Database Management, part of the second-year
curriculum in a Computer Science program. The course consists of 13
weekly sessions, each lasting three academic hours.

All participants in the study were third-semester Computer Science
students  who  shared  a  common  academic  background,  having
completed the same set of introductory courses. Students were divided
into two instructional groups (Group 1 and Group 2). Group 1 (n = 55;
57.3 % of the original cohort) consisted of 18 women (32.7 % of the
group) and 37 men (67.3 % of the group), while Group 2 (n = 32; 33.3 %
of the original cohort) consisted of 7 women (21.9 % of the group) and
25 men (78.1 % of the group). Group allocation was determined by the
department’s  standard  administrative  registration  process  rather  than
by random assignment. This procedure was handled by course admin-
istrators with no involvement from the researchers and without refer-
ence  to  any  demographic or  academic  variables.  Consequently,  while
group  formation  was  independent  of  the  study  design,  it  does  not
constitute true randomization.

4.1.2. Detailed description of the procedure

The experimental protocol consisted of three main stages: the first at
the beginning of the course (week 1), and the second and third toward
the end (weeks 10–11).

First  Stage:  To  ensure  comparability  between  the  two  groups,  all
students completed a baseline questionnaire during the first week of the
semester (see Appendix 3 for the complete baseline questionnaire). This
questionnaire  assessed  key  measures,  including  motivation,  self-
regulated learning (SRL), and self-efficacy. Independent-samples t-tests
revealed  no  statistically  significant  differences  between  the  groups
across most scales and subscales. Specifically, no significant differences
were observed for motivation (t(70) = (cid:0) 0.330, p = .742), self-efficacy (t
(70) = (cid:0) 1.630, p = .108), or SRL (t(70) = 0.595, p = .554). These results
indicate that the groups were equivalent on central baseline measures,
supporting the internal validity of subsequent comparisons.

The  experimental  component  of  the  study  was  conducted  during
Weeks 10 and 11 of the course, specifically in the second half of each
session (approximately 75 min). All sessions took place in a standard
classroom  environment,  and  students  were  instructed  to  bring  their
personal laptops for use during the self-regulated learning activities. To
ensure a balanced comparison, all the new content introduced was of
comparable difficulty.

Second Stage: In Week 10, the instructional topic was using “Group
By” clauses in SQL queries. That week, 55 students (100 %) from Group
1  (18  women,  100  %)  attended  the  session.  This  group  was  asked  to
study the topic independently, supported by a GenAI tool of their choice.
The specific tools used by participants were not tracked in this study.

Students were provided with a brief overview of the required content,
defined  learning  objectives,  and  a  suggested  initial  prompt  to  begin
interacting with the tool. They then engaged in self-regulated learning
using  their  selected  GenAI  tool.  At  the  end  of  the  session,  students
completed a questionnaire (the GenAI Questionnaire) that included both
closed-  and  open-ended  questions  (Appendix 1 contains  the  complete
GenAI Questionnaire). That same week, 32 students (100 %) from Group
2 (7 women, 100 %) attended a session in which they studied the same
topic (i.e., “Group By” clauses) within the same timeframe, but through
traditional  in-class  instruction  delivered  by  the  course  lecturer.  Upon
completion, they filled out a questionnaire (the Lecturer Questionnaire),
which also included both closed- and open-ended questions (see below
and Appendix 2). The two questionnaires (AI and Lecturer) were iden-
tical in format and wording, differing only in the subject of evaluation:
one asked about the GenAI teaching system, while the other asked the
same questions about the human lecturer. This parallel design enabled
direct  comparison  between  students’  perceptions  of  GenAI-based  and
traditional instruction methods.

Third Stage: In Week 11, the instructional topic was using “Derived
Tables” in SQL queries. That week, 49 students (89.1 %) from Group 1
(18  women,  100  %)  participated  in  a  session  taught  by  the  course
lecturer  using  traditional  classroom  instruction,  then  completed  the
Lecturer Questionnaire. Meanwhile, 27 students (84.4 %) from Group 2
(6  women,  85.7  %)  studied  the  same  topic  through  GenAI-supported
self-regulated learning. As with Group 1 in Week 10, students received
an overview of the content, defined learning objectives, and a sample
prompt. They then chose a GenAI tool, engaged in self-directed learning,
and finally completed the GenAI Questionnaire.

In total, 87 students participated in at least one instructional session.
However, only 68 students (78.2 %) were present in both Week 10 and
Week  11  and  completed  both  questionnaires.  These  68  respondents
formed the basis for the analysis.

4.4. Ethical considerations

The present paper reports our findings from the open-ended items,
designed  to  capture  perceptions  and  experiences  relevant  to  our  four
research  questions  (see  Research  Objectives  above).  In  the  Lecturer
Questionnaire, these items invited students to reflect on their learning
experience  in  the  traditional  classroom  setting.  Questions  focused  on
perceived  advantages  and  disadvantages,  emotional  responses  during
the  lesson,  and  the  quality  of  interaction  with  the  lecturer.  A  sample
item: “Describe the advantages of learning course content in a traditional
face-to-face classroom setting for you personally.”

The  open-ended  section  of  the  GenAI  Questionnaire  prompted  stu-
dents to describe their learning experience while using a GenAI-based
chatbot. Questions addressed the perceived benefits and limitations of

4.3. Data analysis

Our qualitative analysis was based on the thematic analysis frame-
work developed by Braun and Clarke [56]. To ensure analytical rigor
and  reliability,  we  used  a  systematic  and  iterative  coding  approach.
Initially, two independent researchers analyzed a sample of 10 responses
to each open-ended question. This preliminary analysis allowed us to
generate  initial  codes  and  identify  emerging  themes.  For  example,  in
response to the item “Describe the advantages of learning the material
using  a  GenAI-based  chat,”  the  following  answer  was  analyzed:  “The
answers are always detailed, there is no limit to the number of examples
the  GenAI  tool  can provide,  and  even  if  something  is  not  understood
after several attempts, it will look for another way to explain.” From this
response, we defined three themes: Responding to questions, Providing
examples,  and  Providing  explanations.  Another  response  stated:
“Concise, includes examples, and directly refers you to additional rele-
vant  websites.  Provides  examples,  focused.”  From  this  answer,  the
identified themes were Online features, Providing examples, and Con-
ciseness/focus.  Following  a  collaborative  discussion  of  these  initial
codes and themes, we developed a preliminary codebook with clearly
defined codes and specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Once  the  preliminary  codebook  was  established,  two  researchers
independently coded a new sample of 20 responses for each open-ended
question (80 responses in total). They then compared their coding de-
cisions and resolved discrepancies through discussion. A high level of
inter-rater agreement was achieved, with 77 of 80 coded responses (96
%) showing full consensus. Intercoder reliability was further assessed
using  Cohen’s  Kappa  (κ  = 0.86,  p  < .001),  indicating  almost  perfect
agreement. No new themes emerged during this phase, confirming the
comprehensiveness and robustness of the coding framework.

After establishing the final codebook, the primary researcher coded
the partial dataset used to create the codebook (120 responses per open-
ended question). A second researcher then conducted a dependability
check by independently reviewing a random sample of 10 responses per
open-ended question, for a total of 40 responses. The second researcher
was in complete agreement with the original coding, further confirming
the analysis’s consistency and trustworthiness. Following this reliability
check,  the  primary  researcher  proceeded  to  code  the  whole  dataset,
completing  the  analysis  of  all  220  responses  for  each  open-ended
question.

This  multi-stage  and  iterative  process  -  combining  intercoder
agreement testing, consensus-based code refinement, and independent
dependability  checks  -  ensured  the  transparency,  consistency,  and
trustworthiness of our thematic analysis. Repeated reading and focused
coding  enabled  the  identification  of  recurring,  distinct,  and  unique
content in participants’ responses, forming the foundation for a deeper
interpretive examination of the tensions, contradictions, and dialectical
relations underlying students’ perceptions.

The  study  received  ethical  approval  from  the  institutional  review
board of the academic institution where it was conducted. Participation
was  voluntary,  and  students  received  academic  credit  for  their
involvement. Participants were informed that their responses would be
used solely for research purposes and that their identities would remain
anonymous.  Each  student  was  assigned  a  random  four-digit  code  to
preserve anonymity while enabling linkage between responses.

5.1. Roles and dynamics

RQ1. How do students perceive the roles and dynamics of learning in these
two distinct educational environments?

Regarding the  roles and  dynamics  of  learning  in  the  two  environ-

while some students expressed strong preferences for one method, many
did  not  perceive  lecturer-led  instruction  and  GenAI  as  opposing,  but
rather recognized their potential complementarity in forming a dynamic
learning system. This tendency was visible in both aggregated responses
and, to a considerable extent, within individual accounts. Second, while
some of the findings aligned with expectations about the two environ-
ments, others subverted them in unexpected ways.

5.3.1. Learning environment: interpersonal vs. individual

When  comparing  peer  learning  in  the  classroom  to  the  individual
learning  experience  facilitated  by  GenAI,  students  emphasized  the
importance of a human presence and interaction with both lecturers and
peers as a key factor in classroom-based learning. Responses citing the
advantages of the social environment found in the classroom highlighted
pragmatic as well as emotional benefits: “For me, a frontal lesson is a
commitment to listen and want to learn; alone at home, it’s harder for
me to obligate myself” (Participant 3584); “The learned information is
reliable, there is human response to questions” (Participant 8519). Some
students  expressed  a  clear  preference  for  the  classroom  environment
specifically  because  of  its  social  and  collaborative  nature:  “I  came  to
class to learn, if I wanted to learn with the chatbot, I would’ve stayed
home” (Participant 9635). At the same time, several students appreci-
ated the personalized and private nature of learning with GenAI. As one
participant wrote, GenAI offers the “ability to quickly obtain informa-
tion on a specific topic that would take longer to search alone, plus it
doesn’t require contacting students or lecturers to try to get the infor-
mation” (Participant 2444), while another noted: “It’s easier for me to
learn individually, and the chatbot is based on that” (Participant 6710).
A third student described the GenAI experience as “like a private tutor
available 24/7 for any question, with infinite patience and ability to drill
down into the material and provide thousands of examples” (Participant
9147).

reflect  stable  individual  differences  in  students’  preferences.  For
instance, it may be that more extraverted students generally feel greater
need for peer interaction and collaborative learning, while more intro-
verted or less confident students prefer to learn privately, shielded from
others’  gaze.  At  the  same  time,  a  comparative  analysis  of  paired  re-
sponses  from  the  same  students  (Table  6)  suggests  that at  least  some
students are able to balance the social value of classroom learning and
the  desire  for  autonomy  and  focused  support  in  a  more  self-paced,
individualized learning environment.

5.3.2. Adaptive learning strategies

In line with the differences just discussed, our analysis shows that
students actively adapt their learning approaches to align with the op-
portunities and constraints of each learning environment. For example,
when learning in the classroom, students understand that they will have
to  concentrate  to  ensure  they  don’t  miss  material.  By  contrast,  when
studying  with  GenAI,  they  can  take  advantage  of  the  opportunity  to
study at their own pace and without external pressure.

Interestingly, many respondents reported incorporating digital tools
within traditional classroom settings to reduce cognitive load, improve
their  motivation,  and  manage  their  time  more  effectively.  As  one
participant explained: “I listen to the lecturer with the presentation open
on my iPad, to follow the lecture and sometimes linger on the material
covered in class at my own pace for deeper understanding” (Participant
9746). In this way students can bring some of the benefits of techno-
logically supported learning into the classroom.

The paired quotations in Table 7 show that students exhibit meta-
cognitive  awareness  by  recognizing  the  distinct  affordances  of  each

5.4. Contradictions and complexities

RQ4. What  contradictions  and  complexities  emerge  in  students’  percep-
tions when they think about the two learning methods?

Students’ testimonies reveal both the complexities of learning in the
digital age, and ways that the availability of complementary environ-
ments  can  help  resolve  tensions  and  contradictions  inherent  to  each.
Three prominent patterns emerged from our analysis, in keeping with
the three key tensions already identified in the literature and discussed
above: the tensions between structure and flexibility, between authority
and autonomy, and between attention and cognitive load. Importantly,
in each case, our findings suggest that students do not choose between
the lecturer-based and GenAI-based environments, but rather navigate
fluidly between them based on evolving needs.

5.4.1. Structure and flexibility

The first prominent tension is that between structure and flexibility.
Our findings confirm that students value traditional classroom instruc-
tion in part for its ability to provide the former, and GenAI in part for its
ability to furnish the latter. For example, students expressed apprecia-
tion  for  the  clear  and  guided  framework  of  traditional  classroom  in-
struction: “It’s easier to understand the material being taught through
the lecturer” (Participant 3823), or “There’s a place to ask questions and
get  answers”  (Participant  4856).  However,  students  noted  challenges
with  the  structured  rhythm  of  classroom  instruction,  which  does  not
always  align  with  individual  learning  speeds.  For  example,  some
described the experience of falling behind following momentary lapses
in attention, or feeling unable to pause and revisit material as needed.

By contrast, the students emphasized the advantages of the flexibility
and accessibility provided by GenAI: “Fast, accessible, and gives answers
to all  my questions”  (Participant 5745); “Explains in detail about the
content,  lingering  extensively  on  questions  in  the  studied  topics  and
practice during learning” (Participant 4444). In particular, GenAI-based
learning  was  praised  for  its  ability  to  accommodate  flexible  pacing,
allowing students to pause, repeat, and clarify information according to
their own needs, an invaluable asset when encountering complex con-
tent or navigating moments of cognitive overload. Yet participants also
reported  concerns  about  the  tool’s  limitations,  notably  the  fact  that
while GenAI may be highly efficient and intuitive, it can also be unre-
liable. For example, from Participant 7417: “Learning with the chatbot is
focused  …  intuitive,”  but  also  “The  chatbot  can  be  misleading  and
doesn’t  always  understand  the  requests.”  Similarly,  from  Participant
6354: “It can help understand specific materials that you don’t under-
stand,  gives  good  examples,”  but  also  “Not  always  accurate  when  it
comes to learning SQL.”  These tensions and contradictions reflect the
layered  nature  of  students’  experiences  with  digital  tools,  which  are
perceived simultaneously as robust supports for learning and as sources
of uncertainty. (We shall return to the question of reliability in the next
section.)

5.4.2. Authority and autonomy

The  second  tension  is  that  between  reliance  on  a  knowledgeable,
guiding authority and the desire for self-directed, autonomous learning.
The lecturer is perceived as a professional and reliable source of infor-
mation,  offering  clarity  and  confidence.  Participants  repeatedly
expressed the value of learning from a reliable human authority: e.g.,
“There’s a place to ask questions and get answers from someone with
experience in the field”  (Participant 0425); “The lecturer explains the
material with authority and credibility, and I feel confident the infor-
mation  is  reliable”  (Participant  2301);  “The  learned  information  is
reliable; there is a human response to questions” (Participant 8519). As
we saw above, by contrast, respondents expressed concerns about the
uncertain reliability of answers from GenAI, e.g., “The chatbot can be
misleading  and  doesn’t  always  understand  the  requests”  (Participant
7417); “Not always accurate when it comes to learning SQL”  (Partici-
pant 6354).

emphasizing the freedom from social pressure and the ability to learn in

such a thing as too much autonomy, and that GenAI-based learning may
present a risk of disengagement in the absence of external structure, a
point raised by participant 6119: “It’s easier for me to ignore and get
busy with other things.”

constraints and affordances of the environment lead to a paradox.

lecturer-based  learning should support  focused attention. However,  it
often imposes cognitive burdens that hinder sustained focus and deep
processing. One student explained: “Sometimes it’s hard to concentrate,

and  the  lecturer  moves  quickly  at  a  pace  that’s  not  comfortable  for
absorbing the content” (Participant 1620). Environmental factors com-

6. Discussion

This study explored students’ perspectives on traditional lecturer-led
instruction versus GenAI-supported learning in database management.
Using  qualitative  analysis  of  open-ended  questionnaire  responses,  the
study responds to a gap in the literature: while most prior research has
examined  students’  attitudes  toward  and  usage  of  GenAI  in  learning
environments  [4,5],  few  studies  have  directly  compared  traditional
lectures  with  GenAI-guided  instruction  from  the  learner’s  standpoint.
Our findings indicate that traditional instruction and GenAI-supported
learning  may  complement  one  another  for  some  students;  however,
this pattern is not uniform. Several participants expressed a clear pref-
erence for one mode over the other, suggesting variation among learners
rather than a consistent pattern.

The  literature  suggests  that  face-to-face  instruction  provides  a
structured  framework,  professional  authority,  and  real-time  feedback
[6,18,57].  Conversely, GenAI  environments  offer  flexibility,  personal-
ized  pacing,  and  an  infinite  supply  of  examples  that  promote  deeper
understanding  [3,13,48].  Our  findings  show  that  for  the  most  part,
students do not have a robust preference for one environment over the
other (though of course some do). Rather, they treat them as comple-
mentary. They value the stability and guidance of the lecturer alongside
the adaptability and depth afforded by GenAI, viewing the two as syn-
ergistic components of a unified, multi-dimensional learning strategy.

Our findings also raise questions about conventional assumptions in
the  literature  regarding  the  dynamics  of  learning in  the  two  environ-
ments.  Typically,  the  literature  presumes  a  clear  distinction  between
instructor-led and GenAI-facilitated learning, such that the former offers
centralized structure and authority, while the latter provides individu-
alized support and accessibility [6,18,48]. Our findings in some ways
subvert these assumptions. Students attributed qualities like immediacy
and precision, traditionally linked with GenAI, to the human instructor,
while  framing  GenAI  as  a  “24/7  private  tutor”  that  fosters  cognitive
development and engagement (through, e.g., tailored explanations and
repetitive  reinforcement).  This  challenges  the  traditional  human-
–technology dichotomy, as many students described a more fluid and
collaborative dynamic between the teacher and the tool.

At  first  glance,  students’  reflections  in  some  respects  may  appear
contradictory: they praised GenAI for fostering autonomy and emotional
comfort,  yet  simultaneously  questioned  its  reliability  and  risk  of

attention across the entire lecture, with little opportunity to adjust the
pace or revisit challenging content. Asking questions in front of peers
often caused discomfort due to the fear of social judgment or delaying
the class. In contrast, GenAI-supported learning allowed for self-paced
exploration,  pausing,  querying,  and  gradual  understanding  construc-
tion,  enhancing  emotional  comfort  and  fostering  autonomy  and
confidence.

Our analysis draws on three well-established educational paradoxes
or tensions discussed in the literature: structure vs. flexibility [15,16,31],
authority vs. autonomy [17,38], and attention vs. cognitive load [34,58].
However, rather than viewing these as binary tensions, students in our
study described navigating a dynamic continuum, relying on structured
lectures for clarity and grounding, and turning to GenAI for independent
exploration and personalized depth as needed. Both the instructor and
GenAI thus serve as mediators, helping learners select the appropriate
strategy for each phase of their learning journey.

This complexity is especially evident in the within-participant anal-
ysis of student reflections across our four core themes: Roles and Dy-
namics,  Cognitive  and  Emotional  Processes,  Learning  Strategies,  and
Contradictions and Complexities. Learners oscillate between modalities,
drawing on the structure of lectures for foundational understanding, and

leveraging GenAI tools for elaboration and self-regulation. Building on
these findings, we conceptualize a model that positions the learner as the
central agent in navigating hybrid learning environments.

The  Learner-Centered  Oscillation  Model  proposed  in  this  study  ad-
vances hybrid learning theory by conceptualizing the learner not as a
passive recipient choosing between instructional modalities, but as an
active  mediator  who  continuously  navigates  between  structured,
instructor-led environments and flexible, GenAI-supported exploration.
Our findings also suggest that the rise of GenAI requires a rethinking of
the  CoI  framework  in  contemporary  contexts.  In  the  original  model,
teaching  presence  was  assumed  to  be  human,  social  presence  was
grounded  in  peer  interaction,  and  cognitive  presence  emerged  from
structured discourse. In GenAI-supported environments, however, stu-
dents  reported  experiencing  elements  of  structure,  reassurance,  and
meaning-making  in  their  interactions  with  AI  tools.  The  Learner
Centered Oscillation Model expands the CoI framework by shifting the
focus from system level presences to the learner’s dynamic movement
between them. While the CoI framework describes cognitive presence,
social  presence,  and  teaching  presence  as  structural  components  that
together support meaningful learning, our findings show that learners
actively  move  between  these  presences  according  to  their  changing
cognitive and emotional needs. This movement involves seeking struc-

presence.  In  this  way,  the  model  offers  a  complementary  perspective
that  places  learner  movement  at  the  center  of  the  hybrid  learning
experience.

Unlike  prior  frameworks  that  treat  structure–flexibility  or  author-
ity–autonomy as external instructional tensions, our model frames these
dimensions as internal dialectics experienced within each learner. This
perspective  expands  existing  pedagogical  balance  theories  (e.g.,  [15,
38])  by  emphasizing  dynamic  adaptation  over  fixed  instructional
design,  and  positions  learner  agency  at  the  center  of  hybrid  learning
processes.

6.1. Theoretical contribution

This  study  enriches  hybrid  learning  theory  by  moving  beyond
cohort-level  comparisons  to  reveal  a  dynamic  process  in  which  the
paradoxes  of  structure–flexibility,  authority–autonomy,  and  atten-
tion–cognitive  load  coexist  within  each  learner’s  experience  simulta-
neously and interactively, rather than solely as tensions between distinct
instructional methods [15,16,30,31]. In doing so, we extend the existing
theoretical framework on pedagogical balancing and introduce a model
demonstrating  how  the  learner  is  the  mediator  between  these  poles
within a holistic learning process.

6.2. Practical contribution

Practically,  our  findings  offer  concrete  guidelines  for  designing
instructional environments that harmoniously integrate traditional lec-
tures and GenAI support. In hybrid learning environments, instructors
are  required  to  balance  structure  and  autonomy  consciously.  For
example, instructors can provide clear conceptual framing, illustrative
examples, and initial guidance at the beginning of the lesson to reduce
cognitive  load  and  establish  a  shared  starting  point  for  all  students.
Within this framework, instructors may incorporate brief tasks or pauses
that  prompt  students  to  consult  GenAI  for  elaboration,  review,  and
targeted questioning  [48].  Additionally, we recommend  training edu-
cators to actively manage the dialectic of authority and autonomy by
providing  clear  instructions  on  when  and  how  to  leverage  GenAI  to
sustain high engagement and cognitive focus. These recommendations
serve  as  a  blueprint  for  implementing  responsive,  learner-centered
teaching strategies in blended learning settings. Collectively, they pro-
vide  actionable  guidelines  for  designing  hybrid  courses  that  balance
lecturer-led instruction with GenAI-supported learning.

6.3. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is based on responses from
68 computer science students enrolled in a single database management
course at one institution. This relatively small and homogeneous sample
limits  the  generalizability  of  the  findings  to  broader  academic  pop-
ulations, content domains, or educational contexts. Therefore, caution is
warranted when interpreting and applying the results beyond this spe-
cific setting. Second, relatedly, participants’  demographic characteris-
tics and technological backgrounds were not sampled representatively,
underscoring the need for future research with larger and more diverse
learner samples. Third, group assignment was based on administrative
registration  procedures  rather  than  true  randomization.  However,  we
conducted  baseline  equivalence  testing  on  all  psychological  measures
collected in Week 1 (see the Participants and Procedure section for more
details). Those findings suggest that, despite the lack of true randomi-
zation, the groups were essentially equivalent at baseline, which sup-
ports the internal validity of our comparisons.

Fourth, some variability in student experiences may have resulted
from  differences  in  the  GenAI  tools  used.  Because  participants  were
allowed  to  freely  select  the  GenAI  platform  of  their  choice,  the  tools
likely  varied in interface, response  style, and underlying model capa-
bilities.  This  lack  of  standardization  may  have  introduced  subtle  dif-
ferences in students’ perceptions and interactions. However, since most
students  relied  on  widely  available,  free  GenAI  tools  with  similar
conversational features (e.g., ChatGPT, Google Bard), this variability is
expected to be limited.

Finally, focusing on learners’ subjective, immediate experiences, this
study does not report on quantitative performance metrics, such as ac-
ademic achievement. While relevant quantitative data was collected, its
analysis—addressing  students’  motivation,  learning  satisfaction,  and
academic performance—is presented and discussed in a separate pub-
lication  [59].  This  clarification  serves  to  manage  reader  expectations
and  delineate  the  specific  contribution  of  the  current  study.  As  the

findings are based solely on self-reported data, without behavioral or
performance-based  triangulation.  Accordingly,  the  results  reflect  stu-
dents’ subjective perspectives rather than objectively verified learning
outcomes.  Future  studies  incorporating  observational  or  performance
data could provide a more comprehensive validation of these insights.

6.4. Future research directions

To complete the picture outlined in this study, future research should
include  quantitative  studies  comparing  academic  achievement  in
lecturer-led versus GenAI-supported learning across an entire semester
or longer to assess these approaches’  long-term stability and efficacy.
Comparative studies across disciplines (e.g., humanities versus STEM)
that involve various generative GenAI tools could validate and extend
the  present  findings.  Another  valuable  line  of  research  might  involve
examining how instructor training and integrating high-quality prompt-
design  tools  shape  the  learning  experience  and  deepen  student
engagement.  Finally,  employing  mixed-methods  designs,  which
combine usage analytics from GenAI platforms with in-depth qualitative
data, would enrich our understanding of hybrid learning processes and
inform the development of learner-centered instructional strategies.

All told, while the full implications of GenAI are still emerging, it is
evident  that  higher  education  is  entering  a  new  and  transformative
phase.  Ensuring  the  effective  integration  of  this  technology  requires
thoughtful  design,  pedagogical  sensitivity,  and  ongoing  empirical
investigation [2,48].
